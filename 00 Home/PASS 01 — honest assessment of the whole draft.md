# PASS 01 — the whole thing, read as a story
### 10 Sep 2026. Santi asked: *is it a good book or not yet?*

**Short answer: there is a genuinely good novel in here, and this draft is not yet it.**

Not because of the prose — the prose is publishable. Because of three structural things that a reader will feel as *boredom* and *distance* without being able to name, and one mechanical thing that is embarrassing and takes an hour to fix.

I'd put it at a **strong 7**. Chapters 1–4 are an 8.5. The gap between those two numbers is the work.

---

## PART 1 — What is genuinely good, and I mean genuinely

**1. The method is the most original thing in the genre and it works.**
Nobody else does this. The control case in ch. 6 — where he tests his own ten-year-old technique on a book whose answers are printed on the copyright page, and **fails it, two out of four, plus one hallucination** — buys the reader's trust for the next 80,000 words. Then he keeps spending it correctly: entry 22 dies in Ghent and he *publishes the retraction*; the contractor lead in ch. 28 collapses; he's wrong about Noor. Four negative results, on the page, admitted. That is the anti-Da Vinci Code contract, kept.

**2. The thesis holds and it is not what it looked like at the start.**
*They don't delete catastrophes. They delete hands.* Every edit — *transmitted to* → *arisen among*, *a tradition of instruction* → *a tradition*, *what constitutes an observer remains open* → *now regarded as settled* — performs one operation. And it marries to the INNER track without a seam, which I did not expect to work and it does: ch. 25's re-read of entries 26 and 38 is the best structural moment in the book.

**3. Your mother's two sentences carry the ending, and they turned out to be the spine.**
*Too poor to be depressed. What you feel is fear of life.* → §7 of the runbook → *the revolution is the afternoon.* That chain is the reason ch. 32 works. Without it the ending is a man burying a dog; with it, it's the whole argument landing on a spade.

**4. The dog writing is the best writing in the book and it isn't close.**
Ch. 23's vigil — neither can help the other, neither leaves. Ch. 32 — *he stops meeting you at the door*, planted by a vet three years earlier. The burial with **no borrowed phrases**, which inverts ch. 1 exactly. If a reader cries once, it's there.

**5. The ending refuses every cheat available to it.** No exposure, no chase, no victory. A division sum handed to a conservator, and an extension, and a man who has to be silent for the rest of his life. And the manuscript is the payload, so you're reading it because he stopped.

## PART 2 — What's wrong. In priority order.

### ① Ana disappears exactly when the pressure rises. **This is the biggest problem.**

The Guardrails say: *"Ana and the dogs warm, specific, recurring. The machinery will try to shove her offstage every chapter — refuse."*

I didn't refuse. Count of her name per chapter:

| Ch | 6 | 7 | 8 | 11 | 15 | 16 | 17 | 29 | 30 | 33 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | 1 | 1 | 1 | **0** | 1 | **0** | **0** | **0** | **0** | **0** |

She is functionally absent for the whole Act 1 investigation (6–8), the whole Act 2A back half (15–17), and the entire climax (29–30). Ten chapters, ~24,000 words, in which the person the book is *for* does not appear.

That's not a warmth problem. It's a **stakes** problem: the reader stops being able to feel what any of it is costing, because the thing it's costing isn't on the page.

**Fix:** she needs a beat — one scene, sometimes four lines — in 7, 8, 11, 16, 17. And in 29 and 30 she should be present by *absence he registers*: he's two thousand kilometres away and thinking about the boiler.

### ② Buddy is missing from ten chapters.

Absent from 7, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 27, 29, 30, 33. He is the emotional spine and the clock, and he has holes in him. Every kitchen-table scene should have a dog under the table, because that's free and it's true and it's the difference between a man working and a man alone.

### ③ Chapters 15–16–17 are 5,600 words with twelve lines of dialogue between them.

The sag is real and it's worse than I flagged. Ch. 16 and 17 have **zero** dialogue, zero Ana, zero dogs. Two chapters of a man and paper.

They contain good material — the ink clusters, the unsent letter, *history is a forecast*, the spelling of his own name in a recruiter's email. The material isn't the problem. The problem is that nobody says anything for 3,700 words.

**Fix:** Noor should already exist by ch. 15. She currently arrives in 19, which is chapter nineteen of thirty-three — far too late for a Rocky. Move her first contact to the end of ch. 14 and give her two exchanges in 16, and the sag dissolves without adding a scene.

### ④ Ch. 29 is the climax of the whole LADDER and it is a document read aloud.

3,740 words, four lines of dialogue, and Aylin — who is the best minor character in the book — leaves halfway through so James can read a manual at a shelf. The single most important room in the story becomes an annotated table of contents.

**Fix:** Aylin stays. He reads §4 and §7 *while she is talking to a man about a chiller six metres away*, and the horror is that she's cheerful. And at least one section should be discovered by asking her a question rather than by reading a spine.

### ⑤ James is too self-aware to be in danger.

Gemini flagged this and was right, and I didn't fix it. He pre-empts every objection the reader could raise. He names his own bias, flags his own excitement, diagnoses his own deflections, and grades his own work — often in the same paragraph.

The effect is that he becomes unassailable, and an unassailable narrator cannot be *surprised*, and a character who cannot be surprised cannot be in a thriller.

**Fix:** he needs to be wrong about *himself*, at least twice, in a way he doesn't catch and Ana does. There's one (ch. 22: *right, now there's time*) and it's the best character beat in the book. There should be three more.

### ⑥ "Eleven." One hundred and twenty-four times.

Your chapters have one each. Mine average five and it **accelerates**: ch. 31 has ten in 1,700 words. It's my verbal fingerprint, not James's, and once a reader clocks it the book gets smaller.

Also over-used: *genuinely* (36), *I want to be [accurate/clear/honest]* (32), *for about* (33).

Mechanical, an hour's work, and it must be done before anybody else reads this.

### ⑦ The book stops being funny at ch. 20 and never recovers.

Some of that is designed — the Faucet budget goes to zero after the buy, and Ana is supposed to notice the silence. But I over-corrected: the *narration* went grey too, not just the character. Chapters 1, 2, 9 and 13 are genuinely funny. Chapters 24–31 have almost no wit in them at all.

Weir stays funny in the worst moments and that's why the worst moments land. The dry jokes should keep coming even when James has stopped performing them at tables — because the reader needs relief, and because a man who has lost his sense of humour is less interesting than a man using it in a cold room.

### ⑧ Register uniformity.

Almost every chapter I wrote has the same shape: observation → hedge → finding → flat closer. Over 29 chapters that becomes a metronome. Your four have more variation of *feeling* — warmth, comedy, grief, irritation — than my twenty-nine do.

## PART 3 — The corrected order for the interludes and cutaways

You were right to ask: as placed, **three of them clashed** (two non-James pieces back to back in three separate places). Fixed order, spaced evenly, each one earning its position:

```
Ch 1 – 4
   ▸ INTERLUDE I — Down House, Kent. January 1860
Ch 5 – 8
   ▸ CUTAWAY — A hotel bar in Bratislava. Some years earlier
Ch 9 – 11
   ▸ INTERLUDE II — Gondar, Ethiopia. 1770
Ch 12 – 16
   ▸ INTERLUDE III — The Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem. 1978 and 1991
Ch 17 – 20
   ▸ CUTAWAY — A room with a long table. March
Ch 21 – 22
   ▸ CUTAWAY — Kastanjelaan. A Thursday in August
Ch 23 – 25
   ▸ CUTAWAY — An office above a courtyard. Monday
Ch 26 – 28
   ▸ INTERLUDE IV — Nuremberg. Dawn, 14 April 1561
Ch 29 – 30
   ▸ CUTAWAY — A long-stay car park. November
Ch 31 – 33
   ▸ CODA — The crate
```

**Why each sits where it sits:**

- **Darwin after ch. 4** — ch. 4 ends *"if it was worth doing here, where else?"* and Darwin answers it in the largest possible way: everywhere, by the authors themselves, and one of them regretted it in a letter you can read.
- **Bratislava after ch. 8** — ch. 8 is where he clears the shelf and commits. Immediately: this is what happens to people who work lists. Dread at the exact moment of commitment. (Moved from after ch. 11, where it clashed with Gondar.)
- **Gondar after ch. 11** — ch. 11 is the Bringers punished in every pantheon. Gondar is the book *about* the ones who taught, surviving in the one church that got cut off — and a man laughed at for twenty years for carrying it out.
- **Jerusalem after ch. 16** — **moved here deliberately to break the 15–17 sag.** And it's the right marriage: ch. 12's book was *withdrawn for conservation*, and Jerusalem is forty years of conservation-as-lock, broken by a backup filed a long way off in case of fire — which is James's whole endgame in advance.
- **The long table after ch. 20** — the reader has just watched a man in a station café decline to confirm anything. Now they watch nine people decide something, and the review point comes forward eleven months.
- **Kastanjelaan before ch. 23** — fixed position. It only works immediately before Buddy's collapse.
- **The courtyard after ch. 25** — **moved from after ch. 22.** Now it lands *after* Noor has gone dark, so it retroactively explains the visit, and after the crossing, so it answers "what were they doing while I was on the kitchen floor?" with: *leave him alone, he's worth more quiet.* Colder this way.
- **Nuremberg after ch. 28** — **moved from after ch. 25.** A quiet breath before the depot, and thematically exact: people with no vocabulary reaching for the nearest object on the bench, immediately before a man walks into a room and has to describe it.
- **Car park after ch. 30** — the name is retired the moment the arithmetic is out.
- **The crate** — last page, always.

## PART 4 — What I'd do next, in order

1. **The eleven pass.** One hour. Mechanical. Do it first because it's cheap.
2. **The Ana pass.** Five short beats in ch. 7, 8, 11, 16, 17; her absence *registered* in 29 and 30. Maybe 1,200 new words total and it changes the whole feeling of the book.
3. **Move Noor to ch. 14** and give her two exchanges in 16. Kills the sag.
4. **Rewrite ch. 29 with Aylin in the room.** The biggest single upgrade available.
5. **Buddy under the table** in 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, 27, 30.
6. **A humour pass on 24–31.** Not jokes — dryness. He can be funny and wrecked at once; that's the register of your chapter 1.
7. **Three more places where he's wrong about himself and doesn't notice.**

That's maybe 6,000 new words and two days of surgical editing, and at the end of it I think it's genuinely good rather than promising.

---

## The honest bottom line

The bones are a real literary thriller with a method nobody else in the genre has bothered to build, an argument that holds, and an ending that refuses to cheat. The dog will make people cry. The last exchange of ch. 33 — *"Well spotted. Go on, then." / "Go on what?" / "Check."* — earns the whole book.

But right now it reads as **one voice doing one thing at one temperature for 87,000 words**, and the two characters who make it a novel rather than a case file go missing precisely when the pressure rises, which is exactly backwards.

Fix that and it's a book. It is not that yet.
