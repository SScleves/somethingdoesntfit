# Chapter Nine

I'm going to spend a chapter on a Saturday, and I'd like you to bear with me, because it's the last one of these I get.

Late September. The kind of morning that makes people move to this country and the kind of afternoon that makes them leave. We got to the course at nine, all four of us, in the car that smells permanently of wet dog and the inside of a golf bag.

Here is the standard configuration.

Mora goes out of the car like she's been fired from a cannon and immediately makes for the treeline, because along the treeline there is a ditch, and in the ditch there are ducks, and where there are ducks there is duck poop, which to Mora is not a waste product but a delicacy, a rare and seasonal one, the truffle of the northern lowlands. She is a six-year-old Doberman with the bearing of a Prussian officer and she eats poo. I've stopped pretending this isn't the case at dinner parties. It cost me nothing and gained me the reputation of an honest man.

Buddy walks the first three holes and then gets in the cart. He used to do the front nine. Before that he did all eighteen and then tried to do a bit more in the car park. Now he does three and then stands next to the cart looking at the middle distance with great dignity until I lift him in, which we both pretend is not happening.

Ana comes because she likes the walk and the coffee at the turn, and because — this is a thing about my wife I don't think I've properly said — she is capable of being genuinely, uncomplicatedly happy in a place she has no interest in whatsoever, purely because the people she likes are there. I have never once managed that. I've watched her do it for fifteen years the way you'd watch a man juggle.

And I ruin a beautiful walk one swing at a time.

---

She lasts, on average, until the sixth. Sometimes the seventh, if there's something to look at.

That day she made it to the sixth and then sat down on the bench by the tee and got her phone out, and I said what I always say, which is "the standard is dropping," and she said what she always says, which is "you're the one with the standard," and then Mora came out of the ditch with something in her mouth and Ana screamed and I hit the best drive of my life.

Two hundred and forty metres. Straight. Right down the middle. The kind of shot that if you hit it in front of strangers you get to keep for a week.

Ana didn't see it. She was busy with the dog and the thing in the dog.

I stood there with the club still up and no witnesses, and it was, in a very small and very real way, the happiest I had been in months.

I told Buddy about it. He'd seen it. He was the only one who'd seen it.

"Straight," I said. "You saw."

Two thumps.

"Nobody's going to believe us."

---

On the ninth she laughed at something. I don't remember what. Something about the man in front of us who had been addressing the ball for an unreasonable length of time and had developed a whole ceremony about it — a genuinely funny thing, and she did the laugh, the real one, the one with a sort of collapse in the middle of it that I have been trying to cause on purpose since I was twenty-three.

And I timed it.

Not deliberately. Some part of me that has been running in the background since the funeral logged it and said: *four seconds. Longest since June.*

I stood on the ninth fairway with my seven iron and a sick, quiet feeling, because there is no version of a man who is measuring his wife's laughing that is a good version, and I knew it, and there was nothing whatsoever I could do about it. That part doesn't take instructions. It never has. It's the same part that counted the fourteen tabs.

I want it on the record because it's the first symptom and I missed it. I thought the disease would be about books.

---

We had the coffee at the turn. Buddy got a sausage from the woman at the counter because Buddy has been getting a sausage from the woman at the counter for nine years, and there was a period around the fourth year when I tried to stop it, and the woman and the dog conducted a quiet campaign of resistance which I lost.

Ana said, "Your hands smell of old paper."

"They do not."

"They do. All the time now." She wasn't accusing me of anything. She was tearing bits off a croissant. "It's not bad. It's like living with a small library that's slightly damp."

"That's a lovely thing to say to a man."

"Take it," she said. "There's not much coming."

That's the marriage. I'd have signed for another forty years of it right there at that plastic table, and I want to be clear that I *knew* that at the time. It wasn't hindsight. I sat there in the smell of coffee and wet dog and thought, in these words: *this is enough. This is the whole thing. Don't go looking.*

Same thought I'd had on the seventh in the wet grass, months earlier, and had put on the record.

Here's what's different, and it's the reason I'm writing this chapter at all: the first time, it arrived as weather. It just passed through.

This time it arrived as an offer. There was a version of that afternoon where I put the shelf back, gave the list to a university that would ignore it, and went to the driving range twice a week for the rest of my life.

I looked at it. I genuinely looked at it. Then I said no, silently, to nobody, over a croissant.

So when it all goes wrong later, I'd like it understood that nobody chose it for me and nothing dragged me in. I was asked, clearly, at a plastic table, by my own head, with my wife three feet away and my hand on an old dog. I said no thank you.

---

Two things happened that week that I only understood much later, and I'll put them here because this is where they happened.

The first took eleven seconds. I was setting up the file where I keep the entries — the shelf list, the photographs, the procedure notes — and when it asked me where to put it, I put it inside the Record, behind the password.

Nothing dramatic. It's the same password I've used for a decade, which tells you everything about my professional standards.

But I noticed myself doing it, and I noticed the small, specific reason: not *this is valuable*. Not *this could be lost*. The reason was **if somebody opens this laptop, I don't want that folder to be the one they open.**

I sat back and had a look at that. Ana has never opened my laptop in fifteen years. Nobody comes to the house. There is not a single human being on the planet who has any interest in my folders.

And that's twice now. The folder called Reading, which built the hiding place before there was anything to hide. And this.

The quiet part of me keeps getting there first and it never tells me why until afterwards.

The second thing was Frank.

---

Thursday, kitchen at work, terrible coffee. Frank had the new monitoring tools open on his laptop because Frank has everything open before anyone else, it's his whole personality and I like him for it.

"They're doing the pilot in Q1," he said. "Look at this. It just — it just *finds* it. You don't tell it anything."

"Mm."

"You should be more worried than you are. Enjoy it while it lasts." Skull emoji, verbally, somehow.

And then he asked the question, and he asked it the way you ask a thing you assume is rhetorical.

"Actually — genuine question. How does it know what to ignore?"

And I answered.

I should have said *no idea, mate, before my time* and gone back to my desk. Instead I stood in that kitchen holding a paper cup and told him that the hard part was never detection, that detection is trivial and always has been, that anything can find ten thousand differences a night; that the entire value of a monitoring system lives in what it has been told is normal; that every one of these things ships with a set of defaults that decide, in advance, which classes of event are noise; that those defaults are written by human beings under time pressure and are then copied by everyone forever because nobody reads them; and that if you wanted to make an organisation blind to a specific kind of problem you would not need to break anything, you would only need to be in the room on the day the defaults were agreed, and you would need to be no cleverer than anybody else in it.

Then I stopped, because I'd been talking for a while.

Frank was looking at me with his eyebrows up and a small delighted expression, and he said, "Okay, that's the most words you've ever said."

Then he laughed, and I laughed, and I put a joke on it — something about my swing — and we went back to our desks.

But he'd looked at me for about a second and a half before the laugh. I saw it. It was the same look Ana does over a ruler at midnight.

*You've got the face.*

---

That night I lay in the dark with Ana's breathing beside me and the house doing its noises, and I ran the arithmetic of my own life the way I'd run it on the drive back from the cemetery, and it came out like this:

I had a wife I liked more than anyone I had ever met. Two dogs, one of them running out of time in a way I could measure in stairs. A job that bought all of it and asked almost nothing of me. Saturdays. A good shelf by the window with two hundred books on the floor beside it.

And forty-one lines in another man's handwriting, of which I had verified two.

Everything I had, on one side. On the other, a list I could not put down, written by a man who died before he could tell anybody what it was for, with one word at the bottom in shaking ink.

I remember thinking, quite calmly, before I went to sleep: *there is no version of this where I get to keep both.*

I was right about that. It's the only thing in this whole business I got right the first time.
