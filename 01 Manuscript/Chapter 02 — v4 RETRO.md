# Chapter Two

Somebody in our house had an answer to grief, and it was the dog.

We got home from the funeral a little after six. I opened the front door and Mora came around the corner like a horse that had heard a rumor about food — all ears and momentum, nails skating on the floor — and threw herself at Ana as if Ana had been gone eleven years instead of eleven hours. No concept of graves. No concept of *gone*. In Mora's world there are exactly two states, here and about-to-be-here, and both of them are the best thing that has ever happened.

Ana went down on her knees right there in the hallway, in her funeral clothes, and put her face into that black neck and cried in a way she hadn't cried all day. Not the cemetery crying. The real thing. The kind with no shape to it. And Mora just stood there and took it, tail going slowly, absorbing something that an entire receiving line of human beings, armed with two thousand years of theology, had not known what to do with.

This time Buddy did get up. Lately he greets from the blanket — two thumps of the tail, the short version of a dance he used to do — and waits for us to come to him. We do. You do. But this time he crossed the room to me, slow, deliberate, paying for every step, as if he'd decided that today, of all days, I needed him to be okay.

I remember thinking: the dogs are doing more for us than I did all day. And then: they aren't trying to say anything. Maybe that's the entire trick.

---

Grief, it turns out, doesn't move in. It visits.

For the next few weeks Ana was fine at dinner and destroyed by a drawer. She'd laugh at something on TV — actually laugh — and twenty minutes later I'd find her in the bedroom holding a knitting needle she'd found in a bag. Not crying. Just standing, somewhere I couldn't follow. Her grandmother had left a project unfinished: half a sleeve of something, pale green, needles still in it, paused mid-row. Ana couldn't decide what to do with it. Finishing it felt wrong. Unraveling it felt worse. So it sat on the shelf in the living room, and nobody touched it, and we both looked at it every day and said nothing. Our first haunted object. Very low-budget haunting. Extremely effective.

I did what I could, which was logistics. Coffee appeared. Paperwork got handled. The car got gas without anyone asking. And the occasional did-you-know, the safe kind, the ones that let her laugh without having to talk. Except sometimes I get them wrong.

"Did you know dogs only have two states at home? With you. Or waiting for you."

She looked at me for a second.

"Now I feel sad for them, you’re such a dick, read the room"

"No — that doesn't mean they're *sad.* It just —" I looked at Buddy on his blanket, waiting for nobody in particular. "Never mind."

She was right. It wasn’t a good did-you-know. I’d been doing it long enough to know the difference.

If love were measured in solved errands, I'd be up for husband of the month. Some nights she fell asleep on my chest at nine and I'd lie there under her, one arm dead, watching the ceiling, thinking about the flash in the cemetery for exactly four seconds before filing it — again — under lack of sleep. I was sleeping fine by then. I filed it anyway. The filing system doesn't ask questions. That's what it's for.

At some point I gently floated the idea of her talking to someone. I know that market better than I let on — a story for later. Ana said she'd think about it. She didn't need it anyway. She had the dog.

---

Work, meanwhile, went on being work.

That month it was a new payment system I'd never heard of, owned by a team I'd never met before, in one of our other offices, in a country I've never been to. Something in it was slowing down — not failing, slowing, which is worse, because a dead thing gets attention and a dying thing gets a meeting. The owner walked me through their setup with the confidence of a man pointing out every place the roof has already leaked, and everything he showed me was fine. Everything anyone shows you is always fine. That's not lying, exactly. It's that people show you what they check, and what they check is what has burned them before, and the thing that's killing them is always somewhere else — in the boring corner nobody's been burned by yet.

So I did what I do. I asked the uninteresting questions. Not *what changed* — everyone asks what changed, and the answer is always *nothing*, and it's always wrong. I ask smaller than that. What's this value here? What counts as acceptable? How long has it been flat, and why was flat decided to be good? Three days of that — three days of being the slow annoying guy in the meeting asking the dumbest questions — and there it was: one little value, deep in a queue nobody looked at, that had been quietly climbing for six weeks while every dashboard above it glowed green.

Nobody had lied. Everybody had trusted the margins. Every system ships with an accepted level of error — known glitches that never grew into real problems, noise you learn to sleep through. Which is a strange arrangement, if you stop on it, for an exact science: we build these things out of pure logic, and then we spend years teaching ourselves which of their complaints to ignore.

People think the job is finding things. It isn't. Finding things is easy; the machines find ten thousand things a night and most of them are weather. The job is knowing what matters and what can be ignored, and nobody teaches it, and nobody can tell you how they do it. And the part I'd never get through at a barbecue is that half the time I don't read the thing at all. I read how it's kept. Where it lives, how many copies there are, how deep it's buried, whether somebody made a backup of the backup. A man's filing tells you what he was afraid of losing. You don't have to open anything.

I fixed nothing, by the way. I never fix anything. I just point, and then the people who actually understand the thing go and fix it, and in the retro someone, maybe, says *good catch* in the tone you'd use for a dog that found the keys. Fine. I've made peace with it. Mostly.

There was one moment from that month I'd have filed under nothing at the time, and I'm only mentioning it because of everything that came after. Frank — a colleague, young guy, sharp, permanently vibrating about the future — sent the team a link about the new wave of monitoring tools. AI-driven. Finds anomalies automatically, no humans needed. "Enjoy it while it lasts, boys," he wrote, with the little skull emoji, and everyone laughed.

And then somebody made the industrial-revolution argument — *we always find a way, new jobs always come* — and everybody nodded, because that's what you say when someone leans on the wall of comfort. And it slipped out of me. Quietly. But out.

"Based on what?"

Frank turned. "Based on what, what?"

Work persona back on, half a second, seamless: "Based on what I've seen of my swing, I'm going to need that free time." Laughs all around. Moving on.

The real thought stayed where it lives. It usually does. Thoughts like that are too heavy for a Tuesday.

---

Everyone has a thing they do when nobody's watching. Some men have fantasy football. Some have a garage. I hunt.

Not animals — I don't even like to eat them. And I don't know if I should call it a hobby or a hunt; both make it sound organized, structured, and it isn't. I hunt for things that don't fit. Anything old and slightly wrong. A map that shouldn't know what it knows. A door in a photograph of a monument, ten meters up a wall, opening onto nothing. I'll take it in any form — a book, a website that looks like it was built in 2003 and abandoned mid-sentence, a forum thread where one person is being very carefully ignored by everyone else because their question is too good.

And half the time, honestly, the prize isn't the strange thing at all. It's the explanation. There's a particular flavor of official answer — you learn to taste it — where you can feel the explanation working harder than the mystery ever did. Stretching. Covering. An answer wearing a lab coat two sizes too big. A strange thing is just strange. But a strange thing plus a nervous explanation? Now *that's* interesting. That's not a gap in knowledge. That's a gap somebody filled in a hurry.

And people. People are the best part, and the part I'd least like to explain to my colleagues. There's a man at the second-hand market — I call him Beard Man; we've never exchanged names, it's not that kind of friendship, and at this point asking would ruin something; for all I know he's a Kevin — who deals in old books and talks the way a leaking pipe drips: slowly, steadily, forever, and once in a while something comes out that keeps me up for a week. Most of what he says is nonsense. I want to be clear about that. Ninety percent, nonsense. But I stopped filtering people by their worst sentence a long time ago. Boring people are wrong in boring ways. Beard Man is wrong in *interesting* ways, and interesting-wrong is worth twenty minutes of my Sunday, every Sunday, because interesting-wrong means he's at least looking at something.

The books, though. The books are the branch of it I'd defend in court. I like old books for one specific reason, and it isn't the smell, whatever people say. It's that a printed book can't be edited after the fact. It's a snapshot. Whatever it says, it said it, in that year, in that ink, and no one can reach into it afterward and quietly fix it. The internet is wet cement — anyone can go back and smooth over anything, and the internet's version of history has exactly the credibility of a witness who's allowed to change his statement every night. Paper testifies once. That's why I own a wall of books I'll mostly never read. They're not a library. They're depositions.

First editions are the good stuff — not to own; I'm not rich enough for the ones that matter. To *check.* Because the same book doesn't always say the same thing twice. A claim gets softened between printings, a conclusion quietly retreats, a line that was confident in 1951 goes careful in 1963 — and nobody announces it. You only catch it if you already knew what it used to say. That's the hunt. Not typos. Retractions nobody admitted to. I note them, photograph both pages, and file it. I call the whole thing the Record, which I'd defend as a perfectly reasonable name for a perfectly reasonable habit.

Ana finds the whole thing funny, in the way you find your husband's harmless weirdness funny after enough years. When we met I told her I liked "history," which was true the way telling someone you like "food" is true. By the third year she'd walked past enough open tabs that every December she asks what size I want my tinfoil hat this Christmas. And she calls the Record my *strange sci-fi poetry*, which pisses me off on two technicalities: it isn't sci-fi, and I hate poetry. She knows both things. That's the whole reason she says it. There was one evening — much later, and I'll get to it — when the jokes stopped. But back then it was still funny, and I was still the guy with a weird hobby and a good job and a wife asleep on his chest, and that was the whole story of me. I'd have signed for another forty years of it.

---

Saturdays I play golf. Ana says golf is my actual job and the IT thing is just how I pay for the equipment. She thinks she's joking. She's closer than she knows — I've never seen the job as much more than an enabler. It buys the house, the dogs, the Saturdays. The things that matter, I do for free.

I've tried every sport that would have me, and they all work the same way: something in them quiets the mind, for a while, until the sport itself starts making noise — football's circus, the sweat-and-ego economy of martial arts, the design flaw in everything with a net: you can't play it alone. Golf is the last one standing, and not because of golf. It's the only activity on earth where a married man can vanish for four hours, alone, on purpose, and come home to *good, you needed that* instead of a conversation. Guilt-free solitude, socially certified.

Also — the real reason, the one that would get me blackballed from any serious club — ours is the kind of course that allows dogs, which tells you something about the greens and everything about why I chose it. Saturdays it's the four of us: Ana around the sixth, losing interest with tremendous grace; Mora patrolling the treeline for duck poop, which to her is caviar — she's gross; Buddy walking the front nine and supervising the back nine from the cart; and me, ruining a beautiful walk one swing at a time.

The rest of the week it's just Buddy and me — seven in the morning, dew on everything, and up ahead the same four retirees who have been on the third hole since 1998.

People ask if I'm any good. I tell them I have a lot of fun out there.

Honestly, I haven't personally experienced any of the fun yet — I'm bad at it in ways that make strangers offer advice.

It's not about the game anyway. Golf is the one place where cause and effect are honest — bad swing, bad result, immediately, in public, nobody spinning it. The ball has no version of events. No committee, no teammates, no rival. For four hours the world is exactly what it looks like, and a mind like mine finally sits down and shuts up.

That first Saturday after the funeral I put my drive into the trees on the seventh, same as always, and stood in the wet grass feeling something disgracefully close to peace, and a thought passed through, light as weather: *this is enough. This life is enough. Don't go looking.*

I want it on the record that I had that thought. It matters for later.

---

Which brings me to the tabs.

It was maybe a month after the funeral, two in the morning, house dark, both dogs asleep — Mora upside down like a crashed helicopter, Buddy curled tight the way old dogs sleep, conserving something. I was at the kitchen table with the laptop, doing my usual rounds, and I reached to close the browser and stopped, because I actually looked at what was open.

Fourteen tabs. I'd opened every one of them that night, each leading to the next the way tabs do, no plan, no decision that I could remember making. But there they were. Near-death experiences. A study on deathbed visions. Two forums. A doctor talking about patients who woke from transplants with someone else's cravings. An essay on reincarnation and why some kids remember past lives, with the word *evidence* in the title doing a lot of heavy lifting. Some of it careful. Some of it insane. All of it circling one drain.

No tabs on maps that knew too much. Not one door ten meters up a wall. Not one paragraph that moved between printings. Fourteen tabs, and not a single thing for the Record. The hunter had gone out, all night, and come back with nothing — because he'd been hunting somewhere else.

I sat there and looked at them the way I look at anything that changes when nobody touched it. Because here's the thing about me, the thing all those years of watching have burned in: nothing changes for no reason. There's always a cause. A moment when the change got in. And I knew the moment. I knew exactly when it went in — standing in gold light, holding a hand that had forgotten mine, saying yes at eighty-five percent.

The search hadn't asked my permission. It had just started, quietly, in the background, the way real problems always start — not with an alarm, but with something small, drifting, where nobody's looking.

I looked at the tabs a while longer.

Then I did the most honest thing I'd done since the funeral.

I bookmarked them.
