# A market, Sunday

The market was in a town near the Danish border, in a square with a church on one side and a bank on the other, and he had been coming to it for three Sundays because it was the kind of thing a man does who lives here.

He lived here now. A rented flat over a bicycle shop. A name on the bell that was not his and not the last one either. He'd cut his hair. He'd let his beard come in, grey at the chin, which he hadn't expected and didn't mind. He paid cash. He ran in the mornings, along the water, not too far, not too fast. He had been careful in all the ways he had been trained to be careful, and in two or three he had invented, and he was good at it. He'd always been good at things.

He bought bread. He bought a coffee from a van and stood with it by the cheese stall, because the cheese man talked about weather in a dialect he enjoyed, and because from there he could see both ends of the square.

He saw the first one at ten past eleven.

A man in a good coat at the flower stall, choosing tulips. Mid-fifties, a scarf, a face Kalder knew from a room in Leiden with a long table, nine years ago — a man who'd once told him, kindly, that the hardest part of the work was the paperwork. The man did not look at him. The man was very interested in tulips.

Kalder turned, unhurried, the way you'd turn to check the time on the church, and saw the second.

A woman by the bakery van. Red hat. He'd sat next to her at a dinner in Antwerp. She had a paper bag in one hand and nothing in the other, and she was not looking at him either.

He finished his coffee.

There was a third by the church steps, and probably a fourth behind him where he couldn't see without making it obvious that he was looking, and he didn't look. He stood by the cheese and did the arithmetic, the way he'd been taught, and the arithmetic came out the way it does when people who know the method use the method on you. No hand. A Sunday. A plausible ending somewhere quiet. Weeks from now, maybe. Or this afternoon.

The cheese man asked if he wanted the usual. He said yes.

His phone rang while the cheese man was wrapping it.

It was the old phone. The one he'd kept, switched off, in the lining of the jacket, for no reason he had ever said out loud. He'd switched it on that morning for the first time in five weeks, to look at something. He hadn't told himself what.

He looked at the screen and almost laughed.

"James."

"Julian." A kitchen behind the voice. A dog's nails on tiles. "You can see them?"

"I can see three."

"I don't know how many there are. I didn't find you." A pause. "I want you to know that. I'm not that guy. I asked, and they found you. They're much better at it than me."

"They are." The tulip man had bought his tulips. He was standing with them by the fountain. "Why ring?"

A long breath down the line. A dog, close to the phone, sighing in that long theatrical way old dogs do.

"Part of the deal was you gone," James said. "I got the rest. I wanted to say one thing, and I wanted it to be me."

"Go on."

"You shouldn't have touched my dog."

The cheese man held out the parcel. Kalder took it and gave him a note and waved away the change.

"No," he said. "I shouldn't."

He meant it. Not as an apology — he had never in his life done anything as an apology. As an error. The one entry in nine years he would go back and change. Everything else had been necessary, or clever, or both. The dog had been quick. Quick was the mistake. A man doesn't forgive you a thing like that, and a man who doesn't forgive you makes phone calls, and a man who makes phone calls to the right people on the right day costs you a Sunday.

"Is he all right?"

"He lay down on the sixth green on Monday. Right next to the hole."

Kalder smiled at the fountain. He couldn't help it. "They'll have hated that."

"The greenkeeper pretended not to see."

"Good man."

The red hat had moved. Not closer. Just to a place that was closer to where he would have to walk.

"I liked you," Kalder said. "I'd like that to be on whatever you're keeping. All of it was work. The golf, the barbecue, Ana. And I liked you anyway. That wasn't the plan."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yeah," James said. "That's why it was so fucking hard."

Kalder laughed then, properly — the one from the back nine, the one people turned round for. The cheese man glanced up. The tulip man did not.

"Tell Ana it was very good. The pills. I worked it out in the car. I'd have bet on you."

"I'll tell her."

"And James —"

But the line had gone. He looked at the phone. James had hung up on him. He found, standing in a square in the north with a parcel of cheese and three people he'd once had dinner with walking very slowly toward him from three directions, that this pleased him more than anything had in five weeks.

He put the phone back in his jacket. He tucked the cheese under his arm. He turned toward the church, where the third one was, because it was the shortest walk and he'd always been a man who liked to get on with things.

The woman in the red hat reached him first. She said his name — the real one — quite warmly, as if they'd run into each other by chance.

"Hello," Kalder said. "Isn't it a beautiful day."
