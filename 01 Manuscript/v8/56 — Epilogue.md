# Epilogue

Buddy died four years later, on a Tuesday in April, at home, on his blankets by the radiator, at a quarter past two in the morning.

We were both on the floor. That's where he was, so that's where we were. He'd been slowing down for a long time — the back legs first, then the appetite, then the thing in his eyes that used to follow me round a room — and the vet had said, a week before, kindly, that it would be soon and that he didn't think it would be hard. He was right, for once. I'd have preferred him wrong.

Buddy's head was on my knee. Ana was on the other side of him with her hand flat on his chest, the way she does, counting without counting. Mora was in the doorway, grey in the muzzle herself now, not letting anyone in or out, which at a quarter past two meant nobody. At some point his breathing changed. Then it got slower. Then there was a breath, and we waited for the next one, the way you wait for a ball to drop on the far side of a hill, and it didn't come.

Neither of us said anything for a long time. The fridge hummed. Mora lay down in the doorway with a groan.

Then Ana said, "Well. That's that, old man," in a perfectly steady voice, and started crying the real way, the way with no shape, and I held her, and the dog was between us and still warm, and I kept my hand on his ear because it was soft and I didn't want to stop.

---

Frank came in the morning with beer, at nine, which is a crime, and sat on the kitchen floor where the blankets had been. He's thirty-three now and has a girlfriend who does yoga and he's started saying *we* about weekends. He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "Fuck," and then, "He was a good foreman," and then nothing, which was exactly right.

We buried him in the garden that afternoon, by the gate, where his water bowl used to be — the gate a man once walked up to at night with a bowl of his own. I'd checked the rules years ago. You can, here, if the garden's yours and the hole's deep enough. I made it deep enough. It took me most of the afternoon because there's a root, and I swore at the root the whole time, and Ana brought me tea and stood at the edge and didn't help, and Ida — I'll get to Ida — kept trying to get in the hole with me.

I put him in on his blanket. The grey one.

Then I stood there with the spade and the sky doing nothing in particular, and I said it.

"He's in a better place. I'll see him soon."

Ana looked at me. She'd heard me say a version of those words once before, at a grave, in a voice that was about eighty-five percent yes and fifteen percent question. She knew the difference. She's always known the difference.

"You said it," she said.

"I know."

"You meant it."

"I know what it means now."

I can't explain that better than that, and I've tried, in a lot of drafts. It's not that I'm sure. I'm a man who checks things for a living and I can't check this one. It's that I know what I'm going to do with it. When it's my turn — and it's coming, it's coming for everybody, that's the one thing in this whole story nobody disputes — I'm going to do what I did on the kitchen floor four nights in November. I'm not going to be afraid. I'm going to remember on purpose, the way they do, the same method, only for leaving. And there's going to be an old grey dog at the bottom of whatever it is, waiting, with that patience that used to break me, and that's how I'll know I got it right.

Ana put her hand on my back.

"Okay," she said. "Come inside. You're filthy."

Ida came that summer, from a woman near Deventer with a litter and a card in a bakery window. Brown, all feet, ears that hadn't decided yet. Not a replacement; Ana was very clear, and Mora was clearer — she took one look at the puppy and went to lie in the bathroom for three days. We called her Ida. There was no argument about the name, which in our house was a first.

One night in October I went up to bed and got to the top of the stairs and turned round, the way you do.

Ida was sitting at the bottom step.

Not following. Sitting. Looking up at me. She was six months old, made of springs, she could have been up those stairs in two seconds, and she'd never seen Buddy do anything in her life, and nobody had taught her. She just sat there at the bottom with her head a little on one side and watched me go, with that patience.

I stood at the top for a long time.

Then I went back down, and sat on the bottom step next to her, and she climbed into my lap, all elbows, and licked my ear and then sneezed in it.

"Okay," I said. "Okay. I see you."

Ana called from upstairs to ask what the fuck I was doing on the stairs at midnight.

"Nothing," I said. "Checking something."
