# Chapter Three

> Status: ✅ LOCKED · RETRO 8 Sep: the 'did-you-knows I couldn't say' paragraph inserted after 'So I read' (revert if unwanted)

The bookmarks needed a folder. The folder needed a name.

I named it "Reading." Two seconds, no thought — and, I realized much later, a perfect disguise. A folder called Reading is invisible. Not even Ana would click on it. And here's what stopped me when I finally audited that little moment: Ana wasn't looking. Nobody was. There was nothing to hide and no one to hide it from — and some quiet part of me had already built the hiding place anyway. The same part, I assume, that had opened fourteen tabs without asking. It was ahead of the rest of me the whole way. It usually is.

Then I started reading. Properly. Not tabs at two in the morning — reading with a pen.

It turns out humanity has been filing incident reports on death for five thousand years. Nobody calls them that. They're called testimonies, visions, scriptures, case studies, forum posts at three in the morning. But strip the vocabulary and that's what they are: eyewitness accounts of the one outage everybody experiences and nobody can reproduce on demand. And I'd never read them. That's the part that got me, those first weeks — not what was in the reports, though some of it was the strangest material I've ever put in front of my eyes. What got me was the fact that I'd been thinking about this my whole life — secretly, subconsciously, in the waiting rooms of my own head — and I'd never once dared to actually look. Society works in a funny way about exactly one topic: death is coming for every single one of us, and it's still not polite to ask about it. You need an excuse. A diagnosis. A funeral. A philosophy degree. You're allowed to *cope* with death all you want. You're just not supposed to *investigate* it.

So I read. Lunch breaks. Late nights. The twenty minutes in the car outside the supermarket that Ana thinks I spend on a podcast.

And for the first time in my life I was collecting did-you-knows I couldn't say out loud. Not at a table. Not to Ana. They went in the folder with the rest, and the folder got heavier.

And I'll tell you what I found, because it surprised me, and I don't surprise easy.

I expected garbage. I found garbage — oceans of it, glowing, self-published, aggressively capitalized. But you learn to sort. You always learn to sort; it's the whole job of being me. And under the garbage there's a layer that doesn't behave like garbage. Doctors — real ones, with careers to lose — quietly publishing accounts their training has no shelf for. Patients flat on a table, monitors reporting nobody home, who wake up and describe the conversation in the hallway. The scuff on top of the cabinet. The thing the surgeon said when it went wrong. Details that were checked, by annoyed professionals, hoping to be able to file the whole thing under imagination — and that held.

Individually? Explainable. Every single one. Anesthesia is weird, brains are weird, memory is a liar with a good face. I know all the outs; I collected them like everyone else who wants to sleep well.

But two things snagged me.

The first: the reports rhyme. Across countries. Across centuries. Across people who'd never read each other — farmers and cardiologists, kids and monks. Not identical, nothing's identical, but *structured*, the way independent witnesses of the same accident disagree on the color of the car and agree on the direction it was going. Coincidence produces noise. This wasn't noise. This had a shape.

The second thing was more interesting, and it took me longer to admit I was seeing it: the denial had a shape too. For the reports, you had to squint to find the pattern. For the explanations, you didn't — they came pre-patterned, the same handful, everywhere, repeated with the confidence of arithmetic. Oxygen deprivation. A dying brain misfiring. Evolution's little mercy, a comfort program that switches on at the end. All plausible. All soothing. And almost none of it testable — which didn't slow anyone down. I knew that taste. It's the exact flavor I'd spent years collecting in the other hunt: the explanation working harder than the mystery. A lab coat two sizes too big.

Take the famous one. *The brain releases DMT when you die — that's all this is.* Repeated everywhere, calmly, case closed. Fine print: nobody has ever measured that in a dying human brain. Finer print: the people who actually take DMT — and there are forums full of them, extremely willing to share — mostly come back from a different country entirely. Geometry. Entities. Impossible architecture. Not the tunnel. Not the life review. Not your grandmother waiting in good light. So the official comfort is: an unmeasured chemical produces an experience it doesn't actually produce, and this settles the matter. It doesn't settle anything. It just ends the conversation — which, I was starting to suspect, was the entire job description.

And when I caught myself thinking that — lying in the dark at one a.m. with Ana's breathing beside me — I did the responsible thing. I made a note of both shapes, filed it, and told myself the jury was still out.

---

Ana found the notebook on a Tuesday.

Not found like discovered — found like it was on the kitchen table where I'd left it like an idiot, open, my handwriting all over a page with the words *veridical perception???* triple-underlined. She was passing with her coffee. She stopped. I watched her read upside down, which she does better than most people read right side up, and I sat there with my toast, acting casual, very calm, deeply interested in butter and jelly.

"Is this about Grandma?"

And here's where I have to be honest about the kind of man I am, because a better one would have said no. A better man would have said: it started there, but it's becoming something else, something with folders. What I said was nothing. I did a shrug. A *maybe, who knows, grief is strange* shrug. I let her write the story herself, because I could already see which story she was writing, and it was a kinder one than mine.

She put her hand on the back of my neck and kissed my shoulder.

"Good," she said. "It's good you're doing something with it. Even if it's weird. Your weird is how you love things."

Then she took her coffee and went to work, and I sat there with my toast and my triple-underlined question, feeling like a man who'd been given a medal by mistake and decided to keep it.

*Your weird is how you love things.* She wasn't wrong, is the terrible part. She was just early. The weird was how I loved things, and it was in the process of choosing a new thing to love, and neither of us stood at that kitchen table and asked the follow-up question. Follow-up questions were supposed to be my department.

---

The forums were a different education.

Reading is safe. Reading is a man in the audience. But after a few weeks I had questions the books weren't answering — and one in particular, the simplest one, the one under all the others — and there's only one place to take questions like that: to the strange and dark corners of the internet, where the people who never stopped asking went to live.

I know those corners. I've lurked them for years — for the maps, the doors, the conspiracies. I knew the ecosystem: ninety percent noise, nine percent lonely, one percent something else. What I'd never done was speak.

One night I typed a question into a thread. Nothing dramatic. Something like:

**Has anyone here had an NDE and is willing to talk about it?**

I looked at it for five minutes in complete silence.

Then I deleted it and closed the laptop.

I told myself it was because the thread was old, the crowd was wrong, I might get scammed, the question needed work. I came up with ten other reasons before I even stood up.

But the real reason was stupider. And it made me a hypocrite, just like at the funeral. I knew it even then: people who post questions like that get a label, and the label doesn't come off.

Some part of me — the part that maintained the barbecue persona, the part that kept the folder named Reading — wasn't ready to be seen looking. Not at this. Not with these people. You can be open-minded about almost anything and still flinch when it's your turn to be seen believing.

I want that on the record too.

Buddy skipped the front nine on a Wednesday.

We got to the course at seven like always, dew like always, and he got out of the car, walked to the first tee, considered the fairway stretching out in the morning light — and sat down next to the cart. Looked up at me. Not sad. Not sorry. Just: *I'll be supervising from here today, boss.*

I stood there with my driver in my hand for longer than I want to admit.

Then I loaded him in the cart, and we played the slowest, dumbest, most beautiful nine holes of my life, me hacking out of the rough, him riding shotgun with his ears up like a foreman inspecting a site, and I talked to him the whole way around, which I do anyway, but that morning I heard myself doing it. Somewhere on the sixth I said, out loud, to a dog, on an empty course: "You'd tell me if you knew something, right?"

Two thumps of the tail, a sideways look, and one small bark.

That's the closest thing to a straight answer anyone had given me in weeks.

---

Sunday, the market, Beard Man.

He was mid-sentence when I got there. He's always mid-sentence; I've decided the sentence started before I was born and I just catch my allotted segment of it. That week's segment was about a comet, or a prophet, or a comet that ruined a prophet — honestly I'd stopped tracking, because behind him, on the ground, where the boxes of unloved books go, there were two crates I hadn't seen before. Fresh meat. No — scratch that. Poor animals. One of these days I need a better word than hunting.

Estate sale, he said, when he saw me looking. Some professor. Whole house of books and the family kept the furniture. He said it the way he says everything — no bitterness, no judgment, a pipe reporting on water pressure — but the sentence sat in me anyway. A whole life of reading, and the family kept the furniture.

I went through the crates the way I always do, spine by spine, half-kneeling on the cobblestones while the market shouted around me. Mostly nothing — it's always mostly nothing; that's what makes it hunting. But not this time. Textbooks on ancient history. Condensed classics, the good ones. And then the sci-fi, which stopped me cold. My first thought — I remember it precisely, because of how wrong it turned out to be — was: *great taste.* No filler, no airport paperbacks, no gaps where the popular stuff should be. Ubik. A Canticle for Leibowitz. And in all that old cloth, one modern paperback read half to death: Blindsight. So the man had still been buying at the end. Still going. Even one Borges filed in with them — technically wrong, which I noted with the small satisfaction of catching someone else's filing error.

It took me a long time to understand that taste had nothing to do with it. These weren't his favorite books. They were his conclusions. I couldn't read them yet. I could only tell they were in order — someone else's order, built on a rule I didn't have.

At the bottom of the second crate, older spines. Cloth. Pre-war, some of them. Mythology, mostly — the serious kind, footnotes in three languages — and a few odd ones out: a survey of flood legends, something on calendar systems, a thin one with no title on the spine at all.

I bought eleven of them. Beard Man charged me almost nothing and threw in the twelfth free, "because twelve sounds better." Twelve apostles. Twelve tribes. Twelve months, twelve hours, twelve of everything that was ever supposed to be complete. Or the man just likes round dozens. I've spent an unreasonable amount of time on that one, and I want you to know I resent every minute.

I carried them home in two bags, the paper handles cutting into my fingers the whole way, pleasantly, like the opposite of a warning.

I'm going to stop here and let you have the moment, because I didn't get one. Nothing announced itself. No music played. The books went on the shelf by the window, the dogs inspected the bags for food, Ana asked how much, I lied by a small honest-marriage margin, and the evening went on the way ten thousand evenings had gone on before it.

One of those twelve books is the reason I'm telling you this story.

But that's Chapter Four's problem. That night, I just shelved it with the others, patted the stack the way you'd pat a dog you don't know yet, and went to see what Ana wanted to watch.

The wall of comfort held for exactly nine more days.
