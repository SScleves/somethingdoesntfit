# Monday

The sixth green at seven in the morning in December is the loneliest place I know, and I went there on purpose.

Nobody plays in December. The course is open because the course is always open, and the greenkeeper waves at you from the shed with the face of a man who's seen your type before — the ones who come out in the frost because something at home is worse. I'd slept forty minutes in the car — Ana says thirty — and none at all in the forty hours before that. My eyes felt like someone had sanded them.

I'd rung ahead from Frank's to ask if I could take the dog in the cart, which is not allowed. The woman in the shop said, "Buddy? The old one? Go on," because they'd known him for years and because nobody was going to see.

Frank, handing him back at the door of the flat, had looked about as tired as me.

"He snores," he said.

"I know."

"Like a *man*. Like a fat man on a train." He crouched down and put his forehead against Buddy's forehead for a second, which I'd never seen him do with anything. "He ate a croissant. I didn't give it to him. He took it. Off the table. With his face."

"Did you say no?"

"I said no. He looked at me." Frank stood up. "Go. Text me. Don't text me. I don't know. Text me."

---

Buddy rode beside me with a blanket over his back and his chin on the edge of the cart, watching the fairways go by like an old man revisiting a town he used to live in. He can't walk a hole anymore — hasn't since the vet. But he can ride, and he likes the air, and I think he likes being somewhere that smells of nothing but grass and cold and, faintly, the greenkeeper's diesel, which to him is probably perfume.

I put the phone face down on the bench by the sixth green. I'd promised Ana — face down, on silent, and I'd look at it when I'd finished the hole and not before. I'm a man who checks dashboards for a living. I check the weather app when I can see out of the window. Face down was a big ask, and she knew it, and she'd asked anyway.

Then I took the putter and three balls out onto the frost and started knocking them around, badly, while twenty kilometers away twelve people walked into a room with coffee.

---

I wasn't in the room, and I'm glad. What I know about it I got later — from Anneke, on the phone, and from Pieter, who heard it from someone who heard it from someone, which is how the bank hears everything. The two versions agree, which at a bank almost never happens, so I'll trust it.

The foundation's annual confirmation was item four. After the quarterly numbers and a thing about the Antwerp office. It had been item four for as long as anyone could remember, and it usually took about ninety seconds: the chair reads the trustees' names, someone proposes, someone seconds, hands go up, next.

This year, when the chair finished the names, the man from Wassenaar said, "Before we vote," and put a sheet of paper on the table. Slightly wavy, Pieter's source said. Like it had been wet.

The woman from Voorschoten put hers next to it.

And then — this is Anneke's version, and she's not a woman who decorates — there was a silence of the kind she'd only heard in a boardroom twice, both times in 2008, and in it nine more sheets came out of nine more folders and went down on the table. Not slammed. Just put down, the way you put down a card when everybody's already seen the hand. Leiderdorp's had a corner missing.

The chair looked at them. He looked at Anneke, who had never in four years spoken before a vote.

She said she'd approved the foundation's invoice thirty times and would like to read the line into the minutes. She read it. *Continuity reconciliation, per flagged person.* She read the number of persons on the last invoice, and on the first one, in 1994. She said she'd asked the foundation's director, on Saturday, by phone, what the line reconciled, and he'd said heritage, and she'd asked him to define heritage, and he hadn't.

Then — and this is the part Pieter's source loved — she asked for a vote to withhold confirmation of the trustees pending an audit. Bunnik seconded before she'd finished the sentence.

Eleven to one.

The one was the farmer from Woerden, who'd driven back from his boat at four in the morning and walked in at five to nine with my envelope still sealed in his coat. He voted no, Anneke said, not because he disagreed, but because he hadn't read it, and he was the kind of man who wouldn't vote for anything he hadn't read. She said she respected that enormously. She said she'd given him her own copy at the coffee break and watched him read it, and that he'd gone a color she hadn't seen on him before, and asked if he could change his vote, and been told no, that's not how minutes work.

Then she proposed that, pending the audit, the bank use its right under the funding agreement to put in interim trustees. She had names. One of them was hers.

That carried too.

By half past nine the foundation that had been counting a four-hundred-year-old bank's questions for thirty-one years had new trustees and a new chair. The old ones had been thanked for their service. The old man had been, in the language of these things, invited to step back. The quarter's schedule, the one with eleven addresses on it and Ana's mother's house and the word *Companion* in that font, was sitting on Anneke's desk unsigned, and would stay unsigned, she said, until somebody could explain to her satisfaction what a *visit* was.

Nobody shouted. Nobody was arrested. The minutes ran to a page and a half, and you could read them start to finish and not know anything had happened at all.

---

I'd holed out on the sixth by nine, in eleven strokes — a record, not a good one — and I'd sat down on the bench next to the phone and not turned it over. Buddy had gone to sleep in the cart. The sun had come round enough to start melting the frost in a line across the green, and I watched the line move, the way I used to watch progress bars when I was a junior and didn't know yet that they lie.

I did not pray. I'd like that known. I thought about it, though. I thought: if there is anybody, now would be a good time, and then I thought that if there was anybody they'd been watching this whole year and probably had opinions about the torn corner.

The phone buzzed against the bench at twenty to ten. It buzzed twice. I let it buzz a third time — I don't know why, some superstition about seeming calm in front of a phone — and then I turned it over.

"Mr Klippe."

"Mrs de Wit."

"It's done." Her voice was exactly the same as at six in the morning on her step. "Eleven to one. I'm the chair. The trustees are out. The schedule isn't signed and won't be." A pause. "I'm not going to give you the details. You weren't there, and I'd like that to stay true."

"Okay."

I couldn't say anything else. I was looking at the dog.

"He's asked to see you," she said. "The old one. Thursday, in the reading room. I'll be there. He'd like to talk, he says, because it no longer matters." Another pause. "Bring nothing. I'll tell you the terms there. I don't like telephones."

"Can I ask you something?"

"Once."

"Why am I still here?" It came out smaller than I meant. "They've done it before. The canal. The blue chair. It worked. Why not me?"

She didn't answer straight away. I heard her close a door on her end.

"Because it was cheap," she said. "Before. One man, one address, a plausible ending, nobody to connect it. That's the whole method — nobody ever sees a hand." A breath. "You've given two hundred people a page with their own name on it. Eleven of them sat in a room this morning holding one. If anything happens to you now, two hundred people know who did it, and they're the two hundred who do the work. A body would confirm every envelope." The faintest dryness. "You made yourself too expensive. I don't think you planned it."

"I didn't."

"No. I didn't think so." Then: "Go home. Sleep. There's frost."

She hung up.

---

I said "fuck" — quietly, to the green, not angry, just to have said something out loud to somebody. Then I sat there a while longer. The greenkeeper came past on the mower and lifted a hand and I lifted one back. I texted Ana one word, which was *eleven*, and then I texted Frank the same word, and Frank sent back the skull and then, about a minute later, *wait is that good*, and then, *ok it's good, I googled the board*, which I still don't understand.

Then I rang my dad, which I hadn't planned and don't do on Mondays, and he asked if something was wrong, and I said no, and we talked about his gutters for six minutes. It was the best conversation we've had in years. He doesn't know why.

The line of melted frost reached the far edge of the green, and the whole thing went from grey to that green that looks freshly painted.

Buddy woke up. He looked at me. He looked at the course. He made the small grunt that means he'd like to get down, which he hadn't made on a golf course since the summer, and I lifted him down — carefully, all twenty-eight kilos, my back registering a formal complaint — and set him on the grass.

He stood there with his legs not quite trusting him and his nose up in the cold. Then he walked out onto the sixth green. Four steps. Slow. He stopped about a meter from the hole, right in the sun, and lay down on it, on the actual putting surface, which you are absolutely not allowed to do, and put his chin on his paws.

The greenkeeper, at the far end of the fairway, saw. He looked for a second. Then he turned the mower round and went to cut something else.
