# The night before

We left on Sunday after dark, with a Doberman in the back and twelve envelopes in a supermarket bag on my lap, and I'd like to say we had a plan. We had a list and a full tank. Ana had a thermos. I had a stomach that had stopped working on Friday and hadn't sent anyone to explain.

Ana drove. She's a better driver than me in every way you can measure — and in a few you can't, like how she doesn't take it personally when a Volkswagen cuts in. I only drive when she lets me, which is mostly when she wants to sleep. She drove the way she does anything she's decided on, both hands, radio off, and Mora's head between the seats breathing on the gearstick like a second engine.

Buddy was at Frank's. That had been its own negotiation. Ana's mother's house was on the schedule — "Companion," in a font — so her mother was at her sister's in Gouda, and that left Frank, who lives alone in a flat with a bike in the hallway and a fridge full of oat milk and had never once been responsible for anything that breathes.

"What does he eat?"

"His food. It's in the bag. Not your food."

"What if he wants my food?"

"He'll want your food. He's a dog. Say no."

"I can't say no to that face, mate. Look at it." Buddy was looking at him from the bottom of the stairs to the flat — Frank's on the first floor — with the expression of an old man who has been told about the stairs and is filing a complaint. "Do I carry him?"

"You carry him."

He carried him. Twenty-eight kilos of grey dog up a narrow Rotterdam staircase, Frank going red and saying *fuck* on every third step, Buddy calm in his arms like a bishop being moved to a new diocese. At the top Frank put him down and said, "I still don't believe any of it, you know," and I said I knew, and he said, "Go on. Go. I've got the dog," and did the skull at me with his actual face, which I'd never seen him do without a phone.

---

The list was twelve names. Twelve people who would sit in a room at nine on Monday morning and, at item four, confirm the trustees of a foundation that had been invoicing their bank per flagged person since 1994. Eleven of them had never heard my name. The twelfth was Anneke, who'd had her sheet for a week and read it twice, which I'm told is a thing she does not do.

Each envelope had a sheet. For the board the last box said something different from the others. Their names weren't missing from the list — they were the list, practically. So it said: *On Monday you will be asked to confirm these trustees. This is what the foundation charges, and for what. These are the people it keeps. These are your staff who are not kept. Please check before you vote.*

*Please check.* I'd rewritten that line eleven times. Every version that sounded clever sounded crazy.

"Read me the first one," Ana said.

"Leiderdorp. A woman. Treasury, twenty years. Two kids, one horse."

"How do you know about the horse?"

"She's got a horse in her profile picture."

"That's not knowing about a horse, James. That's a picture."

She was right. I wrote *horse?* next to it, like a man doing something.

---

Leiderdorp was a letterbox. A neat brick house with a light on downstairs and the blue flicker of a television, and I'd decided — we'd decided, at the kitchen table, with the kettle — not to knock. A man at your door at nine on a Sunday night with a document is a man you call the police about. A letter is a letter.

So I walked up the path with the envelope like it was a parcel of something alive, and I put it through the slot, and the slot had one of those brush things inside, and the envelope stuck. Half in, half out. I pushed. It folded. I pulled it back to straighten it and the brush took the corner off.

Inside, a dog started barking. A small one — the kind that barks with its whole body.

I stood there with half a sheet of evidence in my hand and the other half somewhere in a stranger's hallway, and the television went quiet.

I ran. I'm not proud of it. I ran back down the path and got in the car and said "Go, go, go," and Ana didn't go, because Ana doesn't go for anybody who says it three times. She looked at me, and at the torn corner in my hand, and said, "Did you just post half of it?"

"Most of it. Eighty percent. The important part."

"Which part's in your hand?"

I looked. The bottom right corner. The word *check.*

"Fuck," I said.

She laughed so hard she had to put her forehead on the wheel, and Mora barked once at nothing, in solidarity, and the hall light came on in the house and we drove away with our lights off for the first twenty meters like idiots in a film, and then turned them on because it's illegal.

"Good start," Ana said. "Very professional."

---

After that I had a system. I'm good at systems. I folded each envelope once more, and I held the flap of the letterbox up with two fingers, and I fed the envelope in slowly, like you'd feed a pill to a dog — and I want it understood that this was the most competent I was all night.

The Hague, two, ten minutes apart. Both letterboxes, both fine, both in streets so quiet I could hear the canal. At the second one a cat watched me from a windowsill with the face of a cat who had seen men post things before and thought less of all of them.

Delft. Leidschendam. Wassenaar. At Wassenaar the house had a gate, and the gate had an intercom, and the letterbox was on the far side of the gate. We sat in the car for four minutes looking at it.

"Throw it over," Ana said.

"It's a bank document."

"It's an envelope. Throw it."

I threw it over. It went beautifully, a little spin on it, and landed face down on a wet gravel drive about a meter from the door, and I stood at the gate in the drizzle and thought about everything I'd ever read about the history of the world, the edits, the forty-one books, the hill in Turkey, the professor in his blue chair, and all of it came down to this — a man in a damp coat watching an envelope get rained on in Wassenaar.

A light went on in the porch. The door opened. A man in a sweater came out, looked at the envelope, looked at the gate, looked at me.

"Klippe?" he said.

I didn't say anything. My mouth wouldn't.

He bent and picked the envelope up and shook the water off it. "Anneke rang. Four of us, this afternoon. She said a man might come with an envelope and that I should read it and then ring her." He looked at the car behind me, at the Doberman's head in the back window, ears up, like a hood ornament. "She didn't say you'd throw it."

"There's a gate."

"There's a bell on the gate."

"I didn't want to ring the bell."

He nodded like that was a reasonable thing for a grown man to say. "I'll read it tonight." Then, because he was Dutch and it was raining: "Do you want a coffee?"

I said we had more to do. He said, "Then go," and went in, and I walked back to the car, and Ana said, "Well?" and I said, "Anneke's been ringing people," and Ana started the engine and said, "Good," in a voice that told me how scared she'd been and wasn't going to say.

So that was two already turned. That's the thing nobody tells you about doing something like this — that halfway through, it stops being yours. Anneke had rung four. One of the four, in Voorschoten, met us at the door at half past eleven in a dressing gown with her own copy already printed, because Anneke had scanned hers and sent it, and she held out my envelope back to me and said, "I don't need two. Give it to someone who hasn't got one." It was the first time anyone had given me anything back in the whole year. I sat in the car afterwards and couldn't talk for about a minute, and Ana let me, and Mora put her nose in my ear, which is wet and enormous and didn't help at all, and helped.

---

The wrong house was in Zoetermeer.

Number fourteen. I walked up to number fourteen, I fed the envelope in with my two-finger pill technique, and I was halfway back down the path when the door opened behind me and a very large man in boxer shorts said, "Hey."

I turned around. He had my envelope in his hand.

"This isn't me," he said.

"It's — fourteen?"

"Fourteen B." He pointed. Next door. A second door I hadn't seen, round the side, with a second bell. He looked at the name on the envelope. "That's the guy upstairs. He's never in. He's got a boat."

"Okay."

"What is it?"

I have spent eleven years at a bank explaining things to people who don't want them explained, and in that moment, in Zoetermeer, at a quarter to midnight, with a stranger in boxer shorts holding the end of the world, the best I had was: "It's about his pension."

"At midnight?"

"It's urgent. For a pension."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Okay, man," and posted it through the B door himself, carefully, with two fingers, and went back inside. I'd like to think he went to bed and never thought about it again. I'd like to think a lot of things.

"Wrong house?" Ana said.

"Wrong house."

"Was it a handler?"

"It was a guy with a boat. Next door. His neighbor's got a boat."

"So it was nobody."

"It was a very large nobody in his underwear."

She nodded. "This is how they're going to make the film," she said. "The underwear guy. They'll cut everything else."

---

The van picked us up after Zoetermeer, on the A12.

White, no windows in the back, the kind every plumber in the country drives. It came onto the motorway at a junction behind us and stayed there. Three cars back. Then two. Then three again. Ana saw it first. She said, "There's a van," in exactly the voice she'd use for *there's a sign,* and I turned round, and Mora, who'd been asleep across the back seat with her legs in the air like a dead insect, turned over and sat up and looked out the back window and went completely still. She never goes still. She's a Doberman. She was built still and then someone forgot to turn it on.

"What do we do?"

"Nothing." My voice came out about a third higher than normal. "We drive. If they're following us they're following us to a letterbox. That's a fingerprint. They don't do fingerprints."

"You don't know that."

"I've read their paperwork."

"You've read their paperwork," Ana said, and changed lanes, and the van didn't, and then it did.

Forty kilometers. I counted. I counted the way I count everything when I'm scared, which is out loud in my head — exits, kilometer markers, the number of times Mora's ears moved. I held the supermarket bag of envelopes on my lap with both arms like it was a child that might jump out. I thought about Henk Boersma in his canal. I thought about Noor's message, *there's a van on my street,* and then nothing. I thought, very clearly, *if this is it, I'd like it to be after the next envelope, at least, so I've done eight.*

At the junction for Gouda, the van indicated — politely, in plenty of time — and came off, and was gone.

Mora watched the empty road behind us for another minute. Then she lay back down with a groan and put her chin on my seat.

"That was a van," Ana said.

"That was a van."

"I've decided it was a van."

"Good decision."

We never talked about it again. I've thought about it plenty. I think it was a van, and I think those were the longest forty kilometers of my life, and I think both of those things are true, and that's what they do to you, in the end — you can never again just see a van.

---

At midnight I was at the depot.

"I'm not coming in," Ana said, in the car park, with the engine running. "I'm sitting here with the dog. If you're not out by one, I'm coming in with the dog, and I'm not asking anyone's badge."

"That's —"

"That's not a negotiation."

The building looked the way it always looked, low and long and grey behind its car park, the foundation's name on the sign and a smaller name under it, and no lights on the desk floor where I'd eaten a sandwich at desk forty during the drill. One light upstairs, behind the glass door.

Aylin had propped the outer door open with a fire extinguisher. That's the detail I can't get over. In a year of watching her — the pencil, the cards squared to the edge of the desk, the visitor book in its own grey box — I'd never seen her let a single thing lean on anything. A fire extinguisher, against a door, at midnight. It was the loudest thing she ever said to me.

She walked me up without a word. The reading room was lit by the one lamp on the long table. The visitor book was on it, open at 1931, and beside it a flat scanner, the kind libraries use, already warm.

"Forty minutes," she said. "He reads on Sunday nights. He comes at one."

"Okay."

She sat down at her desk with her back to me and picked up a card, and I understood the deal. She wouldn't watch. She wouldn't help. She wouldn't stop me.

My hands were shaking so badly I put the first page in upside down. Then the second. Somewhere around page ten I got a rhythm — lift, flatten, lid, button, the little light crawling across — and I stopped reading the words, on purpose, because I had forty minutes and because I was afraid of whose names I'd see. You can't not see some. *Seated.* *Quiet.* *Deceased.* *Quiet.* The same few words for ninety-five years in a dozen different hands, and I turned the pages like a man stacking plates.

And under my feet, the whole time, the floor hummed.

I'd heard it at the drill and filed it under air conditioning. It wasn't. It was lower, steadier — a big thing kept running in a room under a room — and somewhere around page sixty I stopped scanning for a second and just stood there with my hand flat on the table, feeling it come up through the wood. The seats weren't a list. They were a place. Right there. Under the carpet tiles.

Mora would have hated it. She hates the dishwasher.

I finished at twenty to one. I put the book back exactly how it had been, open at the front, and slid the memory card into the last envelope, the twelfth — Anneke's — and stood up.

"The last column," Aylin said, without turning around. "Next to you. What does it say now?"

I looked. December. My name, in her hand. One word.

"Pending."

"Good." She turned a card over. "When it changes, I'd like to be the one who writes it. I think I've earned that much."

I went down through the dark floor and out past the fire extinguisher and Ana had the car moving before my door was closed.

"Well?"

"I've got it. All of it."

"And?"

"The floor hums."

She didn't ask what that meant. She knew me well enough to know I didn't know either.

---

Three more after that. Utrecht, a letterbox, no lights. Bunnik, a man who opened the door himself at two in the morning because he couldn't sleep, read the first paragraph standing in his socks under the hall light, said "Fuck" very quietly, then "Thank you," and closed the door. And one we couldn't do — a farmhouse past Woerden at the end of a dyke road, no car, no lights, nobody. I put the envelope through the door anyway.

"He's away," Ana said. "That's one who votes without reading it."

"I know."

"Ring Anneke."

"It's three in the morning."

"Ring Anneke."

I rang Anneke. She answered on the second ring in a voice that had not been asleep, listened, said, "I'll try him. Thank you. Go to the next one," and hung up.

"I like her," Ana said.

"She approved the invoice thirty times."

"And then she read it."

---

There was no next one. There was only Anneke's.

She lives in a terraced house in Rotterdam-Kralingen with a bike chained to the railing and a pot of something dead on the step. We got there at ten to six, in the first grey, and I sat in the car with the twelfth envelope for a while because I'd suddenly forgotten how to stand up. A year. Twelve envelopes. A torn corner in my coat pocket that said *check.*

I walked up the three steps. I lifted my hand to knock.

The door opened before I did.

She was dressed. Not in a dressing gown — properly dressed, for work, at ten to six on a Monday morning, shoes on, glasses on her head. She looked like she'd been standing in her hall for some time.

I held out the envelope. "The visitor book. All of it. Nineteen thirty-one to now."

She took it. She didn't open it.

"I've read it," she said. "The sheet. All of it. Twice." She looked past me at the car, at Ana with her head back against the headrest, at the dog's ears in the back window. "Go home, Mr Klippe."

"The farmhouse — Woerden —"

"I got him at four. He's driving back. He's not happy." She almost smiled. "Go home."

She closed the door. I stood on her step and looked at the dead plant for a while.

When I got back in the car, Ana didn't ask. She looked at me and I said, "Twelve," and she put her forehead down on the steering wheel — just for a second — and then lifted it and started the engine.

"Frank's," she said. "Buddy. Then bed. And then you're going to go and hit a ball with a stick and not be anywhere near any of this."

"It's Monday."

"Monday you hit a ball with a stick and keep your phone face down." She pulled out. "You've done your bit, Mulder. Now you just have to not be in the room."

I was asleep before Kralingen. The last thing I remember is Mora's chin landing on my shoulder, heavy and warm and smelling, I'm sorry to say, very strongly of whatever she'd found in Anneke's front garden.
