# The final conversation

Aylin booked it. That's the part I keep coming back to. The Thursday after the vote I came up the stairs with nothing in my hands — Anneke had said nothing, and I'm a literal man — and Aylin looked up from her cards and said, "He's in. He's got the room till four. I've put you down," in exactly the voice she'd used on the second of December. Then she wrote something in the visitor book. I didn't look.

He was at the long table. Anneke was next to him, not by the window this time — next to him, with a notebook, like a lawyer sitting with a client who's agreed to talk. No tray. The lamp on. He looked, I'm sorry to say, extremely well. Better than me. I'd slept about nine hours since Sunday.

There was no dog under the table. First table in a year. I noticed it the way you notice a tooth is gone.

"Mr Klippe. Sit. You've brought nothing."

"I was told."

"Good. People have been bringing things to this table for forty years. I never needed any of it." He folded his hands. "I've been retired. I'm to have an office and no keys. I find I don't mind. I'd like to talk — because I enjoy it, and because it no longer matters. So. Ask."

I'd made a list all week and thrown it away that morning in the car park.

"Why does it never come out?"

He laughed, the small pleased sound from December. "Oh, that's the right one. Everybody asks *what is it*. Nobody asks why it never comes out." He tapped the table near Anneke's notebook. "Write that down."

"I'm not your secretary," Anneke said, and didn't write it down.

---

"It comes out all the time," he said. "That's what you've got wrong. It comes out every few years, and every time, we're there first, telling it bigger. You know the phrase — soft disclosure. Films easing people toward the truth." He shook his head. "It was never disclosure. It's inoculation. Show people the truth as a film and they can never again hear it as a fact."

"That's a nice line. Did you practice it?"

"For about thirty years. *Men in Black* — every man who finds the agency is now the man in the film. *I Origins* — a good theory, you can track a person by the eye across lives, so we made it a love story with a twist and nobody ever said it again with a straight face. The moon as a collector of souls — that one was just for fun. Sometimes we have fun. *Inception*, so the writers we nudge would think their best ideas were their own dreams. *Dracula*, so *we don't die* belongs to a man in a cape."

"Right."

"And Walt."

"Walt Disney."

"Walt was our best customer. He never knew."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me like a sneeze — a real laugh, in that room, with the lamp — and once it started I couldn't stop it, and I had to put my hand over my face.

"Something funny?"

"Walt fucking Disney," I said. "Sorry. You've got a building full of — and you're telling me *Walt Disney*. Is he frozen? Is that next? Is he downstairs?"

"Freezing," he said, quite seriously, "is meaningless. I'll come to that."

"Course you will."

Anneke was looking at her notebook. I think — I'm not sure — the corner of her mouth moved.

---

"The second thing is the mirror," he said. "Sometimes a person is handed a true thing. Not finds it. Is handed it. A writer in California, 1974 — pink light, he said. It told him his son was sick, and he took the boy to a doctor and the boy *was*." He spread his hands. "And then he spent eight years and a million words trying to prove to himself it hadn't happened. We didn't touch him. The true thing, in a mind trained to doubt, eats itself. Now anyone who has what he had thinks of him and thinks *madman*."

"You've got a shelf," I said. "The professor had a shelf. VALIS was on it."

"Of course it was. He was a careful man."

"So which is it — you made him or you just read him?"

"We read everybody, Mr Klippe."

---

"The internet frightened us," he said. "I was in this room when it arrived. For five years I thought it was the end — people telling each other things faster than we could tell the other version. Then the social part came. Then the machines that write." He smiled. "We'd misunderstood our own job for four hundred years. We thought it was to make people believe the wrong thing. It's not. It's to make them doubt everything. Doubt is cheaper than belief. A machine that makes everything sound equally possible is the best employee we've ever had, and it doesn't want a seat."

"You're saying AI is yours."

"I'm saying it doesn't need to be."

I thought about Frank and his skull and his tools that were going to replace all of us. I thought about page 212. I didn't say any of it. I was scared he'd say *yes* and I'd believe him, which is exactly the problem he'd just described, and I was sitting in the middle of it, believing.

---

"The seats," I said. "Under us. I can hear them."

"You found the seats and thought they were the point. They all do. A few hundred people kept alive through something, coming out, inheriting." He made a face like he'd smelled milk. "That's what they're told. The seats are fuel. Promise a middle manager a seat and he'll keep the machine running for thirty years. Give him one and he'll do anything to deserve it. Nobody's being saved. Freezing —"

"Is meaningless. Yeah. Walt."

"— is meaningless," he said, slightly louder.

"And the boy in Tibet?" I don't know why I asked that one. It was on the list I'd thrown away.

"Real." He said it like he was confirming a train time. "The search finds something, often enough. We left it as public as possible. Robes. Cameras. Once it's done in a palace, it can't be done in a kitchen. A boy who points at his dead grandfather's pen and says *mine* — his mother thinks of the palace, and laughs."

I thought about a hand cracked at the knuckles. I didn't say that either.

---

"And now," he said, and leaned in — not for secrecy, for pleasure — "the one you came for."

"I didn't come for anything. I was told to come."

"You came for the forty-one." He waited. I let him. "You think we took a fact out of those books. *Given* to *arose*. We didn't take a fact. We took the passing. How a thing goes from a person to a person. And the thing that was passed —" he lowered his voice — "is how to keep what you are across the gap. Nobody here is frozen, Mr Klippe. Some of us simply remember. Life to life. That's what we took out of every book in the world. And kept."

The floor hummed.

"So you come back," I said. "Same people. Same building. Same names, more or less."

"More or less."

"With the manual."

"With the manual."

And then I said it, and I hadn't planned to, and it came up from a kitchen floor in November, the fourth night, and I said it the way you'd say it to a dog.

"It's not for staying."

He looked at me.

"The remembering," I said. "What you kept. I had it — not from a book. On a floor. And the thing you're shown, when you're shown it, isn't how to come back. It's how to leave. Remembering is so you don't have to do this again. You've got the instructions for the door, and you've been using them to go round and round the same room."

It was very quiet. Even the floor seemed to go quiet, which it didn't, I know it didn't, but that's how I remember it.

"Yes," he said.

That was all, for a second. Then: "We chose to stay. What would you have done? On one side of the door, everything you've ever been afraid of. On the other, this room, these people, the work, a chair you know. We chose the room. Every time it costs us more. Every time there's less of us to bring." He looked at his hands. "We don't know where the door goes. We stopped wanting to know a long time ago."

I wanted to feel sorry for him. I checked, the way you check a pocket. I didn't.

---

"So tell it," I said. "If you're done. If it doesn't matter. Let it out."

"It can't come out," Anneke said.

We both looked at her. She hadn't spoken since *I'm not your secretary.*

"I've spent a week on this," she said. "Not his version. Mine. Suppose everybody knew, tomorrow, as a fact — that it goes on, that fear's what scatters you. What happens on Thursday? Nobody goes to work. The hospitals, the water, the grid — all of it runs on people who are more afraid of dying than of their jobs." She closed the notebook. "They made sure of that. It's been built that way on purpose for a very long time. Take the fear out fast and you don't get enlightenment. You get collapse. And after a collapse with no continuity, the people who run what's left are killers and savages. That's not his theory. I've read the recovery plans. That's what they're for."

"So the deal's the same as it always was," I said.

"No. The deal's mine now." She opened the notebook again, to a page she'd already written. "The nightly job goes. Frank closes it, through a ticket, like a normal person. The line on the invoice steps down — not to nothing, there's a contract, but down. The annex gets rewritten by HR, not by a foundation. We'll help where we see fit. We won't help it collapse. Continuity stays. And you keep quiet." She looked up. "The bank, the names, the foundation. Nothing."

"And him?" I didn't say the name. I didn't have to.

"Julian Kalder's contract is under review. He didn't come in Monday. He's not answering." She paused. "He's not the bank's problem anymore. He may be somebody's. I've chosen not to ask whose."

The old man smiled at the table. It was the only time I wanted to hit him.

"And you can write a book," Anneke said. "You asked me the day you gave me my sheet whether you would ever be allowed to. Under your own name. It claims nothing and names nobody. If anyone asks, it's a novel."

"We've been publishing those for four hundred years," the old man said. "Nobody reads the middle."

"I read the middle," I said.

"Yes. Well. There's one of you."

"Agreed," I said. To her, not to him. "All of it."

"Good." She capped her pen. "Is there anything else?"

"One thing. It's small."

"Say it."

I didn't say it out loud. I leaned over and said it to her, low, close to her ear, like a man telling a secret at a wedding, and the old man watched us and didn't hear it, and I've never written it down and I'm not going to now. It wasn't about the bank. It was mine.

She sat back. She looked at me for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That I can do." And she wrote one line in the notebook, and turned the page so it was face down.

---

He stood up. He was old and it took him a moment. Anneke didn't help and he didn't ask.

"Leave it," he said to me.

Two words. The ones Pieter had used. The ones the whole building was built on.

"Not as a threat. As advice from a man who's had the keys. You've bought time — a generation, perhaps, in this part of the world. Then it starts again somewhere else, under a different word. You'll be dead by then. So will I." He looked at the door. "When it's your turn, do what you did on the floor. And go. Don't come back. I'd like one of us to get it right."

He went out. Anneke stayed sitting with her hand flat on her notebook.

"Is he right?" I said. "Eighteen months and you're him?"

"I don't know." She stood. "I approved an invoice thirty times without reading it. I'm reading things now." She picked up her coat. "He's got the room till four. Stay, if you like. It's the only room in the building that doesn't hum."

That wasn't true. It hummed. But I stayed.

I sat at the long table with nothing on it and went through what he'd said, one thing at a time, like a list of alerts at the end of a shift. And I couldn't check any of it. Not one film. Not one writer. Not Walt. Every piece fit the next one perfectly, all of it, one long clean line from Hollywood to the floor under my feet — and that's what bothered me. Real things don't fit like that. Real things have a torn corner somewhere. He'd done to me what they do to everybody: handed me a story so neat it was impossible to believe and impossible to throw away.

I decided I'd work out which parts I believed later. Somewhere with grass. Somewhere with a dog.

When I went down, Aylin didn't look up. But the visitor book was open on her desk, and next to December, next to my name, the word *Pending* had a neat pencil line through it, and nothing written beside it yet.
