# Leave it

Nothing happened.

I want to say that first, because I'd been waiting for something for a year, and I think anyone who's listened to me this long has too. The date at the bottom of page 212 — the review date, end of the quarter — came on a Tuesday and went. On the Wednesday I went to the course at seven with the dogs. The retirees were on the third. The dew was on everything. Nobody was standing on any green with a flask. The world didn't end. It never once has, as far as I can tell, which is the main argument against it.

Ana's mother came back from Gouda with a cake and a list of things her sister had said about her. The hat went back on its own hook. Mora checked every room for the postman, twice, then ate a sock. Buddy went to his blankets by the radiator and lay down with that long sigh that sounds like a man getting into a bath, and I stood in the hall and thought: *this is enough.* Then I thought, *don't go looking.* And then I laughed out loud in my own hall, alone, because I'd thought that exact sentence once before on the seventh tee in April, and look how that went.

Ana heard me from the kitchen.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You laughed at nothing in the hall."

"I'm fine."

"You're laughing at nothing in the hall, Mulder. That's not fine. That's a symptom." She came and looked at me. "Is it over?"

I thought about it properly. I owed her that.

"I don't know," I said. "I think it's smaller."

"Smaller's good." She went back to the kettle. "I'll take smaller."

The invoice came on the first working day of January, like it always did. I didn't go looking for it. That's not true — I had a filter on it from October and it popped up on my second screen at 08:51 with a little blue dot, and I sat there for a while with my coffee and didn't click it.

Then I clicked it.

*Continuity reconciliation.* Same post box. Same font. Same everything, except the number, which for the first time since 1994 had gone *down.* Not to nothing — there's a contract, Anneke had said, and contracts at a bank die slower than people. One step down. A clean amount, the kind a committee agrees on. In the corner of the PDF, a set of initials in blue ink that hadn't been there in thirty years of scans: *A. de W.*

Thirty-one years of steps going up. Six of them. One of them the week I'd typed a question into a forum at two in the morning with my handle that nobody else would ever get. And now one going the other way.

I printed it. I don't print things. I folded it in four and put it in my wallet behind my driving license, where it still is, getting soft at the corners.

---

The job took a month.

Anneke had said it would be boring, and she'd meant it as a term, and it was. That was the whole beauty of it. There was no midnight. Nobody pulled a cable. On the second Monday in January I opened a ticket like a normal person, in the normal queue, with the normal template.

*Summary: Unowned scheduled job rcl-agg (02:14 daily) — no owner, no documented purpose, outbound transfer to external address. Request review and decommission.*

*Priority: Low.*

That was Frank's idea. "Low," he said, leaning over my shoulder with a sandwich. "You put High, someone reads it. You put Low, it goes through on a Friday."

"That's horrible."

"That's the bank, mate." He chewed. "Assign it to me."

"You can't assign things to yourself."

"I can if you assign it to me." He pointed at the screen with the sandwich and dropped a piece of tomato on my keyboard. "Go on."

I assigned it to him.

It sat in the queue for nine days, which is normal. Someone from change management asked for a business justification and Frank wrote *no business owner identified* and nothing else, which, it turns out, is the most powerful sentence in a four-hundred-year-old bank. Someone from security asked if the outbound address was known, and Frank wrote *no*, and someone from security wrote *ok* and approved it. Pieter signed the change. He didn't ask me anything. He looked at the ticket number on his screen for a second longer than you look at a ticket number, and clicked.

On the last Thursday in January, at 02:14, nothing ran.

I know because I was up. Of course I was up. I was on the kitchen floor with Buddy, who had decided that three in the morning was the time for a drink and then a long conversation about it, and I had the laptop open on the tiles and the dashboard up, and at 02:14 the line where the job had been for thirty-one years just wasn't. No error. No alert. A little gap in a graph, the way a tooth goes.

I sat there and watched the gap for about four minutes. Buddy put his chin on my foot.

"That's it," I told him.

He didn't care. He was right not to.

Frank closed the ticket at 09:03 the next morning with the comment *Decommissioned. No impact observed.* and sent me the skull, and then a second message: *No impact observed lol.* And then, a minute later, a third: *still don't believe any of it btw.* And then: *but I believe the invoice.*

We filed one more, the same week. A dull one. Against the new tools — the ones I'd read all four hundred pages of, the defaults — asking that the rule on page 212 be reviewed as *possibly over-broad, suppresses legitimate unowned-job detection*. Priority low. Frank's name. It went to the vendor's queue, wherever that is, which is where things go to die.

"They'll never touch it," Frank said.

"Probably not."

"Then why'd we file it?"

"Because it's wrong," I said, "and you write it down when something's wrong."

He thought about that. "That's the least cool thing you've ever said."

"Thank you."

A parcel came in February. Not for me — for Ana. A Leiden postmark, no return address, a conservator's careful handwriting on the label. Inside, flat between two boards, a page from something very old that had been torn once and wasn't anymore. You had to hold it up to the window to find the join.

No note.

Ana held it up to the window for a long time.

"Who's this from?"

"A woman who repairs paper."

"Is she okay?"

"She's quiet."

Ana lowered the page. "That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "She's okay."

She looked at me to see if I was lying, which she can do from across a room, and then she put the page back between the boards and put the boards on the shelf next to her grandmother's photo, where they still are. Mora sniffed the shelf and was told no.

---

I started writing in March.

I'd been allowed to, under my own name, a book that claims nothing and names nobody — and the second I sat down to do it I found out that claiming nothing is really hard. Every page I wrote wanted to accuse someone. I'd write a sentence and look at it and think *based on what?* and delete it. I deleted a lot. Some nights all I kept was the dogs.

Ana read the first chapter over my shoulder, which I hate.

"You made me sound mean."

"You are mean."

"I'm *right*. That's different." She read a bit more. "You can't put *fuck* that many times."

"I counted. It's less than you say in a day."

"I'm not a book." She kissed the top of my head. "It's good. It's weird. Your weird is —"

"I know."

"Don't let them make it normal."

On the Wednesday after that I took Buddy to the course in the cart, early, and played the seventh. My drive went in the trees. Obviously. I stood at the edge of the rough looking for the ball, and Buddy, from the cart, did the thing he does — stared at a spot in the long grass with total confidence, which is wrong about half the time.

It was there.

"Okay," I said. "Show-off."

He thumped his tail on the seat twice. I chipped out sideways, like I always do, and we went on.
