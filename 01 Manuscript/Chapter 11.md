# Chapter Eleven

I'd verified six entries by December, and six is not exciting. Six is two afternoons a week for three months, forty euros of postage, and a lot of standing at a kitchen table with a finger on a line.

But six is enough to see something, and what I saw made no sense to me, so I did a thing I had never done in eleven years.

I asked Beard Man a question.

---

You have to understand the ecosystem. Beard Man and I have a relationship built entirely on him talking and me listening, and it has functioned beautifully for over a decade precisely because nothing is required of either of us. He does not know my name. I do not know his. He is somewhere between sixty and eighty; the beard makes it impossible and I suspect that's the point.

That Sunday it was four degrees and raining in a committed way, and he had the tarpaulin up over the trestle tables and was standing under it in a coat that has its own weather.

He was mid-sentence. He is always mid-sentence.

"— which they'll tell you was a granary," he said, as I arrived, as though I'd stepped out for two minutes and not two weeks, "and I say, fine, a granary, wonderful, show me the grain."

"Morning."

"They never show you the grain." He moved a box six inches for no reason. "Never in my life has anyone shown me the grain."

I stood there and let three or four minutes of it go past, the way you let a bus go past, and then I did the new thing.

"Can I ask you something properly?"

He stopped.

I'd like to describe what that was like. It was like watching a machine that has been running unattended in a basement for forty years notice that somebody has come down the stairs.

"Properly," he said.

"Properly."

He put down the box. "Go on then."

---

"The old myths," I said. "The ones where somebody brings people something. Fire, farming, writing, the calendar. There's always a giver."

"There is."

"What happens to the giver?"

And Beard Man looked at me for a good three seconds, in the rain, under a tarpaulin, with his hands in his coat, and said:

"Nothing good."

Then he pulled a crate out from under the table, turned it over, sat on it, and gave me forty minutes.

---

I'm going to give you the shape of it rather than all of it, because he doesn't tell things in order and about a third of it was, as ever, nonsense. But this is the part that stood up.

"Prometheus," he said. "Everybody starts at Prometheus. Brings fire. Gets chained to a rock, gets his liver eaten daily, for ever. That's the punishment for the gift. And people think it's a story about theft, but read it again — he isn't punished for stealing. He's punished for *distributing*. The gods don't want it back. They want it not to be down here."

"That's one."

"You want more? Have Enki. Mesopotamian. Gives humanity the arts of civilisation — the *me*, they're called, the whole toolkit, law, weaving, kingship, the lot — and depending on which tablet you read he either gets it stolen off him by a goddess while drunk, or he's the one who wanted the flood *not* to happen and had to warn a man in secret, through a wall, because he wasn't allowed to warn him directly."

"Through a wall."

"Through a reed wall. Talks to the wall, so he can say he never told the man." He was enjoying himself. "That's a very lawyerly god."

"And the flood?"

"Ah, well, that's the other half, isn't it. The flood is what happens *because* people got the toolkit. The gods send it because we've got too loud, too many, too capable. Depends on the version. Sometimes it's noise. Sometimes it's rebellion. Sometimes there's no reason at all and someone just decides."

I said, carefully, "And in every version somebody gets warned."

"Every single version." He held up a finger. "That's your interesting bit, and nobody notices it. There is always one who tips them off. Enki. Prometheus tells his own son. Noah gets it from the top, which is the boring version. In India, Manu, a fish tells him. There's always a leak."

---

He went on. I'm compressing.

The Watchers in the Enoch material — a whole class of beings whose entire crime is *teaching*. Metalwork, cosmetics, root-cutting, astronomy, the making of weapons. They come down, they teach, and for that they're bound in the valleys of the earth until the judgement. Not for violence. For curriculum.

Thoth in Egypt, giving writing, and the king in Plato — Beard Man got there before I did, which annoyed me — complaining that writing will *ruin memory*, which is a very strange thing for a story to be worried about unless somebody, somewhere, was actually worried about it.

Quetzalcoatl, who brings maize and the calendar and gets driven out.

And then the one he saved for last, and this is the one I went home with.

"Then there's the other trick," he said. "The rewrite."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning look at what happens to the old ones when the new lot arrive." He counted on his fingers, badly, because of the gloves. "The Persian gods become demons in India. The Indian gods become demons in Persia. Same words, opposite sign. Baal is a god with a job for a thousand years and then he's the devil. The horned one in the woods who looks after the animals — he's fine, he's popular, he's got shrines — and then one day he's got the horns and the hooves and he's the enemy. Nobody invented the devil. They just re-labelled the local staff."

"That's an argument about conquest," I said. "The winners demote the losers' gods. That's not mysterious, that's just — politics."

"Course it is." He seemed delighted I'd said it. "Absolutely it is. That's exactly what it is."

"So?"

"So notice which ones get demoted first." He leaned forward on the crate. "It isn't the war gods. Nobody bothers with the war gods; you can always use a war god. It's the ones who *gave people something*. The teachers. The ones with the knowledge in their hands. Those are the ones who come back as devils."

---

I stood in the rain and did the thing I do, which is try to break it immediately.

"Give me a counter-example," I said.

"A what?"

"A myth where someone brings knowledge and it goes well. Where the giver is honoured, ends happily, gets a temple and a pension."

He thought about it. He actually thought about it, for longer than I expected, staring at a box of water-damaged atlases.

"There's honouring," he said slowly. "There's plenty of honouring. Thoth's got temples. But they're all —" he did a rolling gesture — "they're all *after*. He's honoured as a god, up there, at a distance. I can't give you one where the fellow does the handing over and then just carries on living among them, respected, no punishment, no exile, no rock." He shrugged. "Maybe there is one. I'm not a scholar. Everyone who told me I was, I've outlived."

"That's the honest answer," I said.

"It's the only kind I've got left. Cheaper."

---

Here's what I did with that, and here's where I have to hold my own line.

That evening I sat at the kitchen table with the list and six verified entries and a notepad, and I wrote out, in two columns, what the six edits actually did.

*we have not yet reconstructed* → *doubtless simpler than they appear*
*transmitted to the early* → *arisen among the first*
*evidently older than the astronomers who recorded it* → *first correctly described by* [a named man]
*we cannot at present account for* → *is likely to reflect local development*
*brought from elsewhere* → *emerged in situ*
*a tradition of instruction* → *a tradition*

Every one of them the same move. Every one of them takes out a giver and leaves the gift.

Six. From four decades and three languages and publishers who never met.

And now the mythology on top of it, doing the same thing three thousand years earlier: the giver gets punished, exiled, chained, re-labelled as a devil.

I sat with that for a long time and then I wrote down the objection, because that's the job, and if I don't write down the objection I'm just a man with a wall of string.

*Objection: I have been looking for edits that fit this shape. I found them. Of course I did. Somewhere out there is a book where the second impression put a giver IN, and I have never once gone looking for it, because it wouldn't be interesting.*

So I went looking for it.

Three weeks, which Ana noticed, because three weeks is a length of time.

"What is it now?"

"I'm trying to find one that goes the other way round."

She had the fridge door open and she stopped with it open, which she does, and which costs us a fortune.

"So you're trying to prove yourself wrong."

"That's the job."

"Mm." She got the milk out and shut the door with her hip. "I like this one. Do more of this one."

And she went through to the other room, and I sat there at the table feeling absurdly pleased with myself, and it did not occur to me for about another year that the version of me she liked was simply the version that could still be talked to.

I'm going to give you the result flat, because I promised myself I'd report the negative ones with the same face as the positive ones.

I found no counter-examples.

And here is the important sentence, the one I'd fight anybody about: **that is much weaker evidence than it feels like.** Not finding something is not finding something. I could not prove I'd looked in the right places. I could not prove I'd have recognised one if I'd tripped over it. A man who searches for three weeks and finds nothing has established one fact, and it is a fact about the man.

But six is six. And the direction is the direction.

I wrote it out properly and put the date on it:

*Six verified. All six travel the same way: from someone did this, toward it just happened. I do not know who is doing it or when they started. I know which way they push.*

---

There's a last bit of that Sunday and it's why I couldn't sleep.

I was packing the books he'd sold me — three, four euros, he barely counts — and I asked him the throwaway question, the one you ask with your back turned.

"Where do you get all this? The gods, the reed wall. You haven't read all that."

"Course I haven't read it." He was folding the tarpaulin. "I've listened to it. Forty years of men standing where you're standing, telling me things. You lot all talk. You think you're browsing."

Then he said, without any change in his voice at all, still folding:

"Your professor used to ask me this. Same thing. Years back. Who gets punished."

I stopped with a book half in the bag.

"He asked you that?"

"Sat on that crate, where I was sitting." He tied the string. "Different question at the end, though."

"What was the question at the end?"

Beard Man picked up his box and looked at me with an expression I've never been able to name, out of a face I have been looking at for a decade and could not describe to a police artist.

"He wanted to know," he said, "if anyone ever came asking about *him*."
