# Down House, Kent
### January 1860

The house is in Kent, sixteen miles from London and further than that in every way that matters, which is why he bought it. There is a strip of gravel path he walks in a loop when he is thinking, out past the kitchen garden and round through the trees, and on a good day he does five circuits and on a bad day he does not go out.

That winter there are a great many bad days.

He is fifty. He has been ill for twenty years with something nobody has ever named — vomiting, boils, a shaking of the hands, weeks in which he can work for two hours and no more. He has tried the water cure. He has kept a diary of his own symptoms with the same patience he gives to barnacles, which is a great deal of patience, and it has told him nothing.

On the twenty-fourth of November, six weeks before, a publisher in Albemarle Street put twelve hundred and fifty copies of his book into the world and the entire printing was taken by the trade in a day. He was not in London for it. He was at a hydropathic establishment in Yorkshire, being wrapped in wet sheets.

Now they want a second edition, and he has the sheets in front of him, and he is going to change something.

---

The book is called *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection*. It argues, at very great length and with an almost apologetic courtesy, that species were not made. That they came to be, slowly, by a process with no author and no intention in it, out of variation and death.

He knows exactly what he has done. He wrote to a friend, before publication, that it was like confessing a murder.

The response has arrived in the post, sack by sack, for six weeks. Some of it is glorious. Some of it is from men he loves. And some of it is from Adam Sedgwick, who taught him geology in Wales when he was twenty-two and took him up hillsides and showed him how to read a landscape, and who has now written to tell him that he has read parts of the book with absolute sorrow, and that he believes the doctrine to be false and mischievous, and that it is a moral question as much as a scientific one.

His wife is a believing woman. She has told him, in writing, years earlier, that she is afraid for him — not for his reputation, for his soul; she is afraid that they will not meet again afterwards, and she has written it down and given it to him and he has kept the letter and written on it, at the bottom, that he has kissed and cried over it.

They buried a daughter nine years ago. She was ten. He wrote a private memorial of her a week after and then never, in any surviving document, discussed the religious question in those terms again.

We do not know what he was thinking that January. Nobody does. There is no letter that says *I am about to do this and here is why.* What survives is the change itself, and what he said about it afterwards, and those two things are all anyone is entitled to.

---

The last sentence of the book is the most famous sentence he ever wrote. It is about a tangled bank, birds singing, insects flitting, worms crawling through the damp earth, and how these elaborate forms, so different from each other and so dependent on each other, have all been produced by laws acting around us.

In the first edition it ends like this:

> *There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one; and that, whilst this planet has gone cycling on according to the fixed law of gravity, from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.*

*Having been originally breathed.* Passive. No agent. A sentence with a hole in the middle of it exactly where a hand would go, and the hole is not an accident; he is the most careful writer of English prose in nineteenth-century science and he does not leave holes by accident.

For the second edition, published on the seventh of January, eighteen hundred and sixty, three thousand copies, he puts a hand in it.

> *…having been originally breathed **by the Creator** into a few forms or into one…*

Three words. The sentence is otherwise untouched. The tangled bank stays, the worms stay, the grandeur stays, the fixed law of gravity stays. Length barely changes. A reader who knew the first edition and skimmed the second would very likely not notice, and almost nobody in the world knew the first edition, because it had been on sale for six weeks.

---

He did not announce it. There is no note in the second edition drawing attention to the alteration. There was no need; it was 1860, and a second edition was allowed to differ from a first, and everybody understood that books breathed.

He kept altering it. Six editions in thirteen years, and by the sixth there is scarcely a page of the first that survives untouched — a scholar counted them all a century later and found that the majority of the sentences in the book had been changed, added, or removed. Some of it is improvement. Some of it is a man answering his critics in the text instead of in the press.

And some of it is retreat. In the later editions he pulls back on how long the earth has had, because the greatest physicist alive has calculated that the sun cannot be that old, and Darwin has no way to argue with the physics and no idea that the physics is wrong. He calls that calculation an odious spectre. He gives ground anyway, in print, in the book, quietly, and the ground he gives is time, which is the one thing his whole argument needs.

The physicist turns out to have been mistaken. Nobody knew about radioactivity yet. The retreat was unnecessary, and by then it was in the text, and the text is what the world reads.

---

Three years after he added the three words, he wrote to his closest scientific friend about something else entirely, and near the end of the letter he said this:

> *I have long regretted that I truckled to public opinion & used Pentateuchal term of creation, by which I really meant 'appeared' by some wholly unknown process.*

*Truckled.* It is not a word we use now. It means to submit, to give way to somebody who has power over you, and it is not a word a man uses about himself kindly.

He never took the three words out. They are in the third edition, the fourth, the fifth, and the sixth. They are in most of the copies printed since. If you own a paperback of *On the Origin of Species*, go and look at the last page: unless somebody has gone to the trouble of giving you the first edition, the Creator is in there, breathing, and the man who put him there spent the rest of his life regretting it in private correspondence.

Nobody edited Charles Darwin.

He was under no threat. Nobody came to the house. There was no arrangement, no instruction, no visit from anybody at all. There was a sick man of fifty in a village in Kent, with a wife who was frightened for him and a dead child and a teacher who had written to say he was sorry, and there were three thousand copies to be printed, and the pressure was simply the ordinary pressure of being a person among people.

He did it himself. That is the whole point of it. That is why it is the first thing in this book that you can go and check.
