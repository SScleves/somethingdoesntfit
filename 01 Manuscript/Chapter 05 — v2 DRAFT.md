# Chapter Five

The next three weeks are easier to tell as days than as arguments, so that's how I'll do it. I had arguments. They weren't the part that moved.

---

**Saturday.** I drove to Antwerp to look at printing plates.

That sentence is going to sound like a man following a lead. It wasn't. It was a man buying himself a Saturday of feeling like he was doing something. There's a museum there in an old printing house, the kind of place where they keep the presses and the type and the plates behind glass and a very patient woman explains the trade to schoolchildren. I stood in front of the cases for two hours. I read every card. I asked the patient woman three questions and she answered all of them properly, without the faintest idea why anyone would want to know, which is the best kind of source there is.

And what I learned was: yes. Everything I'd guessed was right. You can reopen a plate. You can lift out a few words and set new ones in and lock it back up and print, and if you're any good, the page will show a hairline nothing that a hundred years and a bad scan will finish hiding for you. It happens. It has always happened. It's a trade, not a miracle.

I got back in the car at four in the afternoon feeling like I'd won something, and got about as far as the ring road before the feeling turned over and showed its other side.

Because *possible* isn't *evidence*. I'd driven a hundred and forty kilometres to establish that a thing which had obviously happened could happen. That's not investigation. That's a man collecting permission slips.

I want to be accurate about the shape of that day, because I did it again later, several times, and I'd rather you see the move early: when I can't move the case forward, I go and learn something adjacent, and the learning feels exactly like progress. It isn't. It's the difference between reading about a fire and going to the fire.

---

**Sunday.** Theory one, properly stated for the first time.

Here's what it took me two weeks to say cleanly, and I only got there by having to explain it to nobody, out loud, in a kitchen: *this is an ordinary event in the history of printing that I don't happen to know about.*

Not "the author revised it." The author was two years in the hills of eastern Turkey by then and nobody ever found him, so unless he was correcting proofs from a considerably better place than I've been promised, that door is shut.

Not "you misread the printing statement." I've now read that statement more times than the man who set it.

Just: there is some perfectly dull mechanism — house practice, a legal thing, a rights thing, a war thing, some arrangement between a publisher and a printer that everyone in 1931 understood and nobody has needed to explain since — under which you go into a plate, change a clause, leave the typos, don't announce it, and keep calling it the first edition.

I could not find that mechanism. That is not the same as it not existing, and I said so to myself every day, in those words, like a man taking a pill.

---

**Wednesday.** The dealer.

I'd written weeks earlier to a bookseller three countries away, the sort who lists things with words like *gatherings* and *cancellans* and photographs the gutter. The kind of email where you're careful to seem less interested than you are.

He answered on a Wednesday, eleven days later, and I read it standing in the hall with my coat still on.

It was a good reply. It was also, and I mean this without complaint, almost entirely useless. He agreed the impressions were what they said they were. He agreed the plate damage indicated no recasting. He said, and I'm quoting because I've read it enough times to have it, *the practice you describe is not unknown, though I would want to see the leaf.* Then he explained, at some length and with obvious pleasure, three ways a man in my position usually turns out to be wrong.

Not one of the three applied.

He closed by asking, mildly, what the variant was.

I didn't tell him. I typed a paragraph explaining that it was a small matter of phrasing in a book of no importance, deleted it, and wrote four words instead: *Still checking. Thank you.*

I stood there afterwards with my coat still on and thought about the fact that I had now lied, twice, quickly, on instinct, to two different people, in order to protect a sentence about flood stories that I myself had called the folklore equivalent of a comma.

Nobody hides something boring.

---

**Thursday.** The excitement, and what I did about it.

I caught myself, halfway through the week, taking the stairs two at a time. Not for any reason. There was no news. There was a fresh idea about ecclesiastical printing licences that had occurred to me on the toilet, and I was hurrying to go be wrong about it.

I stopped on the landing and stood there and had a very unpleasant thirty seconds with myself.

Because I know what excitement does. I've spent twenty years watching people be certain. They're never certain about the dull answer. They're certain about the interesting one, and they get certain about it early, and everything they look at afterwards is quietly recruited. The one thing I've ever been able to sell, professionally, is that I don't do that: I go and check the boring corner first, because the boring corner is where it always is, and it never gets applause.

And here I was, taking the stairs two at a time.

So I wrote a line at the top of the page — the real page, the one with the two photographs on it — and left it there where I'd have to read it every time I opened the file.

*You want this to be true. Weigh accordingly.*

It didn't help. But I want it on the record that I knew.

---

**Friday.** The thing I'm not going to tell you about.

I'm going to be straight with you once, here, and then not raise it again for a long time.

At about four in the morning on that Friday — after I'd read for the second time that the man who wrote the book had walked out of a town in southeastern Turkey in 1929 and never been seen again — I got up, went downstairs, and spent forty minutes building something.

It is not clever. It is the most ordinary thing in the world; it is, more or less, what I do for a living, turned around and pointed at me. It took forty minutes because I had to look up two things I should have known.

I am not going to describe it. Not out of drama — because the whole value of the thing is that nobody knows it's there, and I am not sure yet who is going to read this.

What I will tell you is that when I finished, I sat in the dark kitchen with the laptop shut and felt, for about ten seconds, absolutely ridiculous. A man in his forties in a dressing gown, in a house in a quiet country, with two sleeping dogs and a wife upstairs and a mortgage and a good job, taking precautions against a publishing decision made in 1931.

The most ridiculous act of my adult life, easily. I have never regretted it.

Then I went back to bed and lay there until it got light.

---

**Saturday.** The dishes.

Ana washed; no — I washed, she dried, that's the treaty, that's always been the treaty. We were most of the way through the pans when she said it, not looking up.

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The submarine thing. You're right here and you're eight hundred metres down."

"I'm here."

"Your hands are here."

I didn't say anything. That's new for me; normally there's a line ready. She handed the pan back because I'd missed a corner and I did it again and gave it back and she dried it, and neither of us said anything for a while, and the not-saying went on long enough to become a thing in the room with us.

"Is it the professor's books?"

"There's a printing thing that doesn't add up. It's nerdy. It's boring. It's a me problem."

Every word of that was true. It was also a lie, assembled entirely out of true words, which is the only kind I've ever known how to tell her.

She put the pan on the rack. She still hadn't looked at me.

"Don't disappear into it," she said. "She's been gone six weeks. I'd like my husband on land for a while."

"I'm on land."

Third one that week. I meant it as a promise. I've noticed since that I only ever say things like that in the present tense, which is a coward's tense — it commits you to nothing after the sentence ends.

She went up. I stood in the kitchen with the water going cold.

---

For the record, so it's said once and I never have to explain myself again: I've been in and out of therapy my whole adult life, and every one of them has eventually let me go with some version of *there's nothing here to treat.* I've been fired for being fine more times than most people have been hired.

So I asked myself their question, because I know it by heart. *Is this a grieving man who found a misprint and needs it to be bigger, because bigger is easier to hunt than a dead grandmother he wasn't even entitled to mourn?*

Partly. Yes. Some of the heat on this was grief looking for a job, and I'll sign for that.

But partly isn't wholly. Grief could explain why I cared about the word.

It could not put the word on the page.

---

There's one more thing about that fortnight and it takes one line.

When I was ten I read something in a book, and later the book didn't say it, and every adult in the room arranged their face for a child who had confidently invented something. I have photographed everything I care about ever since. That's all. That's the entire story, and I've never told it to anyone, including the professionals, because said out loud it's nothing.

It stopped being nothing on a kitchen floor at four in the morning, and I'd like to move on.

---

**Sunday.** Nothing happened.

I mean it. I want one of these in the book. I did the shopping, I lost eleven balls at the driving range, I watched most of a film with Ana and Mora between us like a draught excluder, and Buddy came and put his chin on my foot at some point and left it there, and I didn't think about 1931 for four consecutive hours.

The thin book was on the kitchen table when we went up, where it had been since I'd carried it home in a paper bag with eleven others.

Still shut. I'd taken it down and put it back three times by then.

I turned off the light and left it there.
