# The Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem
### 1978, and then 1991

There is a room in Jerusalem that people called the Scrollery, and for a long time the most important thing about it was who could not go in.

It's in the Rockefeller — the old Palestine Archaeological Museum, limestone, an octagonal tower, built with American money in the thirties, on the seam. Long tables. Good north light. And on the tables, under glass, laid out and lined up and slowly darkening, the fragments.

There were something like fifteen thousand of them from one cave alone. Cave 4, found in 1952 by Bedouin who got there first, as they usually did. Not scrolls, most of them; scraps. Some the size of a hand and some the size of a stamp, and the work of matching them was the work of a lifetime, and everybody understood that, and nobody has ever seriously argued otherwise.

Eight men were given them.

That's not a conspiratorial way of putting it; it's the arrangement. An international editorial team, formed in the early fifties under the direction of Roland de Vaux, a Dominican priest and archaeologist, working under the Jordanian antiquities authority in what was then East Jerusalem. Eight scholars, each allocated a body of material, each responsible for publishing it. No Israeli scholars, for the plain political reason of where the border ran. They were, by all accounts, serious men. Several of them were brilliant.

And the material was theirs. Not owned — *assigned.* Which in that world is the same thing, because the convention was that you did not publish another scholar's assignment, and you did not see it either, and the assignments passed down to students and heirs when men died.

So: no photographs to outsiders. No transcripts. No access. For a scholar not on the team, the situation was that the most significant manuscript find in the history of the Hebrew Bible existed, was known to exist, was catalogued, and could not be read.

For forty years.

---

Imagine a woman standing in the corridor in 1978. She is thirty-four. She has come from a university in the north of England on a grant that cost her department more than it could afford, and she has an appointment which turns out, when she arrives, not to be an appointment.

Everyone she meets is perfectly civil. That's the thing she will remember, twenty years later, when it's all over and she's telling it at a conference dinner: nobody was rude to her. A man explains the conservation position, and he is not lying; the fragments were in a bad state, and light and handling were genuinely destroying them, and any conservator alive would have said the same. Another man explains, kindly, that the material she wants is Father So-and-so's, and Father So-and-so is in Europe, and one really can't.

She asks whether there are photographs.

There are photographs. There are excellent photographs; they had been taken from the beginning, on plates, because everybody involved understood that the physical objects would not last and the images would.

She asks whether she might see the photographs.

And the answer to that is also no, and the reason for the no is not conservation, because a photograph cannot be damaged by being looked at. The reason is the convention. The photographs belong to the edition, and the edition belongs to the men.

She goes back to England. She writes a paper about something else. She is not a martyr; nothing happens to her at all. She simply spends the rest of her working life unable to check something, and she gets used to it, the way everyone did.

---

In 1977 an Oxford scholar named Geza Vermes wrote, in print, that the situation was becoming *the academic scandal par excellence of the twentieth century.*

Nothing happened.

It was said again through the eighties, louder, largely by an American lawyer-turned-editor called Hershel Shanks in a popular magazine, and there were letters, and there were conference rows, and it became one of those things a discipline knows about itself and does not fix. The team's chief editor gave a newspaper interview in 1990 so unpleasant that he was removed from the post; the new editor-in-chief, Emanuel Tov, expanded the team enormously and started publishing at speed. It was already too late to be the story.

Because the thing that broke it was not an argument.

---

There were two blows and they landed a fortnight apart in September 1991.

The first: two scholars at Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati — Ben Zion Wacholder and his graduate student Martin Abegg — took a concordance. Not the texts; the *index* to the texts, a card concordance made by the team decades earlier for their own use and circulated to a handful of libraries. It listed every word in the unpublished material, with its context, and its location.

Abegg fed it into a computer and ran it backwards.

You can reconstruct a text from a good enough index. Word by word, line by line, the thing reassembles. It's tedious and it's imperfect and it is completely legal, and it took a graduate student with a PC about four months, and in September 1991 they published the results. The unpublished material was suddenly, partially, publicly readable, and it had been reconstructed out of a finding aid the team had made themselves.

The second blow was smaller and it was the one that ended it.

On the twenty-second of September 1991, the Huntington Library in San Marino, California — a research library in a garden famous for its azaleas — announced that its complete photographic set of the scrolls was open. To anyone. No permission, no assignment, no letter of introduction.

They had the images because in the fifties and sixties, sensibly, security copies had been made and deposited in a few places around the world in case something happened to Jerusalem. A backup. That is the entire mechanism of this story: somebody, decades earlier, worrying about fire, had put a copy somewhere else.

The library's director, William Moffett, took the decision and defended it publicly. The Israel Antiquities Authority threatened legal action. There was about five weeks of noise.

By the end of October the restrictions were gone, and they have never come back, and today you can look at the photographs on a screen for nothing, in your kitchen, at one in the morning.

---

Among the material that spent those forty years on those tables were fragments in Aramaic of the Book of Enoch — the book about the ones who came down and *taught*, who gave metalwork and cosmetics and the reading of the sky, and were bound in the valleys of the earth for it — in copies far older than any complete version the world had.

Nobody sat in a room in Jerusalem deciding that the world should not read that. That is not what happened and it would be a poorer story if it were.

What happened is that eight serious men were given something, and the convention said it was theirs, and the conservation argument was true, and the political arrangement was real, and every individual no was reasonable.

And it held for forty years, and it was broken in a fortnight by an index somebody had made for their own convenience and a photograph somebody had filed a long way away in case of fire.

*The Huntington's announcement was reported in the New York Times on 22 September 1991. The photographs are online now. So is the concordance.*
