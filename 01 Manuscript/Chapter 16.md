# Chapter Sixteen

I'd had that list for two years before I read it properly, and when I finally did it took an afternoon, and I did it by not reading a single word of it.

This is the only genuinely professional thing I do, so I'd like a moment with it.

At work, when somebody hands me a system and says *find the problem*, the amateur move is to read the logs. There are ten million lines of logs. You will find, in ten million lines, whatever you have decided to look for, and you will find it in about an hour, and you will be wrong.

What I do instead is look at how the thing is *kept*. Which files are backed up and which aren't. What somebody bothered to write a script for. Where the copies are. What's been left running that nobody uses. A system's filing tells you what the people who built it were afraid of losing, and it tells you before you open anything, and it doesn't lie, because nobody edits their filing to fool you. They don't think of it as speech.

So one Saturday in March I put the list on the table and covered the two right-hand columns — the *before* and the *after*, the actual content, the part I'd spent two years on — with a strip of card.

And I looked at what was left.

---

Here's what was left.

**Ink.** Six inks, not two. The grandfather's brown-black for entries one to fourteen. Then the professor's blue, which is four different blues over what must be decades, plus a black patch in the middle (entries twenty-three to twenty-seven, all in one sitting, all in black, by a man who had presumably lost his pen). And then the shaky ballpoint at the bottom, on its own, for one word.

**Pressure.** You can feel entries one to fourteen from the back of the page. The old man wrote like he was engraving. The professor's early entries are light. His late ones are heavy again.

**Spacing.** Entries one to fourteen are evenly spaced, ruled by eye, consistent, the work of a man who sat down to make a fair copy of something and did it in a few sittings. The professor's are not. Some are crammed. Some have half a page of air after them.

**The gaps.** And this is the one. The professor's entries come in **clusters** — four or five in the same ink, the same pressure, obviously the same period — and then a change of ink and a change of hand-steadiness, and then another cluster.

Six clusters in about thirty-five years. Between two of them, if I read the deterioration of that handwriting correctly, a gap that has to be six or seven years long.

I sat back and thought about Els Maasen's kitchen. *He stopped. Completely. For years.*

The gap is in the ink. The man's abandonment of his life's work is physically present in the object, and you can see it without reading a word, and I had been staring straight through it for two years because I was busy reading the interesting part.

---

The other thing the card showed me was the marginal notes, which I'd always treated as decoration.

Twelve entries have them. Twenty-nine have nothing.

I made a list of the twelve and looked at what the notes actually are, and they sort into exactly three kinds:

A **year** (four of them). A **place** (two — one of them *the hill*). And a **person** (five). Of the five people, three are named. Two are described: *poor woman* and *the son, if he answers*.

Thirty entries with no note are edits he verified and filed.

Twelve entries with a note are edits where he knew, or suspected, **who had been standing next to it when it happened.**

That's not a bibliography. That's a witness list, hidden inside a bibliography, by a man who had decided not to write down anything that looked like an accusation.

---

Entry seventeen's person is Ilse Brandt-Ostrander, and she is the reason I understood the professor before I ever understood the Keepers.

She was a real surveyor with a real career, and she is on the title page of the 1963 *Highlands of Anatolia* and on the title page of the 1971 reprint, and the 1971 reprint carries a two-line preface, over her name, thanking colleagues for corrections. She lived until 2011. Her papers went to a small institute that took eight weeks to answer my email and then answered it properly, because — I have thought about this a lot — the people who look after dead academics' boxes are almost always delighted that somebody has asked.

Her correspondence is nineteen folders of the dullest material on earth. Permits. Travel claims. A four-year argument about a jeep.

And one letter, 1974, to a colleague, unsent — it's in the file with a note in her own hand saying *not posted* — of which the only line that matters is:

*You ask why I agreed to the revision. I was shown what would happen to the department.*

That's it. That's all of it. No detail, no name, no threat described. *Shown.* Not told, not threatened — shown.

She signed the correction, kept the letter she didn't send, and lived another thirty-seven years.

---

Now here is the question I could not leave alone, and it turned out to be the important one.

She was alive until 2011. Her address was in a professional directory. The professor knew her name — it's in his own margin. He was in the same profession, on the same continent, with a car.

**He never went to see her.**

I checked as hard as anyone can check that sort of thing. There's no correspondence from him in her papers, and the institute searched properly, twice, because by that point the archivist was more interested than I was.

I sat with that for a fortnight and I could only make it come out two ways, and I want to give you both because I am still not certain and it matters enormously.

**One:** he was frightened, and going to see a witness in person was the line he wouldn't cross.

**Two** — and this is the one I believe, and I believe it because of a single sentence his daughter gave me across a kitchen table: *I was ashamed of what it had cost you.*

He had gone to see somebody, once. And it had cost them.

I have no proof of that. I have a gap of six or seven years in the ink, a man who stopped completely and gardened and went to his grandson's graduation, and a daughter who says the shame came before the fear.

You do not stop for six years because you got frightened. Fear doesn't work like that; fear makes you careful, and careful men keep going quietly, which is what he'd been doing for twenty years already.

You stop for six years because of something you did.

And if I'm right, then the professor's method — paper only, no people, never knock on a door — was not caution. It was a rule he made afterwards, the way you make rules afterwards, and he kept it for the rest of his life, and it is the reason he died with a list nobody could act on.

I want that noted here, at the exact point I noticed it, because it is going to be relevant to what I did later, and I would like it on record that I saw the trap clearly, in March, two years before I walked into it deliberately.

---

**Entry forty-one.**

I've been calling it forty-one all along and I should say that it isn't numbered. The professor numbered everything up to forty. The last one has no number, and it's not in the ruled columns, and it doesn't have a source or a page or two printings or a before and an after.

It has:

A set of coordinates. Six decimal places, which is a man being precise to about a hundred millimetres, which is either meaningful or the ordinary vanity of copying down what a screen gives you.

A date, circled twice.

And, in the shaky ink, underneath, added later, on its own:

*Ask.*

The coordinates are in southeastern Turkey. I'm not going to give you the numbers. They are about forty kilometres from a town called Şanlıurfa, which the older maps call Urfa, which is where H. M. Tresidder was last seen in 1929 walking east, and which is nine kilometres from a hill that somebody deliberately filled in eleven thousand years ago.

The date is the fourteenth of October.

Hendrik Maasen died on the twenty-second of September.

He circled a date three weeks after his own death, and he did not live to keep it, and I have never been able to find out what was supposed to happen on it, and by the time I could have asked anybody, I was the only person left who knew the question.

---

That night I did the last thing, and it's the small one, and it's the one that broke the whole case open ten months later.

I took the forty entries and I put them in a spreadsheet, and I ignored the sources and the pages and the befores and the afters, and I typed in only the years — the year of impression A and the year of impression B.

Forty rows. Two columns of numbers.

Then I sorted by the second column, which is the year the change happened, and I looked at the list of years.

I want to be honest about this moment because it's not dramatic and I nearly missed it. I looked at forty numbers on a screen late at night, and I thought *huh*, and I saved the file, and I went to bed.

They're not spread out. They come in bunches, with long quiet stretches between them, and the quiet stretches are getting shorter.

I didn't do anything with that for ten months.

I just stopped being able to look at the list the way I used to, because I'd finally understood what I was holding.

For two years I had been reading a list of forty things that were changed.

What I actually had was a list of forty **dates** on which somebody, somewhere, decided it was time.
