# Chapter Fourteen

I wrote to her. Paper, an actual letter, because an email from a stranger about your dead father is a thing you delete and a letter is a thing you have to put down somewhere.

I told her the truth in five sentences: I bought some of her father's books at a market, one of them contains a notebook in his handwriting that I don't think was meant to be sold, I have spent a year reading it, I would like to give it back or at least tell her it exists, and I am not press and I am not selling anything.

She rang me nine days later at ten past eight in the evening while I was loading the dishwasher.

"You're the book man."

"I'm — yes."

"Come Thursday," Els Maasen said. "Not Friday, Friday's deliveries. And don't bring flowers, I've got a shop full of the bloody things."

---

Deventer is an hour and a bit. The shop is fabric, upholstery, curtains, that world, and it smells like a fabric shop, which is a smell I hadn't had since my mother, and it took the top off me before I'd even said hello.

She's mid-fifties. Reading glasses pushed up. The kind of person who talks to you while continuing to do a task, which I like enormously and which is also, I found out over the next hour, a way of not having to look at somebody.

We went up to the flat above and she made tea I didn't want and put it in front of me and didn't make one for herself.

"So," she said. "Show me."

I put the list on her kitchen table.

She looked at it for a long time. She turned four or five pages. Her hand went to her mouth once and came down again.

"That's him," she said. "That's his writing when he's being careful. He had a horrible scrawl normally. This is his — this is what he did for other people."

"There's an older hand as well. The first fourteen entries."

"That'll be my grandfather." She said it without any surprise at all, which surprised me. "He was the same. That's the thing you're going to want to understand and I'm not sure I can help you: it wasn't one man with an obsession. It was two, in a relay."

---

I'd better do the death now, because I know what you're expecting and I want to disappoint you at once, exactly as I was disappointed.

"He died in his chair," she said. "The blue one. About four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, and Mrs Vermeer next door found him at seven because he hadn't put the bin out and he always put the bin out."

"Was there —"

"There was nothing." She looked at me properly for the first time. "I know why you're here. You want it to be something. A doctor came, and then the other doctor came because that's the law when there's no one there, and there was a post-mortem because I asked for one, because I was exactly where you are now. His heart. Sixty-nine, high blood pressure for fifteen years, wouldn't take the tablets consistently, drank a bottle of wine most evenings and told me it was two glasses." She shrugged with her whole body. "He died like a Dutch man of sixty-nine dies. I'm sorry. I know that's not the story."

"It isn't a disappointment," I said, and I meant it. "It's a data point. It's a good one."

"That's a horrible way to talk about my father."

"Yes," I said. "It is. I'm sorry."

And she laughed — one short unwilling noise — and said, "No, you're not, but at least you didn't pretend," and after that she was different with me, and I've thought since that being caught being myself did me more good in that kitchen than an hour of the persona would have.

---

Here's the part I went home with.

"Was he frightened?" I asked.

And Els Maasen said: "No. He was ashamed."

I put my cup down.

"Say more about that."

"He stopped." She turned her hand over. "In about — 2009? 2010. He stopped, completely, for years. He was ill for a bit, and then he retired, and something happened at the university that he never told me about and I've stopped wanting to know. And after that it was gardening. Cycling. He came to things. He was at my son's graduation and he was *there*, he was funny, he was —" she stopped for a second. "Those were the best six years of having him as a father, and I got them at fifty-one."

"And then?"

"And then about six months before he died, he started again."

"Do you know why?"

"No." She said it hard. "I asked. I asked more than once. And what he said was —"

She got up and went to the window, because it turns out I'm not the only one who can't say a thing while being looked at.

"He said: *I let it go because I was tired and I was ashamed of what it had cost you. But I've been checking, and it's still happening, and it's faster.*"

I sat in that kitchen with my hands around a cup of tea I hadn't touched and had the closest thing to a physical shock I've ever had from a sentence.

Because I hadn't told her about the dates. I hadn't told anybody about the dates. I'd noticed, that autumn, in a spreadsheet, on my own, that the entries in that list cluster — that they come in bursts and the bursts are getting closer together — and I had not said it to a living soul, and here was a dead man saying it to his daughter in her kitchen six months before his heart stopped.

*It's still happening, and it's faster.*

---

Two more things from that flat.

The first: I asked whether he'd left papers. Notes, files, drafts, anything.

"He burned them," she said.

"He —"

"In the garden. That last winter." She said it flatly, and I could tell she'd had to make peace with it and hadn't quite. "In the fire basket, over about three weeks. I came round and there he was in a coat and a scarf feeding paper into it, very calm, very cheerful, offering me a coffee. Boxes of it. Forty years."

"Did he say why?"

"He said he was tidying up. He said —" she frowned, getting it right. "He said, *I've made a clean copy of the only part that matters, and the rest is just me being right in private, and nobody needs that.*"

I said, "The rest is just me being right in private."

"That's what he said."

And there it was. The fair copy. My wife had spotted it in nine seconds fourteen months earlier — *he's crossed nothing out* — and here was the confirmation from a woman who watched him do it: he had a working archive of forty years, and he made one clean, patient, checkable copy of it, and then he destroyed everything else and left the copy in a house that he knew, at sixty-nine, with a bad heart, would be cleared by his daughter.

He didn't hide it. He didn't post it to a newspaper. He copied out the evidence in his neatest handwriting and put it where it would go into the world in the most random possible way, and then he waited, awake, at two in the morning, for whatever came.

The second thing was at the door.

"Do you want it back?" I asked. "The list. It's yours."

She looked at the book in my hands for a long moment.

"No," she said.

"Els —"

"No." She opened the door. "I've watched this thing eat two men in my family. I'm not having it in the house." Then, and this is the sentence I drove home with: "You seem all right. You've got a wife?"

"Yes."

"Does she know what you've got?"

---

The decoy results were waiting for me when I got back.

I want to lay them out the way I laid them out that night, because it's the only time in this whole business where I had a result that came out exactly as designed, and I have never been more frightened by anything in my life.

**Post three — the crank version, capital letters, the wrong date.** Thirty-one replies in nine days. Twenty-two of them mockery, four of them agreeing with me enthusiastically for reasons that had nothing to do with anything, three of them arguing with each other about something else entirely, one of them a man selling a book. Nobody corrected the deliberately wrong date. Nobody looked anything up. It had done its job, which was to be exactly what it looked like.

**Post two — the plausible false one.** The invented edit, in the correct grammar, with photographs of a page where nothing had happened.

Nothing. In nine days, two views and no replies. Which was the result I wanted least, because it's the control, and a control that does nothing tells you the room is empty.

**Post one — the true one. The dull one. Entry three, page 44, two printings, no theory, no adjectives.**

Two replies.

The first was from a man in Norway who collects the same series of paperbacks and wanted to tell me about the dust jackets.

The second was posted at 11:04 in the morning on a Wednesday, four days after mine, by an account with a real name on it, an institutional affiliation, a fifteen-year posting history and about nine hundred contributions, most of them technical, most of them helpful, several of them genuinely funny. A real person with a real life, or the most patient thing I have ever seen.

It was polite. It was expert. It was three paragraphs long.

It agreed with me.

And then it did the thing.

It said — and I am paraphrasing only slightly, because I have that post saved in four places — that I was quite right that the wording had been revised between printings, that this was routine in popular titles of that period, and that in fact I had slightly *understated* the case, because the original phrasing had always been considered unfortunate; and it offered, helpfully, a better way to describe what had happened.

*It is more accurate to say that the later printing corrects an overstatement in the first.*

I sat and looked at that sentence for about an hour.

Because here's what it is. Nothing in it is false. Every clause is defensible. A reasonable expert could write it in good faith and I would put money on the fact that this one did.

And what it does is take an event with a hand in it — *somebody went into this book and changed what it claimed* — and hand you back a sentence in which the book corrects itself. Nobody did anything. It was just tidied. The first version was unfortunate.

Same move. Same direction. Not in a 1931 plate. In a forum post, four days later, in the present tense, with a real name on it, about *my* finding.

---

Here's what I could honestly conclude, and I made myself write both halves.

**What it might be:** a knowledgeable enthusiast with an opinion, who read a post in his specialist area and answered it, as people do, thousands of times a day, and whose phrasing I am reading far too much into because I have spent a year training myself to see exactly this shape. This is the likeliest explanation. I wrote it first, and I underlined it.

**What it might also be:** that of three posts — one loud and true, one quiet and false, one quiet and true — the only one that anybody engaged with on its merits was the quiet true one; that the engagement arrived within four days; and that its entire content was a correction of the *framing*, from *someone did this* to *it just happened*, offered generously, by someone with more standing than me.

I did not know. I still don't, not about that specific man, and I never will.

But I had run an experiment with the results written down in advance, and one of the four lines I'd written had come true, and it was the fourth one.

*Something is reading for content, not for volume.*

---

And then, at twenty past nine the next morning, at my desk, pretending to read a ticket, I got a message that was not part of the experiment at all.

Not on the thread. A private message, from an account I had never seen, sixteen words long.

> **Whoever "corrected" you used a phrase my supervisor showed me on a photocopy in 2009. Who are you?**

I read it about forty times.

Here's what I did, and I'd defend it: nothing, for two days. Because the single most likely explanation for a stranger appearing at the exact moment you need one is that somebody sent them — and I had been politely corrected in public four days earlier by an expert with a real name and a fifteen-year history, and it had cost me a night's sleep and a permanent change in the way I read kindness.

So I wrote back one line with a hook in it, and the hook was a mistake I made on purpose.

> **Which photocopy? The one with the two columns, deprecated on the left?**

If she was theirs, she'd say yes. There is no photocopy with two columns; I invented it on the spot. Everybody says yes to a detail. Saying yes is how you keep somebody talking.

She wrote back in under a minute.

> **No. Nothing like that. It was a single page from a house style sheet, no columns, and I only saw it once, and I have thought about it for sixteen years. If you are going to test me please do it better than that, it's insulting.**

Then, ninety seconds later:

> **Sorry. That was rude. You were right to test me. My name is Noor Haverkamp. Hendrik Maasen was my supervisor. He is dead and I would like to know why a stranger is posting his findings on a bibliography forum.**

I sat at my desk in an open-plan office with about forty people in it and did not move for a while.


---

Ana was in bed when I got in. Buddy came off the blanket and got as far as the kitchen door and then reconsidered the rest of it and lay down there instead, in the doorway, which was new.

I stood in the kitchen with my coat on for a while.

Then I did three things, in this order, and they are the three that mattered.

I made copies of the list. All forty-one pages, photographed properly, and then — I'm not going to say where.

I moved the folder off the laptop.

And when Ana asked me at breakfast how Deventer was, I said it was fine, that the daughter was nice, that there was nothing much in the notebook, and that I thought I'd probably taken it as far as it went.

Four sentences. Three of them lies, and the third one was the big one, and she believed me because in fifteen years I have never given her a reason not to.

I told myself I was protecting her.

I wrote that down too, that night, in the file, because even then some part of me knew it would need to be evidence later:

*Told A. it's finished. Reason given (to self): protecting her. Actual reason: unknown. Flag for review.*

Then I went and checked that the front door was locked.

I had lived in that house for twelve years and I had never once done that.
