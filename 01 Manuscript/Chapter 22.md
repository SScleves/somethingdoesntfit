# Chapter Twenty-Two

It took nine weeks to assemble and about four seconds to be sure, which is the wrong way round and I've never got used to it.

Nine weeks of doing nothing. That's the part nobody writes about. There was no moment where I sat bolt upright. There was a barbecue in May and a line about a steam engine, and then June, and then most of July, in which I played golf with a man I liked twice more, and had a beer with him after, and did not think about it, and then thought about it, and then made myself stop, because — and I said this to myself in these words, more than once, standing at a sink — *you are becoming the kind of man who suspects his friends.*

That was genuinely the argument. Not *he can't be*. I had by then a fairly cold view of what could be. The argument was: **you have one friend, and if you take this apart you will not have one, and you will have proved nothing either way.**

So I did what I have never done with anything else in my life. I left a thing unchecked, on purpose, for nine weeks, because I wanted it.

I want that in the book. Everything else I've told you is about a man who checks. This is the one time I didn't, and it's the only time it mattered.

---

The last Saturday in July, and it was one of about four properly warm evenings we get.

We'd played eighteen. He'd been terrible; I'd been worse; on the twelfth he'd hit a tree so squarely that the ball came back past us both at head height and he went down on one knee in the fairway with his arms out like a man receiving a medal. Ana drove over from her mother's to meet us afterwards at the pub near the course, the one with the terrace out the back and the heaters that nobody ever turns on until somebody complains.

She got there at half seven in the green dress and Dave stood up when she came out, which nobody does any more, and she noticed it and I saw her notice it.

She was in a good mood. She'd had a good week. She'd been talking to her mother about the grandmother's flat, which had taken eighteen months to become a thing she could talk about, and it had gone all right, and she was on her second glass and she was doing the toaster story.

"Two weekends," she said. "*Two.* Every wire in that house."

"He'd just bought it," Dave said, on my side, in the way you do at a table.

"He'd just bought it, thank you, that's what he says." She pointed at me with the glass. "I'm standing in the kitchen holding a toaster like I'm in a siege. And there's a man under the stairs with a torch in his mouth going *don't plug that in yet.*"

"Did you find anything?" Dave asked me.

"A mislabelled fuse."

"That's it," Ana said. "That's the whole treasure. One fuse, labelled wrong, in nineteen eighty-something. And he was so *happy*." She looked at me with an expression I had not had from her in about a year. "It's the happiest I've ever seen him. He came up out of that cellar like he'd won something."

Dave laughed. It was a good laugh, unhurried, and he turned his glass a quarter-turn on the beer mat and said:

"Careful man. Nothing wrong with that. Old houses go up, don't they. Like that professor's place out near Deventer."

---

I'd like to describe what happened in my body, because I had read about it and it was nothing like the descriptions.

My heart didn't race. It did one thing — a single hard knock, like somebody testing a door from the other side — and then everything got slow and extremely clear. The heaters. The beer mats. A gull on the roof of the shed. My wife's hand on my forearm, warm, with the ring on it.

There is no professor's place near Deventer.

Hendrik Maasen died in a blue chair in a house in a town four hours from here, of his heart, at sixty-nine, with the bin not put out. Nothing burned. There is no fire in this story anywhere.

What is near Deventer is a fabric shop with a flat above it where a woman called Els sells curtains and once made me tea I didn't drink.

I have never posted the word Deventer. It is not in a single message, on a single forum, under any account I have ever had. There are three people alive who know I went there: Els Maasen, who asked me not to write again; Noor, who I told in April; and my wife, who thinks I went to see a dealer about a book.

I took a drink. I made a long one look like a short one.

"Deventer?" I said. "I thought that was up near Zwolle."

---

The terrace went on being a terrace. Somebody inside dropped a glass and there was the small ironic cheer that always follows.

Dave turned his glass another quarter-turn.

"Zwolle," he said. "You might be right. I'm hopeless with those towns."

And there it was.

Because *you might be right* is what you say when you don't know. It is not what you say when you don't care. A man who has picked up a stray detail somewhere and is making conversation says *is it? I've never been.* A man who is being handed a wrong answer and has decided to take it says *you might be right.*

He'd corrected himself to my correction. He'd let me move it. That's not a slip; that's a decision, made in about a second, and the decision is the confession.

Ana said, "Who's the professor?"

"It's a book thing," I said.

"It's always a book thing," she said to Dave, comfortably, and reached for the bottle.

---

Now the other one, which I did not consciously register at the table and which came up out of me at three the following morning like something surfacing in a lake.

He had called me Jim since the second tee in February. Every time. Twenty-odd rounds of golf, three coffees, one barbecue, a hundred texts about tee times. Nobody else in my life has ever called me Jim — not my wife, not my mother, not one person at work in eleven years. It had become the thing I liked most about him, if I'm honest, because a private name is what you have with people you've known thirty years and I'd got one in a fortnight.

He did not call me Jim again after the word Deventer.

Not once. Not for the remaining eleven minutes.

"That'll be it," he said. "Zwolle. Well — it's a shame either way." He put the glass down and looked out at the car park, comfortable, a man letting a subject go. "You spend enough time round old buildings, you get to hear about the ones that don't make it. It's always the same thing — nobody's fault, nothing sinister. Old wiring. A man on his own who isn't as careful as he used to be." He turned back to Ana. "Sorry. That's morbid for a Saturday."

"It really is," Ana said.

"Occupational, I'm afraid. It's mostly what I do — surveys, valuations, what a thing's worth and what it'd cost if it went." He smiled at her. "You'd be amazed what people don't insure. Nice house, good street, a dog, all the photographs in one place — and then a boiler nobody's looked at since two thousand and nine."

"Right," said Ana, "and now I'm going to go and look at the boiler."

"Do," said Dave.

---

He didn't stay long after that. He'd said he wouldn't; there was a thing in the morning.

He put a note under his glass — he always paid in cash, which I had noticed, and had filed, twenty times, under *he's fifty-five* — and stood and put his jacket on, and he was completely, entirely, unimprovably normal. That's the part I can't get across. Nothing dropped. There was no cold flatness behind the eyes, no long look, none of the things you read.

He shook my hand.

"Good game today, James."

He kissed Ana on the cheek, which he'd done at the barbecue and which she let him do again, and said, "Get him to come out more, he's a hermit," and Ana said "you have *no idea*," and he raised a hand at the door and was gone.

---

I sat there.

Ana poured the last of the wine into her own glass and said, "Well, he was on form," and then, after a second, "That got a bit weird at the end, didn't it. The house thing."

"He's an insurance man."

"He's not an insurance man, he does something with assets, you told me." She was licking her thumb, getting a bit of label off the bottle. "Whatever. He's lovely. I'm glad about him."

And I sat on a terrace under a heater that nobody had turned on, next to the person I love most in the world, and felt — and I have gone over this and I'm not going to dress it up — *sad*.

Not frightened. That came later, in the car, and then for about eleven months.

Sad, in the specific way you're sad when a thing you enjoyed turns out not to have been the thing. I had not had a friend since I was thirty-three. I'd had one for five months. I had genuinely, stupidly, at forty-four years of age, been looking forward to Saturdays for a reason that wasn't the dog.

---

In the car she said, "Are you all right?"

"Yeah."

"You've gone quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"You've gone *quiet* quiet."

And here is where I lost my marriage for a year, on the N-road, at nine o'clock on a Saturday evening, in about ninety seconds, and I'd like to walk through it slowly because I've never been able to make anybody understand it, including me.

I could not tell her.

Not *would not*. Could not, in the sense that I opened my mouth and did the arithmetic and there was no first sentence available. Try it. Try starting it. *The man you spent the evening with knows I went to Deventer.* And she says, so what, you told him. *I didn't.* And she says, you must have. And she is right — that is the *correct* thing to say, that is what a reasonable person says, and I would say it — and then I am a man in a car explaining to his wife why an ordinary sentence from a friendly man means what I say it means, and the only way to get there is to start three years earlier, with a page number, at midnight, with a ruler on the table.

And she'd been happy tonight. That's the thing I keep coming back to. She'd had a good week and she'd talked to her mother about the flat and she'd worn the green dress.

So what I said was: "Long week."

"Okay," Ana said.

Two streetlights.

"Do you know what's funny," she said, and she wasn't being funny, "is that I nearly asked you tonight whether you'd got any further with the thing. The library thing. The board thing. And I decided not to, because we were having a nice time, and because you'd have told me it was nothing." She was looking out of the window. "That's what we do now. We both decide not to ask."

"It's nothing."

"Right," she said.

And then, because I'm me, and because in a crisis I reach for the tool nearest my hand, I said:

"Big Christmas coming up. You'll be wanting to know my hat size."

There was a silence of exactly the wrong length.

"Don't," Ana said quietly. "Not tonight."

I have never in my life heard a joke land so badly, and we both heard it, and neither of us said anything for the last eleven kilometres.

---

She went to her mother's on the Tuesday.

It wasn't a scene. There was no shouting; we have never once shouted. She packed on the Monday night while I was downstairs and I knew she was doing it and I stayed downstairs, and in the morning she said, "I'm going to Mum's for a bit. She needs help with the flat anyway." And I said okay. And she stood in the hall with the bag and said the only hard thing she said in the entire business:

"I've been on my own in this house for about two years, James. I'd rather be on my own somewhere it's true."

She took Mora. That was practical — her mother's got the garden and I'd be at work — and it was also not practical at all, and we both knew it.

I watched them go from the front door. Mora went into that car like it was a resurrection, because to her every single thing that happens is the best thing that has ever happened, and that is the great mercy of dogs and also, on that particular morning, quite hard to watch.

---

Then it was me and Buddy in a house.

Here's the thing I have to put down, and it's the worst sentence in this book, and I've written it four different ways trying to make it sound better and it doesn't.

I did not go after her.

I stood in the hall for a while and then I went and made coffee, and by about eleven o'clock that morning I had noticed something in myself that I would give a great deal to have not noticed, which is that some part of me — small, quiet, extremely well organised — had gone *right*.

*Right. Now there's time.*

Nobody coming down at midnight for water. Nobody to be normal in front of at breakfast. No lying about Deventer. A whole house, an old dog who requires nothing but presence, and four hundred hours.

I have been unable to decide, ever since, whether that thought was the disease or whether it was just me. I have put it to two people. Noor said it was the disease. Ana, years later, said it was just me, and said it kindly, which was worse.

That afternoon I got the list off the shelf and cleared the kitchen table properly for the first time since March.

Buddy came and lay under it, on my feet, the way he has for four years, because a man at a table is a man staying put, and that's all he's ever asked for.
