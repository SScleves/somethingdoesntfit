# Chapter Thirty

It took two months and it was the best work I have ever done, and every single document in it is public.

Here is the whole method in one paragraph, because it isn't clever, it's just patient.

A continuity site — a deep facility built to keep people alive through a period during which the surface does not function — is a building, and buildings have to be provisioned, and provisioning is procurement. You cannot buy eleven years of food, water treatment, medical stores, filtration media and diesel without generating documents, and the documents specify **quantities**, and quantities divide.

That's it. That's the entire trick. **Take what they bought and divide it by how long they bought it for, and you get how many mouths.**

I did it for three sites in two countries over eleven years of awarded contracts and framework agreements, cross-checked against air-handling design capacity, which is filed with building control, and against the water treatment licence, which is filed with the environmental authority, and against the medical stores schedule, which is filed with a pharmaceutical regulator because you cannot warehouse that quantity of anything without telling somebody.

Four independent sources. They agree to within about eight per cent.

The three sites are provisioned, in total, for approximately **three hundred and forty people.**

---

Now the other number, and this one took the second month, and it is the number that makes the first number mean something.

Those sites are not empty. They are not sealed vaults waiting; they are *operating facilities*, staffed and maintained, and have been for years, because you cannot mothball plant like that. They have engineers, technicians, security, plant maintenance, medical staff, catering, drivers, cleaners. Some are direct employees; most are on contracts held by named companies, and those companies file accounts and headcounts, and job advertisements are public, and every one of those roles has been advertised at some point in the last decade because people leave.

Add the depot in Anatolia. Add its conservators, its facilities engineer, its cataloguer with Ottoman and Latin, the woman at the reception desk with the visitor book, the man who tells Dr Pekcan the chiller is fine.

I could not get an exact figure and I have never claimed one. What I could establish, four different ways, is the **order of magnitude** of the people who currently keep those places running.

It is between eight hundred and a thousand.

Three hundred and forty provisioned. Around nine hundred maintaining.

---

I want to be extremely careful here, because this is the last inference I am going to ask you to accept and it is the one everything rests on, and if it is wrong then I have wasted three years and I am a man who was wrong.

**What I have:** provisioning for 340. An operating population of ~900. Both from public documents, four independent sources per number, agreeing to within eight per cent.

**What I do not have:** a list of names. There is no list of names. I have never seen one, I do not believe I could ever get one, and anybody who hands you a list of names in a story like this is writing a different kind of book than the one you're holding.

**What the arithmetic supports:** that the number of people provided for is much smaller than the number of people who keep the provision working.

**What the arithmetic does not settle:** which 340.

And here is the thing — I do not need to settle it. That's the whole design and I want it stated plainly, because I have thought about the ethics of this for three years and I am not comfortable and I have done it anyway.

**I am not going to tell anybody they are not on the list. I am going to tell them that there is a list, and that it is about a third of the size of them, and that they can check the arithmetic themselves in an afternoon.**

Every step is public. Every division is one I did on paper. Anyone with an evening and a calculator can reproduce all of it, and disagree with it, and correct me — and if I am wrong, then the correction costs those people one evening and costs me my last three years, and I would rather that than be a man who asked anybody to take his word for anything.

---

I went back on the fourteenth of July. A Wednesday, because Dr Pekcan has the plant meeting on Tuesdays.

I had requested two items, both dull, both from the 2017 schedule. She had them ready. She said the chiller had failed in June and that she had been proved right, and she was extremely pleased about it.

I let her talk about the chiller for four minutes and then I said, "Aylin, I need to tell you why I'm actually here, and you're going to want to sit down, and afterwards you may want to call somebody, and that's fine."

---

It took an hour and ten minutes.

I did not accuse her of anything, because she has not done anything. That is the single most important fact in it, and it took me two months to build the whole approach around that one sentence.

I told her what her building is. I told her about a paperback with a ziggurat on the cover and about page 118 and a plate blemish and forty ruled lines in a dead man's handwriting. I gave her the four printings I had brought with me in a bag she'd made me leave at reception and which I asked her to fetch, and I laid them on the reading-room table under her own lamp, laid flat, the way a woman in Leiden taught me, and I showed her two states of the same page.

She is a conservator. It took her about nine seconds per item.

She did not argue with the objects. Nobody who knows paper argues with the objects; that's why they've spent a hundred years working on everything except the objects.

What she argued with — and she was formidable, and she was right to — was everything else. That the direction of the edits was my interpretation. That thirty entries is not a hundred years. That her institution has a published policy and public accounts and a board with two people on it she would trust with her children. That every single acquisition in her building came in through a documented, legal, professionally supervised transfer and she could show me the file for any of them.

"Yes," I said. "I know. I think all of that is true."

"Then what are you accusing us of?"

"Nothing," I said. "You're the best-run archive I have ever stood in and I mean that. I'm not here about the acquisitions."

I put the two sheets of arithmetic on the table.

"I'm here because I'd like you to check my division."

---

She read it twice. She is not a mathematician and she did not need to be; it's four divisions and a comparison.

Then she said, "Where is the list?"

"There is no list. Not that I can get."

"Then this is nothing."

"It's arithmetic," I said. "It's about eleven documents, and all eleven are public, and I have brought the references, and you can do the whole thing yourself tonight. I'm not asking you to believe me about anything. I'm asking you to do a division."

"And what do you imagine happens then?"

And I said the sentence I had written on a piece of paper in a hotel room in May and had carried in my wallet for two months.

"Then you know that there is a number, and that you are probably not in it, and you get to decide what to do about that. That's the entire offer. It's not a request. I'm not asking you for anything and I'm going to get on a plane on Friday."

---

Somebody came the next day.

I don't know who called; I've never asked and I'd rather not know, and if it was Aylin then she did exactly the right thing and I have never held it against her for one second.

He was in the hotel restaurant in Şanlıurfa at half past eight in the morning, at a table with a view of nothing, with a coffee, in a raincoat he had folded rather than hung, and he stood up when he saw me and said my name.

Both of it. Correctly.

"James Klippe. You must be very tired. Sit down. The coffee here is better than the station, which is a low bar and I'm afraid it's the only one I've got."

Three years, and I had genuinely never expected to see him again.

---

I'm going to give you his argument in full, because it is the best argument anybody made to me and it deserves to be stated properly, and because I did not have an answer to it for about forty minutes.

"You've done something interesting," Bram said. "I want to say that first because I doubt anybody else will. You have not tried to publish, which everybody does; you have not tried to steal anything, which the serious ones do; and you have not tried to persuade a journalist, which is the one that always ends worst for everybody, including us. You've gone and done a division sum and handed it to a conservator."

"Are you going to tell me it's wrong?"

"I have no idea whether it's wrong. That is not my area and I am not going to pretend it is." He turned his cup. "May I make the argument? Not to change your mind. You're forty-five and you've spent three years on it, I'm not going to talk you out of anything over breakfast. I'd like it on the record, as you'd say, that you heard it."

"Go on."

---

"Something happens to a place. A city, a coast, a region — it doesn't matter which, and it doesn't matter what; it can be water, it can be weather, it can be the ground.

"Now. Suppose it simply happened. Nobody's fault. In that case people are extraordinary. I have seen it and so have you and so has everyone. They dig each other out. They queue. They give each other things. Within a year they're rebuilding and within ten it's a story their children are bored of, and the whole episode ends up making them *closer*, which is one of the more remarkable facts about your species and I don't fully understand it.

"Now suppose exactly the same event, exactly the same damage, exactly the same number of dead — except that everyone knows a person *chose* it.

"They stop digging.

"They stop within about a day, and they go and find who did it. And they are almost always wrong about who did it. And the finding takes a generation and kills considerably more people than the original event, and at the end of it the place is worse than if nobody had ever lifted a finger."

He drank some coffee.

"That's all. That's the entire thing. There is nothing else in it and there never was. Nobody has ever needed to hide a catastrophe from you — you can look at a catastrophe all day long, and people do, they watch it on their telephones. What cannot be permitted into the record is **the hand.** The sentence that says somebody chose it.

"Because when there is only God to blame, Mr Klippe, we forgive him. And when it is our fellow man, we destroy him. And we have never once, in the whole of recorded history, made an exception."

---

I sat there and I could not answer it.

For about forty minutes I sat in a hotel restaurant with a very good argument in front of me and nothing to put next to it, and there is a version of me that got on the plane on Friday and stopped.

What I had — and it took me most of those forty minutes to find it — is not a refutation. I don't have a refutation. His argument is probably true. That is the horrible part and I have never got round it: I think he is *right* about what people do.

What I have is that it isn't the argument for what they actually do.

---

"Can I put something to you," I said.

"Please."

"Your argument is about catastrophe. About the sentence that says a person chose the flood. Fine. I'll give you that one — I'll give you the whole of it, I think you're right.

"But that's not what's in the manual."

He looked at me.

"I don't know what manual you're referring to."

"No," I said. "Of course."

And then I told him about my mother.

---

I was twenty-three. I was abroad, I'd finished studying, the money was stopping in September and I'd known for two years. And that summer I stopped sleeping, and I rang my mother and told her I thought I might be depressed.

She said two things.

*You're too poor to be depressed.*

And: *That's not depression, it's fear of life. You're scared.*

"I was furious for about eleven years," I said. "And then I understood that she was right, and that she was right in a way that neither of us meant.

"Because by November I had a job I hated and rent I couldn't make and a landlord and a bus timetable — and I was *fine*. I was fine for eleven years. Nothing was solved. Nothing was even looked at. What happened is that a big frightening question got crowded out by a small urgent one, and a small urgent problem is survivable in a way that a large open one isn't, and I took that trade every day for a decade without knowing I'd been offered it.

"So here's my objection, and it's the only one I've got.

"You have just made me a very good argument about panic. But you are not in the panic business. You are in the business of making sure that on the evening a man might have asked the question, the boiler is broken.

"That isn't protecting anybody from the hand. That's making sure nobody ever has a spare hour."

Bram did not say anything for a while.

"And your position is that the spare hour is valuable."

"My position is that it's the only thing that's ever found anything," I said. "Every single item on that shelf in that building was found by somebody who had an afternoon. You know that better than I do — you're the ones who keep the *originals*. You are the only organisation on this earth that has physically demonstrated that it thinks the record matters more than the story, and you have three hundred and forty places, and you have not put your own conservators in them."

---

He was quiet for a moment and then he said, mildly:

"You've got the number."

"I've got an order of magnitude and eleven public documents and four divisions."

"Mm." He turned the cup. "That's not nothing."

"No."

"You understand it doesn't stop anything." He said it without any triumph in it at all, which is why I've never been able to argue with it. "That is not a threat, it's the position. You have not damaged us, you cannot damage us, and if you published every word of it tomorrow you would be a man with a spreadsheet, and § — " he stopped, and smiled slightly at himself, and started again. "And there are people whose entire job is that. You know there are. You've met one of them, in a garden, at a barbecue."

"I know."

"So what exactly have you achieved, do you think?"

And that's the last thing I said to him, and I've never improved on it, and it isn't a victory and I have never once pretended it was.

"About nine hundred people," I said, "are going to spend an evening doing a division sum.

"Some of them will get a different answer and write and tell me I'm wrong, and I hope they do, because two of them will be right and I'll have to publish that as well.

"And the rest of them are going to come to work on Monday knowing there's a number.

"That's all. I'm not going to bring the thing down; I've known that for a year. But you cannot throw a switch that depends on nine hundred people doing their jobs on the day, when all nine hundred of them have just worked out they're not invited.

"You'll have to sort that out first. Contracts, incentives, whatever it is you do — and that'll take you, what. Years? A decade?"

I put money on the table for the coffee, which he'd already paid for, and which he pushed back at me, and which I left anyway.

"That's the whole thing," I said. "I've bought everybody a decade. Somebody else can do something with it."

---

He walked me out to the taxi. He was perfectly pleasant. At the door he said something I have thought about more than anything else he said, and I still don't know what it was for.

"Hendrik would have liked this," Bram said. "He never could do the last part. Forty years and he never once put his name on anything and posted it."

Then: "He'd have been sixty-nine this year. No — I'm wrong. He *was* sixty-nine."

And he did up a button and went back inside, and I got in a taxi and went to the airport.
