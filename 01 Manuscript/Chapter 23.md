# Chapter Twenty-Three

Noor's message came on the Thursday, at twenty past six in the morning, which is when she works.

> **who is the man in the blue shirt**

I didn't know what she meant. I asked. She sent it back to me: a photograph, from May, from my own garden. Ana had put four of them in a shared folder for Saskia and I'd sent Noor the whole folder in April because one of them had the shelf in the background and I'd wanted her to see the two states of the 1934 side by side.

I had sent a woman a picture of my own barbecue to show her a bookshelf.

In it, Dave is at the edge of the frame, out of focus, in profile, with tongs.

> **ME:** friend of mine. golf. why
>
> **HER:** call me

---

Leiden is fifty minutes. She works out of two rooms behind a print shop, and the first room smells of wheat starch paste and the second one is where she does the actual work, and it has a table with a light that lies down flat, which by then I understood was the most important object in my life.

She had the photograph printed out, on proper paper, at A4, which tells you something about her.

"Sit down," Noor said. "Do you want tea? No. Sit down."

She put it on the table.

"In September 2017 a man came to the department for eleven days," she said. "He was there for the transfer. When they broke up the special collections there were about forty crates of material that weren't going to the new facility, and somebody has to decide what happens to those, and that somebody is not an academic, it's a consultant, because no academic will sign it." She tapped the paper. "He was called Kalder. Julian Kalder. He was very kind to me."

"Kind how."

"I was thirty-one and I was crying in a corridor, because they had just told me that the thing I had spent four years on was being — the word was *rationalised*." Her mouth did something. "And he came out and stood with me, and he didn't say any of the things, and after a while he said, *this is a stupid week, do you want to go and get a coffee and complain about it,* and we did, for an hour, and it was the only decent hour of that entire autumn."

I sat in a chair in a workshop in Leiden and did not say anything.

"He asked me about Hendrik," she said. "Not much. Lightly. In among everything else. What he'd been like as a supervisor, whether he was still working on anything, whether he'd kept his papers at home or in the department." She looked at the photograph. "And I told him, because I was thirty-one and grateful and he had bought me a coffee."

"You couldn't have known."

"That is not the point I'm making." Her voice went up half a tone, the only time I heard it do that in three years. "The point I am making is that it worked so well that I have thought of him kindly for eight years. Eight years, James. That is a very long time to be wrong about a Tuesday."

---

I'd been braced for the argument. It's a coincidence, it's a common face, it's eight years and a bad photograph. I had four of them ready and I don't think much of any of them now, and it turned out not to matter, because she got up and went to the second room and came back with a loupe and put it on the print.

"Look at the watch," she said.

I looked at the watch.

"Now look at the one in Deventer."

I didn't have one in Deventer. I've never photographed anything in Deventer. What she meant was the description Beard Man gave me on a wet Sunday at a market — *fifties, coat, polite, didn't haggle, had a van* — and the recruiter's consultancy with three faces on a team page, two of which sell dental software, and the third of which I had looked at for nine seconds in February and never gone back to.

I went back to it that evening, in a car park, on my phone.

It's him. It's a bad corporate headshot from the shoulders up and it is unmistakably him, and the name under it is not Kalder, and the biography under that is four lines about asset integrity, and I had looked straight at it in February and seen a stock photo, because in February I had never met the man.

That's not detection. I want that clear. That is a man being handed something twice and only understanding it the second time because somebody else did the work.

---

Then I made my mistake, and it was the biggest one I made in the whole business, and I made it in about four minutes.

Because sitting in that workshop with a printed photograph and a loupe and a woman who had just told me something that cost her, I did the arithmetic and got, very fast, to a good outcome.

*Two of us. She's credible. She has hands, she has a trade, she has an accreditation and a professional body. Put my list next to her testimony and my chart next to her lamp and there is a version of this that goes somewhere.*

I actually said some of that out loud. Not the words, but the shape of it: *this changes what we can do.*

And Noor said, "No."

"Noor —"

"No. I'm going to say this once and then I'd like us to be able to keep talking, so please listen to the whole thing before you start."

She sat on the edge of the table.

"I am not going to publish anything, ever," she said. "Not with you, not on my own, not anonymously. I don't sign open letters. I don't do interviews. I have never once put my name to anything about Hendrik and I never will."

"Because you're frightened."

"Because I looked at what it did." She said it flatly. "Hendrik was the best eye of his generation and he died with forty lines in a notebook and a daughter who won't have his books in the house. His father — did you know about his father? — had the same thing and it took him too. Three generations, James. And the total achievement of all of it, at the end, is that a stranger bought some crates at a market."

"That's exactly why —"

"And I have," she said, "a workshop. Two rooms. A lease, eleven clients, a machine I paid four years off, and an accreditation that took me six years and that any three people can take away by writing letters about my judgement. That's it. That's the whole thing I've got, and it is *enough*, and it is mine, and it is not a compromise, and I would like you to stop looking at me like that."

I hadn't known I was.

"I can do more from here than I could from a newspaper," she said. "I can look at things. I can tell you what a paper is. I will do that for you for as long as you want and I will not charge you and I will not stop. But I am not going to be somebody's witness. I've watched what happens to witnesses in this and it's not martyrdom, it's just — being slowly made unemployable by polite people."

---

I got it wrong. That's the whole of what I want to say about it.

For four months I'd been building her, in my head, into a piece of equipment. The credible one. The one with a trade, who could stand next to me and make me not a crank. Not once had I asked her what she wanted, because I'd assumed that anybody who knew what I knew must want what I wanted, and that is precisely the assumption I have spent my professional life laughing at other people for making.

There's a category on that list of forty. The professor never wrote it down as a category; it's just there in the marginal notes, if you read the notes instead of the entries. Some of the people who noticed vanished. Some recanted and signed their own corrections and lived to be ninety. Some got laughed at until they taught secondary school.

And some noticed, and looked at it clearly, and **stopped**, and were never touched at all, and lived.

I had read that as a warning. It's not a warning. It is an option, and by then it was available to everybody in this except me.

---

At the door she gave me the thing, and she gave it because of the one sensible thing I did all day, which was that I asked her a question instead of making an argument.

I said: "When they broke up the collection — the forty crates that weren't going to the new facility. Where did they go?"

Noor stopped with her hand on the door.

"Some of it was disposed of," she said. "Most of it wasn't. That's the thing people get wrong about deaccessioning — if a thing has provenance, you can't just put it in a skip, there'd be a scandal. You transfer it. And a transfer generates paperwork, because somebody has to be liable for it in the van."

"What kind of paperwork?"

"A transfer schedule. Every crate, every accession number, and a destination." She looked at me. "And they're not secret. They're not even interesting. They get published in the annual report, in an annex, at the back, in six-point type, because they have to be, because there's public money in the room."

"You've seen ours."

"I've seen ours." She opened the door. "It went to nine institutions. Eight of them I know. The ninth is a conservation depot in Turkey that had been in existence for four years and had a very good climate-control specification and no publication record whatsoever."

The street was outside. She was already half turned into it.

"Ask them for something," Noor said. "That's what Hendrik would have done and he never did it. Don't go and look. *Ask.*"

---

Buddy went down at ten past three on the Sunday morning and I want to write this part accurately, because it is the truest thing I know.

I woke up because of the sound, which is a sound I hope you never learn. Not a whine. A scrabble — claws on a hard floor, going nowhere, fast, and then stopping, and then going again.

He'd tried to get up and his back end hadn't come with him, and he had been trying, in the dark, on his own, in the kitchen, for some length of time that I will never know and that I think about most weeks.

He wasn't frightened when I got there. That's what did it. He was *embarrassed.* He had his ears back and he was looking at the floor and his tail was going, low, apologetic, because there was a man in the room now and the situation had become somebody else's problem and he was sorry about it.

I put a towel under him and got him up and he could stand, once he was up. He could stand and he could walk badly. I sat on the kitchen floor with him for about twenty minutes because I couldn't work out what else to do, and then I rang the emergency number, and then I carried thirty-eight kilos of Doberman to a car at four in the morning in my dressing gown and did the seventeen minutes to the clinic on the ring road with the hazards on for no reason at all.

---

The vet was about thirty and had been on since eleven and she was extraordinary, and I have never been able to thank her properly because I didn't get her name.

She got down on the floor with him. That was the first thing she did — not the table, the floor, in the corridor, where he was. She spent four minutes just having her hands on him before she did anything that could be called an examination.

Then she said, "Okay. So he's twelve, and this is his back end, and his back end is going to be the thing. It's arthritis, it's some spine, and there'll be a bit of weakness in the nerves as well." She looked up. "It's not tonight."

I said, "Sorry?"

"You're standing there waiting for me to tell you it's tonight," she said. "It's not tonight. He's not in the kind of pain you're imagining, he's stiff and he's frightened of the floor, and those are different problems and I can help with both."

Non-slip mats. A different anti-inflammatory. Something for the nerve pain that costs a fortune and works. Get the water bowl up off the floor onto a stand so he doesn't have to splay. Don't let him do stairs any more — not *try* not to, don't let him, put a gate at the bottom. And a sentence I have repeated to four people since:

"He'll tell you. They do tell you. It won't be a scrabble at three in the morning, it'll be that he stops meeting you at the door. You'll know because you'll have been dreading it and then it'll happen and you'll think, oh, there it is."

We were home by half five with a bag of things and an invoice for two hundred and ninety euros that I would have paid ten times.

---

Then the part I actually came here to tell you.

I couldn't get him up the stairs and I wasn't going to try, so I brought the duvet down and slept on the kitchen floor next to his blanket.

I did that for eleven nights and the first one was the one that mattered.

I woke up at about six, in the grey, on lino, with my hip complaining — and he was awake. Head up. Watching me.

Not distressed. Not wanting anything. Just lying there in the half-dark with his chin off the blanket, watching a man sleep, the way he has watched every single thing I have ever done in twelve years.

I said something to him, and he put his head down, and I went back to sleep.

And I woke up again at eight and he was asleep, and I was awake, and I lay there on the floor and watched *him*, and counted his breathing, which I hadn't done since Mora was a puppy and I thought she'd stopped.

That's the whole thing. That's what I want on the page.

He could do nothing for me. There was nothing he could do. He couldn't understand one syllable of what had happened in the last three years — not the list, not the coffee at the station, not the man on the seventh, not his own back end. He could not help me and I could not help him and the both of us had a problem that neither of us could get out of.

And neither of us left.

I'd read a lot that year about what people mean when they say company. That's it. That's all it is. It isn't help and it isn't understanding and it has nothing to do with being told anything. It's that when you wake up at six in the grey, somebody else is awake, and they are looking at you, and they have stayed.

---

Noor's last message came on the Tuesday of the second week.

> **A man came to the workshop yesterday. Very polite. He wants to talk about my accreditation and about a client's insurance. He knew the client's name. He was here forty minutes and he did not once say anything I could write down.**
>
> **I'm not going to be able to do this with you any more. I want to be clear that nobody threatened me. I want to be clear that I am fine.**
>
> **The transfer schedules are in the annual reports. 2017, annex C. You don't need me for it, it's public, it always was.**
>
> **Please don't reply to this.**

I read it standing at the kitchen counter with a dog asleep on a duvet on the floor behind me.

Then I did the only thing left, which was that I didn't reply.
