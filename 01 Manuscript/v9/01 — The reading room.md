# The reading room

The building was behind a car park, and the car park was the nicer of the two.

Two floors, gray, no windows on the ground level, the kind of place you'd rent to store other people's office furniture. One sign by the door, with the name of a foundation and the word *Archive* in a font chosen by somebody who wanted you to stop reading. It was the second of December, a Monday, four degrees, and the light looked like it had been used already.

I sat in the car for a minute with the engine off. There was a bag on the passenger seat and no dog, and I'm not used to either.

I was there to keep an appointment, and the appointment wasn't mine. A man I never met had made it, in writing, and then died in a chair in March, and by a route I still can't explain in under an hour it had come down to me, the way the dentist's reminders keep arriving for whoever lived in your house before you. He had written the date on the last page of a notebook. Under the date there was one word. *Ask.*

I'd practiced a first sentence at two sets of traffic lights, like a man about to order in French.

Inside there was a desk, a radiator, and a woman with reading glasses on a cord and a badge that said *A. Pekcan, Keeper of Collections*.

"Good morning. My name is—"

"Mr. Klippe," she said. She hadn't looked anything up. "We were expecting you."

"I don't have an appointment. I mean, I do. It's somebody else's."

"Not today," she said. "But we were expecting you."

She turned a big ruled book toward me and held out a pen. Name, date. I've signed in at a lot of buildings. I signed in at this one. She read my signature upside down, which reminded me of my wife, and I didn't enjoy being reminded of my wife in that building.

"Upstairs," she said. "It's been put out for you."

"I haven't asked for anything yet."

"No," she said, and it was the only time her face moved. "You haven't."

---

The reading room was one long table, eight chairs, and carpet that swallowed every step. I looked under the table. It's a habit. There was only more carpet.

A tray was already out, at the place nearest the door, with two foam wedges on it and a book lying open between them. Not the kind of book I'd come about. A ledger, tall, with a cloth spine and *1931* on a label in brown ink. A printer's order book: columns for the customer, the job, the sheets, the price, the day it went out. Whoever kept it had beautiful handwriting and no interest in being read.

About halfway down the left-hand page, one line had been struck through. One ruled stroke, done by somebody who owned a ruler and wasn't sorry. Beside it, in the margin, in a smaller hand, another line had been written in its place.

I read the struck line and the line beside it. Then I read them again the slow way, one word at a time, with my hands in my lap, because nobody had said I could touch it.

The door opened behind me. An old man came in, in no hurry, wearing a cardigan under his jacket like a man who has been cold in this room before. He sat down across from me and smiled as if we'd met. A woman came in after him, in a dark coat she didn't take off, and went to the chair by the window. She didn't say good morning. She put her bag on the floor and looked at me the way I look at a dashboard.

"You found us on a Monday," the old man said. "That's sensible. Mondays are quiet."

He talked about the archive for a while. The humidity, which was a constant worry. The roof. How many meters of shelving, how few readers. People wrote, he said. People wrote all the time. Almost nobody came.

Then, in the same voice, looking at the tray and not at me:

"You found the seats, of course. Everyone who gets this far finds the seats. A bank's disaster plan, a list of names, dependents per allocation, no dogs." He straightened the ledger a few millimeters on its wedges. "We leave them findable."

I had my first sentence ready. It had nothing to do with seats.

Before I could say it, something started up under the floor. A generator, a big one. I've stood next to enough of them to know the sound. The tray began to shiver against the table. The old man lifted one finger, pleasantly, the way you do when a truck is passing and you'd like to finish your thought afterward.

The woman by the window didn't move.

*Eight months earlier.*
