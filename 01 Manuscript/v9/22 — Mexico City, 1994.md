# Mexico City, 1994

Jacobo Grinberg-Zylberbaum was born in Mexico City on the twelfth of December, 1946. When he was twelve his mother died of a brain tumor, and people who knew him have said that this is where his interest in the brain began.

He read psychology at the National Autonomous University of Mexico. He went to New York for his doctorate and spent it measuring the electrical activity of brains, which is done by fixing electrodes to a scalp and recording what comes off it. Then he went home. He set up a laboratory, and later ran one at the university's faculty of psychology, and in 1987 he founded an institute for the study of consciousness.

He was not a marginal man. He taught, he supervised students, he published in journals of neuroscience. He also wrote more than fifty books, and many of them were about things his colleagues would not have put in a grant application. He spent years with Mexican shamans and healers and wrote down what he saw. The best known was a woman called Pachita, who worked in Mexico City and performed what her patients described as operations, with a hunting knife. Grinberg sat in on them. He did not claim to understand what he had seen. He said he had seen it.

Out of all this he built a theory. He called it syntergic theory. In outline it says that what we experience is produced where the activity of a brain meets a structure in space itself, which he called the lattice, and that one brain's activity can therefore affect another's without anything passing between them in the ordinary way. It is not an accepted theory. He knew that, and he thought the way to change it was to measure something.

So he designed an experiment.

Two people sat together in a room and meditated for twenty minutes, with the instruction to feel each other's presence. Then they were separated. Each went into a chamber shielded against electromagnetic signals, and the chambers were fourteen and a half meters apart. Both were wired to record brain activity. One of them was shown a series of a hundred flashes of light. The other was shown nothing and was not told when the flashes came.

A flash of light produces a recognizable response in the brain of the person who sees it. Grinberg reported that in about one case in four a response of similar shape appeared, at the same moments, in the brain of the person who had seen nothing. He called it a transferred potential. He reported that it did not appear in pairs who had not sat together first.

The paper was published in 1994, in a journal called *Physics Essays*, with three co-authors. One of them was a physicist at the University of Oregon. The title mentions Einstein.

It should be said plainly what happened to the result. Other laboratories have tried versions of the experiment since. A few have reported something like it. None has produced a replication that the field accepts, and most scientists who know of the work consider it unconfirmed. That is where it stands.

Grinberg was last seen on the eighth of December, 1994, four days before his forty-eighth birthday. A party had been planned. He did not come to it.

He was a man who traveled, sometimes without telling anyone, and it was a long time before his family treated his absence as a disappearance. When the police did investigate, they found no body and no trace of where he had gone. Nobody has ever been charged with anything. Several explanations have been offered over the years, some ordinary and some not, and none of them has been established.

His books are still read in Mexico. In 2020 a documentary was made about him. It does not say where he is.

I am not going to connect him to anything else in these pages: I don't know what happened to Jacobo Grinberg, nobody who has written about him knows either, and he is a real man with a real family who are still waiting to be told.

He has not been found. He would be eighty this December.
