# A room with a long table

The table was long because the room had been built for twelve and was only ever used by two, and Bram Lievens sat at the end of it with his back to the window because he liked people to have the light, and because he'd been told once that it was kind, and he had taken it to heart.

"Sit anywhere," he said. "No, not there. That's where the chairman sits when there's a chairman. Sit here. How's the dog?"

"Which?"

"His. The old one."

"Slow. Happy. He puts his hand on its head when he's thinking and doesn't know he's doing it." He sat. "I've told you that."

"You have. I like hearing it." Bram had a folder and didn't open it. He never opened the folder in the first ten minutes; he'd explained once that the folder was for after, that the first ten minutes were for the man, and that he'd learned this from someone who'd learned it from someone, and that it was the only part of the job he'd have missed. "The access went through."

"Yes. Eleven minutes. He thanked me."

"He thanked *Julian.*"

"He thanked the name on the invoice. He knew whose it was. He's that sort." He put his hands on the table, flat. "He's found the foundation's line. He's seen the steps. He'll have the dates by now. The last one's June."

"June," Bram agreed, mildly. "That was you, I think. Your bank."

"That was the index."

"The index is a number, Julian. Somebody has to read it to somebody, and somebody has to write the sum down, and in this region that's me, and I write what I'm told." He smiled. He had a good smile, apologetic, as though the world had been arranged slightly to his advantage and he'd noticed and was sorry. "One region. One handler to a bank. You've got yours and I've got eleven of you, and the people I answer to have never asked me the name of a single dog. So tell me what he's doing. Not what he's stopped. You've been asked that before."

"He's reading. He's quiet at work. He signed the form and he signed the annex, with a note on it, in his own hand, about nine names. He plays on Wednesdays and he's happy on Wednesdays. He's been to Leiden."

"We know about Leiden. Leiden's been quiet for nine years. Leiden's a model." Bram turned the folder a quarter turn on the table without opening it. "And the other thing?"

"No."

"Not yet, or no?"

"Not yet." He said it carefully. "He's close to it. He talks to the dog on the course when he thinks he's alone. I've heard some of it. He's got all the parts and he's never put them together, because he's a man who checks paper, and it isn't on paper."

Bram was quiet for a moment, and then he did open the folder, and took out one sheet, and turned it round so that the light fell on it, and slid it halfway down the table, where it stopped.

"I'd like you to look at this and tell me what you see. Honestly. I'd like to know whether I'm reading it right."

Two columns. The left one was a code and a number and a date. The right one was the same code, and a different number, and the same date.

"It's the weekly."

"It's the weekly for one subject. His. Since the spring." Bram sat back. "Left is reporting. Posts, inquiries, letters to dealers, visits to curtain shops. That's what the nightly job gives us; anyone can fill that column, a machine fills it. You can see him go loud in June and go quiet the day he signed. Lovely. Textbook. The right column is the *index.*" He let it sit. "Which has gone up every week since the spring and has not come down once. Not when he went quiet. Not when his access was narrowed. Not when you gave it back."

"I fill in the right column. I don't understand it."

"You fill it in because you can. I can't. Nobody who hasn't had it can see it in somebody else, which is why we keep people like you." He said it without envy, as far as could be told. "I'll tell you what I was told, which is more than I should, because I like you, and because I think it'll make you better at this." Bram folded his hands. "It was never belief. We don't count belief. Belief's weather; belief goes up every time somebody puts a light in the sky on television and down every time a magician's in the room. The index counts the ones who've stopped being *afraid.* That's all. A reading, every week, of how many people in a given population have stopped needing what we sell."

The man at the table didn't move.

"A frightened reader is a reader," Bram said. "He publishes, or he doesn't; he's loud or he's quiet; either way he wants something, and if a man wants something you can seat him. A man who's stopped being frightened doesn't want a seat. He doesn't want to be believed, even. He just *looks.* And you can't house that, Julian, and you can't laugh it off a stage, because he's not on the stage. He's in a kitchen with a dog."

"He's not stopped being afraid. He's not sleeping."

"He's afraid of *us,*" said Bram. "That's different. That's useful. Fear of us is a man who'll take a seat. I'm talking about the other thing. The one that was in him at his wife's grandmother's funeral and has been going out of him, a little, every week since, like a bath." He looked at the sheet. "He hasn't crossed. You're right. But his slope is the steepest I've seen since Leiden, and Leiden went quiet nine years ago and we've never had a reading on her since, because she stopped reporting, and a machine can only fill the one column."

"So what do you want from me?"

"What I've always wanted. Be near. Know what he loves. And when he gets there, which he will, I'd like to know the week." Bram slid the sheet back to himself. "In the meantime we do the decent thing, which is buy him. That's the plan. It's a good plan; it's the one we had for you. A man who signs what he's given can be given something better to sign. He put his initials on the annex. So next quarter's schedule goes to whoever reconciled the annex, which is how these things have always been routed, and nobody has to hand him anything."

"He'll read it."

"Of course he'll read it. He reads the canteen hours. There'll be eleven addresses on it and he'll know one of them." Bram closed the folder. "What he does then is the test. If he comes to us about it, he can be seated. If he goes anywhere else with it, I'll want to know where."

"And the wife?"

"She'll find it. They always do. Let her. A frightened wife is a seat we don't have to pay for."

He stood, which meant it was over.

"You still take him coffee?"

"Wednesdays."

"Good. I like that."

*The index was read on Monday. It was up. The review stayed at the end of the year, and the man with the flask drove to Zwolle and sat in a blue chair and did not turn the light on for longer than usual.*
