# The long table, again

"You've got the week," Bram said. It wasn't a question. He had the folder and he hadn't opened it, and he was looking at the man at the table the way he looked at the index on a Monday: fondly, and for a number.

"Yes."

"When?"

"I can't give you the night. I can give you the morning after it." He had his hands flat on the table. "Wednesday. He plays at seven, alone, with the old dog in the cart. I was on the terrace with a coffee. He didn't see me. On the sixth he stopped with the putter in his hand and stood there for about two minutes, looking at his hands. Both of them. Turning them over, like a man who's been given the wrong change."

"That's all?"

"He missed the putt and laughed. He's played that course for years and I've never seen him laugh at a putt. He talked to the dog all the way down the seventh. He didn't check the car park once, coming in." A pause. "He's not afraid."

"Of us?"

"Of us, yes. The way you'd be afraid of a car. The other thing's gone. You can see it. He stands differently."

Bram smiled. "Looking at his hands."

"Yes."

"That's the one I like. That's the one that goes in the file, if we kept that kind of file." Bram turned the folder a quarter turn. "And you'd know."

The man at the table didn't say anything.

"I'm not asking to be cruel, Julian. I'm asking because I've got six handlers who'd have sat on that terrace and seen a man miss a putt, and one who'd have seen what you saw, and I need to know I'm reading the right one." Bram folded his hands. "You'd know. You're the only one of mine who would."

"Yes."

"How long, for you?"

"Nine years."

"Leiden."

"The month after the transfer. Her kitchen. I'd been in and out with a clipboard for three weeks, and on the last day she gave me tea and didn't say anything for twenty minutes and neither did I." He looked at the table. "I didn't go looking for it. It was in the room. It went through me, and I drove to Zwolle, and I sat in the car, and I understood what the index was. Before anyone told me."

"And you reported it."

"I reported it."

"Your own."

"I'd been told what the right-hand column was for. I filed it, and I understood that I had become the only instrument you had that could see the thing it was measuring, and that there was a seat in that." He lifted his hands off the table and put them back. "So I asked for the seat."

Bram was quiet for a long moment.

"I've always thought that was the bravest thing anyone's done in this building," he said, eventually, and he meant it, and that was the problem with him too. "And the saddest. You know that."

"Yes."

"And now there's another one. In a kitchen. With a dog." Bram opened the folder, finally, and took out the sheet, and turned it to the light, and the right-hand column had a line on it that wasn't a number. It was a name. "I need the line, Julian. Hands, yes or no. In your words."

The man at the table looked at the sheet for a long time.

"James has hands," he said.

The first name. He heard himself use it and so did Bram, and neither of them remarked on it, and it sat on the table between them like a cup.

"Thank you." Bram wrote. One word, in the right-hand column, next to the name. "Then we do what we said we'd do. He reconciled the annex and signed it. He read the schedule and acknowledged it and didn't go to a newspaper. He's kept a dead man's appointment in his diary since the summer, and the head would like to make the offer himself; he enjoys it. Five seats. Him, the wife, three of his choosing. I'll authorize it today." He closed the folder. "The date stays where it is. And if it comes to visiting anybody, afterward, I'll go myself. I'd like you to hear that from me."

"Because of the dog."

"Was it you?"

"Yes."

Bram waited.

"He was at home every day. The car never left the drive, there was a camera on the front, and he'd made his arrangements around the date you gave me. I needed him out of the house at night for four hours and I got four hours. The item was on the kitchen table. The dog lived." He said it the way he read out yardages. "It was efficient."

Nobody had asked him for it, and they both knew that. The room had heard a great many things said quietly over a great many years, and it did not like this one. Bram looked at the table, and then at the window, and then he moved the folder two inches further from the man's hands.

"He'll know," Bram said.

"He knows."

"And he'll sit across a table from you again, if he's asked to?"

"He's polite." The man thought about it. "And he'll want to see if the dog was right."

Bram stood. He went to the door, and stopped, with his hand on it, and said, not turning round: "Nine years, and you've never once asked me whether I've got them. Hands."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you'd have told me," the man said. "You tell everyone everything. It's how you seat them."

Bram laughed, once, surprised, and went out.
