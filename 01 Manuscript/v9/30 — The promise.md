# The promise

It was four in the morning and I was on the kitchen floor, which by now you'll have gathered is not news.

What was news was that I was frightened. Not the interesting kind. Not the kind I get at work when a system starts telling me what it is, which has a pleasure in it I've never admitted to anyone. The other kind. The kind from a corridor when I was sixteen, which I'd spent twenty-five years making sure I never felt again by the simple method of never being responsible for anyone.

Four hours earlier a man had stood in my garden with a beer and said *Deventer*.

He'd said it lightly. He'd been saying everything lightly all evening, to everyone, and everyone had loved him, Ana most of all; he'd sat with her for twenty minutes by the back door while the sausages burned and listened to her talk about her grandmother and the knitting, and I'd watched her face do the thing it does when someone is actually listening, and I'd been grateful to him. I'd stood at my own barbecue being grateful to him. And then later, by the fence, with Frank telling the gorilla story for the third time, an hour after I'd asked Frank about a book that doesn't exist, Julian had mentioned a town. A small one. In the east. Where a man I never met had died in a blue chair.

I have never said the word Deventer to him. I've been there. A woman who sells curtains knows that, and her father's neighbor, and nobody on a golf course.

That wasn't what had me on the floor, though. I'd had since the pub to get used to the idea of Julian. What I hadn't got used to was two sentences about bloodlines. I'd read them forty times that night, on the laptop, between building the thing and testing it, and they were good. They sounded like me on a bad day. Somebody could write as me. And if they could add two sentences to a post about a book, they could add anything, anywhere, under my name: a threat, a confession, a goodbye. The day I needed Ana to believe me, there might be a page of me saying the opposite, dated the week before.

Buddy wouldn't go near him. All evening. Buddy, who goes near everyone, who has pressed his old head against the leg of every plumber and courier and Jehovah's Witness that has ever crossed our threshold, had lain in the kitchen doorway and watched Julian for four hours and had not once come out.

Ana found me at four. She came down for water and she didn't turn the light on, because she knows, and she sat down on the floor across from me with her back against the oven, and the dogs rearranged themselves around her the way they do, and she waited.

I didn't tell her about Deventer, or the post. I couldn't. I didn't have the sentence yet; I had a town and a paragraph and a dog in a doorway, and if I'd said it out loud at four in the morning it would have sounded like exactly what she'd been afraid, for months, I was becoming.

So I said the only thing I had.

"One day," I said, "I'm going to need you to believe me."

She didn't say anything.

"I'm going to say something, and it's going to sound mad, and I'm not going to be able to prove it. And I'm going to say it to your face. Not in a message, not in an email, nothing with my name at the top. You'll know it's me because I'll say it the way I'm saying this. And that day I need you to run."

"Run."

"Take the dogs and go. To your mother's. Wherever. Don't ask me, don't wait, don't pack. Go."

She looked at me for a long time in the dark. Then she said, in her ordinary voice, the one she uses for the shopping and the toaster and whether we're out of coffee:

"Run where? I've got a Doberman."

And I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me like something breaking, and Mora lifted her head at the sound and put it down again, and I sat on my own kitchen floor and laughed with my hand over my mouth so I wouldn't wake the street, and Ana watched me do it with her arms round her knees.

When I stopped she was still looking at me. And then her face changed, the whole of it, the way a room changes when someone turns the big light off and leaves the lamp, and she said, in the other voice, the one I've heard maybe four times in fifteen years:

"Okay."

Just that. Not *okay, what's going on.* Not *okay, but.* Okay.

I've thought since about what it cost her. To be asked that, by me, at four in the morning, with no reason given, and to say yes. I think it cost her more than anything I did afterwards cost me. I think it was the bravest thing anyone did in the whole of this, and nobody will ever count it, because it happened on a kitchen floor between two people and a Doberman and an old dog who had spent the evening in a doorway, watching a man who asked about him before he asked my name.

She got up and got her water. At the door she stopped.

"He was nice to me," she said. "Julian. Tonight. He was really nice."

"I know."

"I liked him."

"I know. So did I."

She nodded. "That's the bit I don't like," she said, and went up.
