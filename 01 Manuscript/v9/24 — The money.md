# The money

I'll give you the rule first. It isn't mine.

When the files are gone, look for the receipts.

Everybody knows the famous version. In 1973 the head of an intelligence agency ordered a program's records destroyed, and they were, and four years later some twenty thousand pages turned up anyway, because they'd been filed with the financial records. The men who burned the files were thinking about what the files said. The men who kept the receipts were thinking about whether the money added up. The second kind is boring, and boring is what survives.

I didn't know I was following the rule. I thought I was doing my job.

---

On the Monday after the drill Saskia gave me my first real task on the program, with the face of a woman handing over a chore she'd been dreading.

"Annex C. Seat allocation. Once a year somebody checks that everyone with a seat is a real employee with the right number of dependents, and signs that they've checked. Then your line manager countersigns and it goes to the vendor. It's due Wednesday. It's very boring."

"I'll need a role to open it."

She looked at her screen. Then she looked at it longer.

"You've got it."

"I asked ten seconds ago."

"No, you've *had* it. It came through already ticked." She scrolled. Saskia owns the access reviews; a grant she didn't make is, to her, a stain on a tablecloth. "The third week of June. That's not right. Nobody had asked for you in June."

"Who ticked it?"

"It doesn't say who. It says *approved.*" She frowned at it a little longer, and then I watched her decide it was somebody else's problem. "It'll be a batch. They pre-provision things. Wednesday, James."

The third week of June was the week I'd opened a ticket about a job with no owner, three weeks before anybody said the words *the person who reads things.* Somebody had laid me a place at this table before I'd been invited to sit down. I wrote the date on the back of the laminated card, under the nine names.

---

It was a list. Everything in this story is a list. I've come to think that if you want to do something large and have nobody look at it, you do it as a list, because nobody reads lists.

Forty-nine rows. Desk number, name, employee number, role, and a column headed *dependents per allocation* with a two or a three or a four in it. Desk one was Anneke de Wit, our head of risk. Desk twelve was Pieter. *Dependents per allocation: 3.* He has two children. It took me a moment to get to three, and when I did I decided not to mention at home that a wife is a dependent.

Frank wasn't on it. I wasn't on it. Desk forty, where I'd eaten a sandwich on Thursday, belonged to a man in Legal who'd been off sick since March. I'd been sitting in for him. That was why my card had been so white.

Reconciling is what I do. You take their list and your list and you match on the one field nobody can misspell. I pulled the staff export from HR and joined it to the annex on employee number, and forty rows lit up.

Nine didn't.

The nine had no employee number. Where the number should be, each had a reference in a different format, beginning with a V. I took the laminated card out of my drawer and turned it over. The same nine names I'd copied off the empty desks. Eight strangers and a man who makes beer, who had a four in his dependents column.

Nine people with a desk in our block, in our colors, on our contract, and no record of ever having worked a day for the bank.

---

I took it to Pieter on Wednesday at ten. It was meant to be a five-minute countersignature. I'd played at seven, and Buddy was down in the car in the basement garage, windows open, on his blanket, which is allowed for exactly as long as nobody asks.

I put the annex on Pieter's desk with the nine rows marked in pencil.

"These aren't staff."

He didn't need to look. "Vendor allocations. They sit under our block. It's a contract thing; it's been like that since before I got here."

"One of them's a brewery."

There was the pause. I've known Pieter's pause since June. He looks slightly to the left of you, and he seems to be listening for something.

"Leave it, James," he said. "Sign."

He'd said two of those words to me once before, at stand-up, in front of nine people, in his manager's voice. This time it sounded like he was asking a favor.

I read the rest while he waited, because I read the rest. Under the table of names there were two pages of terms in small type. Access. Badging. The hours of the canteen. And among them, one line.

*Companion animals are not accommodated.*

I thought about Buddy, four floors below me, asleep in a car. I thought about what kind of emergency has a canteen and a number of children per desk and has had to write that sentence down. Somebody had asked, once. Somebody had wanted to bring a dog, and they'd needed a clause.

"That line," I said.

Pieter leaned over. "It'll be insurance. It's in all of them."

It isn't in all of them. I've read all of them.

I'd like to say I weighed it. What I thought was: this is a lifeboat with a guest list. Some people have paid to sit down if the worst happens, some of them aren't ours, and Pieter's children are on it, so Pieter isn't going to ask. That seemed like the whole of it, and quite enough. And a man who won't sign is off the program by Friday and reading none of it. And the dog was in the car.

So I did what I do with a reconciliation that doesn't reconcile. I signed it with the exception written in. *Reconciled against HR. Forty matched. Nine vendor allocations, no HR record, noted per line manager.* My initials at the foot of each page, four of them, and my name at the end. Pieter countersigned without reading my note, or having read it and decided it was fine.

"Thank you," he said, and meant it.

I kept the signer's copy. I went down to the garage and let Buddy out and walked him round the visitors' spaces until he'd forgiven me.

---

That evening I looked for the money, because Noor had told me to and because I still had a generator I couldn't account for.

Kitchen table, laptop, Buddy underneath with his chin on my foot. The program gave me the contract ledger, read-only, and nobody had ever asked me not to read it at home.

The generator took four minutes. The foundation owned the building and billed the bank a share of the running costs every quarter, sixty percent, fuel and servicing. Dull. Correct.

But the foundation also invoiced the bank for something else. One line, separately, every quarter.

*Continuity reconciliation (recall index). Per flagged person.* A number of persons, a rate, a total. Approved, for the last thirty quarters, by the same initials. *A. de W.*

I read it the way I'd read the sentence on page 118. First time, nothing: a service line, the kind that gets approved because it was approved before. Second time: *per flagged person.* Third time, out loud, to a dog: *recall index.*

I knew that verb. It was in the file I'd captured in June, in the reason column, next to numbers that weren't mine. *Recalled.* I'd taken it for a product term. And I knew what *flagged* meant, because I'd been flagged. Three times that I'd seen, with a two-word reason each.

The ledger went back thirty-one years, so I went back thirty-one years. I put the number of persons in a sheet, quarter by quarter, and looked at it the way I look at anything that should breathe.

It didn't breathe. Costs wobble. This sat flat for years, the same count every quarter, and then it stepped up, once, by a clean amount, like a dial being turned one click. Then flat again. Six steps since 1994.

The last step was dated the Monday after I'd asked my question at stand-up.

I sat with that. Eleven words, said aloud in June in front of nine people, and the next Monday a foundation had put its price up by one click and somebody in risk had initialed it.

They hadn't only counted me. They'd billed for me.

Then I did the thing I should have done at ten that morning, which is read what a document rests on. Every invoice in the ledger had a line at the bottom in small type. *Basis: client reconciliation, Annex C, as signed.*

Continuity reconciliation. I'd spent three days doing something called a reconciliation, for something called continuity, and I hadn't once set the two words side by side.

I don't know how a seat list supports a bill for people who ask questions. I've thought about it since and I still couldn't draw it for you. What I know is what the paper says: the bill cites the annex, and this year's annex has my initials on every page. Next quarter's invoice would go to Anneke de Wit for approval, and if she ever turned it over to see what it stood on, she'd find me.

That's what I'd signed. I learned it about eleven hours after signing, which for me is quick.

I took the signer's copy out of my bag and put it in the folder with the yellow sheet. Two forms in three weeks, both with my name at the bottom in my own hand.

And one more thing followed, the kind I can't leave alone. If somebody is billed every quarter for a number of persons, somebody is counting persons. Every night, presumably, at a quarter past two. The job I'd asked about in June had been renamed and given an owner and a ticket, and I'd been told, in effect, that this was the end of it, and I'd been quiet at work for three weeks like a tired man.

It was still running. It had to be. The invoice said so.

In the morning I'd go and look.
