# Urfa, 1994

The hill had been looked at before. That is the first thing to say, because it is the thing that matters.

In 1963 a joint survey from Istanbul and Chicago had walked the plateau north-east of Urfa, in the far south-east of Turkey, near the Syrian border, cataloging anything that looked old. They noted a low mound with broken limestone slabs on top, and flint on the surface, and a mulberry tree that the local people treated as holy, and they took the slabs for the markers of a medieval cemetery, and they moved on. The hill was given a few lines in a report. The report was filed.

The farmers called it Göbekli Tepe. Potbelly Hill. They had been plowing around the stones for generations and moving the ones that got in the way, and nobody had asked them, and they had not thought to say.

Thirty-one years later a German archaeologist called Klaus Schmidt, who was forty and had been working at a site farther north that had since gone under the water of a dam, and was looking for somewhere of his own, read the survey's report. He read the line about the hill. He read about a cemetery and limestone slabs and worked flint, and it did not fit: flint like that, by the survey's own description, was wrong for the Middle Ages by about ten thousand years.

So in October 1994 he drove out and climbed it.

---

What he saw from the top he described many times afterwards, and the description did not change, which in an archaeologist is a kind of honesty. The slabs were not gravestones. They were the tops of pillars, T-shaped, limestone, set in rings, mostly still buried, and the whole hill, as far as he could tell from standing on it, was not a hill at all. It was a mound of what people had put there. Fifteen meters of it. Deliberately.

He said he knew within a minute. He said, in the version he gave most often, that he had a choice on that hilltop, which was to walk away and tell nobody, or to spend the rest of his life there, and that there was no third option, and that he chose the second, and that he was right about the rest of his life, because he died in 2014, at sixty, with most of the hill still undug.

The first excavation season was 1995. When the dates came, the oldest pillars had been raised around nine and a half thousand years before Christ. Before pottery. Before farming. Before the wheel, before writing, before any of the things that the textbooks said had to come first, because you cannot build on this scale without a surplus and you cannot have a surplus without agriculture and you cannot have agriculture without settling down. That was the order. Everyone knew the order. Schmidt's pillars were some six thousand years older than Stonehenge, and they had been carved, with reliefs of foxes and boars and vultures and scorpions and a headless man, by people who hunted and gathered and were not supposed to have built anything.

He put it in a sentence that got him into trouble and is now in the guidebooks. *First came the temple, then the city.* Not the other way round. People had gathered to build this, and the gathering had required feeding, and the feeding had, perhaps, taught them to farm. The thing that the whole story of civilization said was the effect might have been the cause.

That is what moved, and it is one line: monumental building came before farming, not after it. The rest is still argued over. Schmidt believed nobody had lived on the hill, and that the builders had in the end buried their own enclosures on purpose; the people who dug after him have found reasons to doubt both. Nobody has moved the date back. The enclosures filled with rubble and bone and flint and earth, and the hill grew a mulberry tree, and farmers plowed around the stones, and a survey saw a cemetery, and the file was closed for thirty-one years.

---

That is the record, and it is enough. But there is one more thing on the record and it belongs here.

Nobody hid Göbekli Tepe. There was no conspiracy. A survey team in 1963 looked at a hill and saw what they expected to see, because the thing they were looking at was not possible, and when you are standing in front of something that is not possible the human eye is very good at finding the possible thing nearby and settling on that. Gravestones. Medieval. Move on. The report was honest. The people who wrote it were competent. They were simply looking at a thing from the wrong side of a sentence that everyone knew was true, and the sentence did the rest.

It took one man, reading an old report slowly, to notice that the flint was wrong.

---

About fifteen kilometers from that hill, in the autumn of 1929, an Englishman with one book to his name walked out of Urfa on foot, heading east, with a notebook, and did not come back. He was last seen on the road. He had spent a year in the region. He had, in his one book, said that the traditions of the first settled peoples appeared to have been transmitted to them, and two years after he disappeared somebody changed that to *arisen among*.

There is nothing in any archive that puts H. M. Tresidder on Potbelly Hill. The farmers who might have remembered an Englishman asking about the stones were dead by 1994. The 1963 survey did not record any earlier visitor. This book will not tell you he found it, because it does not know, and neither did the professor, and neither does James, and he has looked.

What is known is where he walked out of. And what is known is what was a morning's walk from the road, under a mulberry tree, in 1929, where it had been for eleven thousand years.
