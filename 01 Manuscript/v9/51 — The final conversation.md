# The final conversation

Aylin booked it. That's the part I keep coming back to. The Friday after the vote I came up the stairs with nothing in my hands, because Anneke had said nothing and I'm a literal man, and Aylin looked up from her cards and said, "He's in. He's got the room till four. I've put you down," in exactly the voice she'd used on the second of December. Then she wrote something in the visitor book. I didn't look.

"Your request is still open," she said. "You asked for an item on the second and it was marked wrongly. I've corrected the card. The item's on the table." She squared a card to the edge of the desk. "That's how a reading room works, Mr Klippe. Whoever is sitting at it."

He was at the long table. Anneke was next to him, not by the window this time; next to him, with a notebook, like a lawyer sitting with a client who's agreed to talk. The lamp on. He looked, I'm sorry to say, extremely well. Better than me. I'd slept about nine hours since Sunday.

There were two things on the table. A gray folder, closed, with a shelfmark on the label that I'd carried in a dead man's handwriting for eight months. And next to it, square to the edge of the tray, thin, no title, a book that had been in my kitchen until a night in November when a dog went down at the gate.

There was no dog under the table. First table in a year. I noticed it the way you notice a tooth is gone.

"Mr Klippe. Sit. You've brought nothing."

"I was told."

"Good. People have been bringing things to this table for forty years. I never needed any of it." He put one finger on the thin book and pushed it an inch toward me. "This is yours. It was collected in error. I'm told the man who collected it is no longer with us, in the employment sense. Take it when you go."

I didn't touch it yet. I wanted to. I didn't want him to watch me want to.

"And that," he said, nodding at the folder, "you've already read. Mrs Pekcan tells me you copied it by hand and left it on the table. Hendrik argued for two pages and never got as far as the chair. You wrote four lines on a slip and came back twice." He folded his hands. "I've been retired. I'm to have an office and no keys. I find I don't mind. I'd like to talk, because I enjoy it, and because it no longer matters. Ask."

I'd made a list all week and thrown it away that morning in the car park.

"What's it for?"

He laughed, the small pleased sound from December. "Oh, that's the right one. Everybody asks *what is it*. Nobody asks what it's *for*." He tapped the table near Anneke's notebook. "Write that down."

"I'm not your secretary," Anneke said, and didn't write it down.

---

"I'll give you the only news first," he said, "because you've earned it, and because the rest is decoration. You've got the sentence. You've got it from a widow, from eleven strangers, and from a dead Englishman in a folder. You think we took it out of the books because we didn't want people to have it."

"Yes."

"We have it. We've always had it. We use it." He said it like a man confirming a train time. "Some people, near the end, remember the last few minutes of the time before. You know this; you've had a hand. What almost nobody knows is that it can be done on purpose. Not the remembering. The *going*. If you go the right way, holding the right thing, you come back to where you were. Same work. Same room. A chair you know." He opened his hands. "That's what it's for, Mr Klippe. That's all it has ever been for. We come back. And a world full of people comparing what they remember over kitchen tables is a world where the chair isn't there when you get back to it. So we keep the number of people who compare inside a range where they can be laughed at. It's not cruelty. It's housekeeping."

The floor hummed. I thought of a hundred desks, four names each.

"Who's we?"

"Fewer than you'd like. More than you'd hope. In this region, me, and before me a man I remember being." He smiled. "You'll want to know how that works and I'm going to disappoint you. There's no machine. There's no physics. There's a method and a sentence, and the sentence is yours, nearly. May I see your copy?"

I had the notebook in my coat. I hadn't planned to show him anything. I put it on the table open at the page, because I wanted to see what he'd do, which is the same reason I'd invented a book in a pub.

He read it. He read all three sheets of Tresidder, the business and the illness and the stones, with one finger on the line the way Pieter had read his sheet, and when he got to the sentence he took a pencil out of his jacket, an ordinary one, and before I could move he put one line through a word and wrote another above it.

*Don't be afraid. Hold on to whoever is holding your ~~hand~~ name.*

"That's ours," he said, and put the pencil down. "One word. His aunt's version takes you wherever the hand goes, and hands go everywhere, that's the trouble with them. Ours takes you back to whoever has your name. A list. A card on a desk. A register in a room like this." He nodded at the visitor book on Aylin's desk through the door. "Somebody holds your name when you go, and you come back to it. It works. It's the only thing in this building that does."

I looked at my own handwriting with his pencil through it. One line, one word, the rest left alone. I'd seen that done to a plate in 1931 and to a forum post in the summer, and now it had been done to me, at the table, while I watched, and I understood why they'd never needed to clean anything. It's quick, and it's small, and the man who did it is pleased with himself.

"That's an edit," I said.

"That's a correction."

"You left the rest in."

"One always does."

---

"The films," I said, because it was on the list I'd thrown away and I wanted to hear him do it.

He brightened. "Oh, you've had those from every crank in the country. I'll be brief. *Men in Black*: every man who finds the agency is now the man in the film. The moon as a collector of souls: that one was for fun. *Dracula*, so that *we don't die* belongs to a man in a cape. Walt."

"Walt Disney."

"Walt was our best customer. He never knew."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me like a sneeze, a real laugh, in that room, with the lamp, and once it started I couldn't stop it, and I had to put my hand over my face.

"Something funny?"

"Walt fucking Disney," I said. "Sorry. You've just told me you come back from the dead by holding a list, and you've followed it with *Walt Disney*. Is he frozen? Is he downstairs?"

"Freezing," he said, quite seriously, "is meaningless."

"Course it is."

Anneke was looking at her notebook. I think, I'm not sure, the corner of her mouth moved.

"You don't believe the films," he said.

"I can't check them. I can check a plate. I can check a desk number." I looked at him. "You've told me three boasts in a row and I laughed at all three. That's the technique, isn't it. Say it next to something stupid."

"You see," he said to Anneke, "this is why I asked for him."

---

"The seats," he said, and leaned in, not for secrecy, for pleasure. "You'll want to know about the seats."

"Desk twelve."

He stopped.

"Pieter's. And a man in Rotterdam, and a consultant in Nijmegen, and a woman with two surnames. Four names, one chair. There are a hundred desks on that floor and I counted them at the drill. They're not for sitting in. They never were. A seat is what you promise a man so he'll leave a job alone for thirty years, and you can promise the same chair to four men because none of them will ever sit in it." I sat back. "A man told me that over coffee. I checked it against my own initials. The lifeboat's a lie with a floor plan."

He looked at me for a moment with no face ready, the way he'd looked in December when I'd mentioned the dog.

"Yes," he said. "Well. You've saved me a paragraph."

"Why four?"

"Because three looked thin on the invoice."

Anneke wrote that one down.

---

And then I said it, and I hadn't planned to, and it came up from a kitchen floor in November, the fourth night, and I said it the way you'd say it to a dog.

"It's not for staying."

He looked at me.

"The sentence. Your version holds the name and comes back to the chair. His version holds the hand." I put my finger on Tresidder's word, under his pencil line. "A man in a market told me a thing his dealer told him, which the dealer got from a professor, who said it when they buried a dog. *We all go back in the one bucket. The ones who held on come out together.* Together. Not back. You don't hold somebody's hand to come back to a room on your own. You hold it so you don't go on your own. That's the whole instruction, and you've been using it to go round and round the same building."

It was very quiet. Even the floor seemed to go quiet, which it didn't, I know it didn't.

"Yes," he said.

That was all, for a second. Then: "We chose the room. What would you have done? On one side, everything you've ever been afraid of, and whoever has your hand, and you don't get to pick. On the other, this room, these people, the work, a chair you know. We chose the chair. Every time it costs more. Every time there's less of us to bring back." He looked at his hands. "We don't know where the other way goes. We stopped wanting to know a long time ago. Hendrik wanted to know. That's why he wrote two pages and I gave him a date."

I wanted to feel sorry for him. I checked, the way you check a pocket. I didn't.

---

"So tell it," I said. "If you're done. If it doesn't matter. Let it out."

"It can't come out," Anneke said.

We both looked at her. She hadn't spoken since *I'm not your secretary*, apart from writing down *thin on the invoice*.

"I've spent a week on this," she said. "Not his version. Mine. Suppose everybody knew, tomorrow, as a fact, that it goes on, and that you can hold on to somebody through it. What happens on Thursday? Nobody goes to work. The hospitals, the water, the grid, all of it runs on people who are more afraid of dying than of their jobs." She closed the notebook. "I don't know whether that's been built on purpose. He says it has. I've read the recovery plans and I can tell you what they assume. Take the fear out fast and you don't get a better world. You get a week with nobody at the water board. I'm head of risk. I'm not doing that to a country on the strength of one letter."

"So the deal's the same as it always was," I said.

"No. The deal's mine now." She opened the notebook again, to a page she'd already written. "The nightly job goes. Frank closes it, through a ticket, like a normal person. The line on the invoice steps down; not to nothing, there's a contract, but down, and I read it before I sign it. The annex gets rewritten by HR, with one name per desk, and the desks stop being sold. The schedule is not signed, this quarter or any other, until somebody can tell me what a visit is in a sentence I'd put in a board paper. Continuity stays. The seats stay, for the people who think they've got them, because taking them away is louder than leaving them. And you keep quiet." She looked up. "The bank, the names, the foundation. Nothing."

"And him?" I didn't say the name.

"Julian Kalder's contract is under review. He didn't come in Monday. He's not answering." She paused. "He's not the bank's problem anymore. He may be somebody's. I've chosen not to ask whose."

The old man smiled at the table. It was the only time I wanted to hit him.

"And you can write a book," Anneke said. "You asked me, the day you gave me my sheet, whether you'd ever be allowed to. Under your own name. It claims nothing and names nobody. If anyone asks, it's a novel."

"We've been publishing those for four hundred years," the old man said. "Nobody reads the middle."

"I read the middle," I said.

"Yes. Well. There's one of you."

"Agreed," I said. To her, not to him. "All of it."

"Good." She capped her pen. "Is there anything else?"

"One thing. It's small."

"Say it."

I didn't say it out loud. I leaned over and said it to her, low, close to her ear, like a man telling a secret at a wedding, and the old man watched us and didn't hear it, and I've never written it down and I'm not going to now. It wasn't about the bank. It was mine.

She sat back. She looked at me for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "That I can do." And she wrote one line in the notebook, and turned the page so it was face down.

---

He stood up. He was old and it took him a moment. Anneke didn't help and he didn't ask.

"Leave it," he said to me.

Two words. The ones Pieter had used. The ones the whole building was built on.

"Not as a threat. As advice from a man who's had the keys. You've bought a generation, perhaps, in this part of the world. Then it starts again somewhere else, under a different word. You'll be dead by then. So will I, for a while." He looked at the thin book. "When it's your turn, do what you did on the floor, and hold on to the hand. Go. Don't come back. I'd like one of us to have tried it."

He went out. Anneke stayed sitting with her hand flat on her notebook.

"Is he right?" I said. "Eighteen months and you're him?"

"I don't know." She stood. "I approved an invoice thirty times without reading it. I'm reading things now." She picked up her coat. "He's got the room till four. Stay, if you like. It's the only room in the building that doesn't hum."

That wasn't true. It hummed. But I stayed.

I picked up the thin book. Forty-one entries, in a hand I'd learned, and his sentence at eleven, and the last page with its shelfmark and its date and its one word. It was all there. Somebody had been careful with it. That was almost the worst thing.

Then I sat at the long table with a dead man's folder and my own notebook and went through what he'd said, one thing at a time, like a list of alerts at the end of a shift. The films I couldn't check and didn't try. The chair, the name, the coming back: I had his word for it and a pencil line, and I've spent my life not taking anyone's word for anything. Every piece fit the next one perfectly, one long clean line from a flood survey to the floor under my feet, and that's what bothered me. Real things don't fit like that. Real things have a torn corner somewhere. He'd done to me what they'd done to everybody: handed me a story so neat it was impossible to believe and impossible to throw away.

What I could check was on the table. A letter from 1929 with a sentence in it, and the same sentence from a widow and eleven strangers, and a pencil line through one word in my own handwriting, made in front of me, by the man who'd ordered the line through the plate. I'd watched him do what they do. That I could put in front of anybody.

I rubbed his word out. It took a while; he'd pressed. Then I wrote Tresidder's back in, in my own pencil, and under it, so I couldn't later pretend I hadn't seen it: *Changed to "name" by the head of this region, in front of me, in the reading room. Changed back.*

I decided I'd work out which parts I believed later. Somewhere with grass. Somewhere with a dog.

When I went down, Aylin didn't look up. But the visitor book was open on her desk, and next to December, next to my name, the word *Pending* had a neat pencil line through it, and nothing written beside it yet.
