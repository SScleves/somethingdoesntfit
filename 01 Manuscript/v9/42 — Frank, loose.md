# Frank, loose

Frank came round on a Saturday because I asked him to, which I'd never done, and he knew it, and he brought beer anyway, which is how I knew he was worried.

Ana was home. She'd been home a week and a half, with Mora, who had eaten a cushion at her mother's and shown no remorse, and the house had gone back to being a house: two dogs, a kettle, somebody upstairs. I'd forgotten how much of my thinking I do against the sound of another person in a building.

Frank sat at the kitchen table. Buddy went and lay under it, across his feet, which he does to people he's decided about, and Frank said, "Oh, hello," and went very still the way people do, and I thought, good, that'll keep him here.

"So," Frank said. "You've got the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You've had the face since about April. Pieter's noticed. Pieter thinks you're job-hunting." He opened a beer. "I told him you don't job-hunt. You'd have to talk to people."

"Thank you."

"So what is it. And if it's the Egyptians, I'm going home."

---

I'd thought for a long time about how to do this. For Ana I'd put every object on a tablecloth, because Ana needed to see that I'd labeled what I couldn't prove. Frank needed the opposite. Frank needed almost nothing, and no story, and to be left alone with it.

There was a reason for that, and it was his. In his kitchen in the summer he'd put the thin book in the wrong column in under a minute: anything from a market is a story with paper attached. He was right. Most of what I had, somebody had handed me. So I'd spent a week making a pile of the things nobody had. And before I carried that pile into a room in December, I wanted to know whether paper like that could move the most stubborn man I know when I wasn't there to help it.

So I put two things on the table and kept my mouth shut.

The first was a sheet I'd made myself. Thirty-one years of quarterly invoices from a foundation to the bank, pulled from our own ledgers one evening, a line per quarter: *Continuity reconciliation (recall index). Per flagged person*, with the number of persons, the rate, the total, and the same two initials approving every one.

The second was a stack. The same foundation's annual accounts, which it has to publish, like any charity. Thirty-one of them. I'd downloaded them from the register for nothing. Income on one page, grants made on another.

"What am I looking at."

"Money."

"I can see it's money. Whose?"

"The top one's ours. The bank pays that, every quarter, and has since 1994. The stack is theirs, the people we pay. It's public. Anyone can get it."

"And what am I looking for?"

"I'm not going to say."

"James."

"If I tell you, you'll find it to be nice, or you'll not find it to be right. I want to know what's there if nobody tells you. Kill it. Like the screenshot."

He looked at me. Then he looked at the stack, the way he looks at a log file somebody says is clean. "Have you got a pen?"

---

It took him three hours and I'm not going to pretend I enjoyed them.

He started with the invoice, because it was one page. He's good with numbers; it's why he's good at the job. He found the steps on his own in about four minutes. Six of them in thirty-one years, the count going up by clean amounts and never coming back down.

"Per flagged person," he said. "Flagged how?"

"I don't know how. I know what the list looks like. It's the honeypot."

He put the pen down. "The what?"

"The job Pieter told you lot was a honeypot, the week I was out. That's what it sends. Staff numbers and two words. It's billed per head, to these people."

"That's not what a honeypot—" He stopped. He looked at the last step on the sheet, and at the date beside it, and I watched him work out which week in June that was. He didn't say it. He picked the pen up again.

Then the accounts. He checked the income first, which I hadn't expected and should have. The foundation's income from *reconciliation services* stepped in the same six years as my sheet. Their books and ours, kept by different people in different buildings, with the same staircase in them.

"So your sheet's real," he said. "Fine. A bank pays a charity. It's dull, but it's real."

Then he read the grants. All thirty-one years. Heritage. Conservation. Digitization. A great deal of roof. I made coffee. Ana came down, looked at Frank, looked at me, and went back up without a word, which is the nicest thing she's ever done for him. Buddy slept on his feet.

Around midnight he started writing years in two columns on the back of an envelope.

"This is stupid," he said.

"Okay."

"No, it's stupid. Look." He turned the envelope round. "Most years they give money to roofs. Some years they give it to something else. A production company, 2003, enough to make a documentary. A publisher, 2011. A *retreat center*, 2014, with a name like a yogurt. A festival. Something that calls itself a channel. Five of those. Six, if you count the second payment to the yogurt."

"And?"

"And I got bored and wrote down the years where your head count dips. Inside a step. It goes down some years, did you know that? Not much. Fewer people flagged." He tapped the left column, then the right. "They're the same years."

I didn't say anything. I'd promised myself.

"Every time your number's low, these people pay somebody to make a film about pyramids." He sat back. "That's the wrong way round, James. If you were hiding something you'd pay for the nonsense when people got *close*. When the number's high. This is paying for nonsense when nobody's asking at all."

"Why would you do that?"

"I don't know. To keep them asking? Badly?" He heard himself. "Oh, you *bastards*," he said, to the envelope. And then, to me: "You knew."

"I had the two piles. I hadn't put them together. I wanted to see if you would."

"That's a horrible thing to do to a guest."

"You got beer."

He went back down the columns with the pen, because he's Frank and a pattern that neat offends him. And that's where he found the other thing.

"2007," he said. "It's cold. Coldest on the sheet. And there's nothing. No film, no yogurt. Roofs."

I looked. He was right. I hadn't seen it.

"Maybe it's in a different line."

"I've read every line. I've read the *notes*." He turned the page toward me. "It doesn't fit."

"No."

"So what was different in 2007?"

"I don't know."

"Good," Frank said. "I'd have left if you did."

---

"Now," he said. "What's wrong with it."

He told me. He was thorough. He said five grants in thirty-one years is a small number, and a man with a pen can find five of anything. He said foundations are badly run and bill in round figures because somebody's nephew does the accounts. He said *flagged* is a word consultants use for everything, that the job could still be what Pieter said it was, and that my number turning up on a list the night after I'd asked about the list was the most ordinary thing a security tool could do.

"And the book thing," he said. "The sentence. I believe you found it. I think it's a dead scholar's second thoughts, and I think you've built a cathedral on it because you needed somewhere to put what you felt at that funeral. I say that as your friend, and as someone who's watched you do it with smaller things for three years."

I didn't argue. I'd heard it all before, in my own head, in my own voice, which is better at it than Frank's.

"So I don't believe it," he said.

"Okay."

"I don't believe any of the story." He put his finger on my sheet, on the last step. "I believe the invoice."

I looked at him.

"That's the bit I can't do anything with. I've sat here all night trying to make that be something boring, and I can't. Their books match yours. Nobody steps a contract up by one clean unit in the week a monitoring engineer opens his mouth at stand-up, unless the unit is the engineer." He took his finger off. "And I found the film thing myself. That's what annoys me. If you'd told me, I'd have had it in the dead column by ten."

"I know. That's why I didn't. The memo's the table."

"Don't quote me at me."

---

It was nearly a quarter past two when he stood up, and I know that because my phone buzzed on the counter while he was looking for his coat.

It has a sound for this. I'd heard it once before.

*1 new listing. Match: Hengelo.*

I opened it standing up. A terraced house, photographed that week. Three bedrooms, a long garden, somebody's reading glasses still on a windowsill. *For sale owing to the owner's move into residential care. Available immediately.* Thursday had been the date on the schedule. It was Sunday morning now, by fourteen minutes.

"What's that?" Frank said.

"A house has gone up for sale."

"Whose?"

"A woman I've never met. I knew her address and I knew the date, for more than two weeks, and I didn't go, because I've got a rule about not telling people things until I can prove them." I put the phone face down. "She's in a home now. That's what the rule cost, and it wasn't me who paid."

Frank looked at me for a long moment. I saw him decide not to ask, and I was grateful. He's better at that than anyone who's only met him at a barbecue would guess.

"Is that on one of your sheets?" he said. "Her. The house."

"It's in the paper. It's public."

"Then put it with the others," he said, and found his coat.

At the door he said, "What are you doing with it. The pile."

"Taking it to a meeting on Monday."

"With who?"

"The people in the stack."

He nodded slowly. He looked back into the kitchen, where Buddy had got up from under the table and was standing at the window in the dark, watching the street.

"He does that," Frank said.

"He does that."

"I still don't believe it," Frank said, and went out to his car, and sat in it for a minute before he drove away.

Ana came down. "Well?"

"He believes the invoice."

"Is that good?"

"It's Frank," I said. "It's the best he's got. It's better than I had."

I cleared the table. The photographs of the thin book and Els's folder went back in the drawer. They were true and they'd been handed to me. What went in the bag for Monday was the other pile. Two copies of a book I'd compared myself. Captures I'd taken. A sheet of invoices I'd pulled. Accounts anyone can download, and an envelope in Frank's handwriting. Five weeks of a forum, copied nightly. Ten addresses, checked, with one more line to add.

I added it.
