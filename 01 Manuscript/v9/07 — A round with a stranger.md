# A round with a stranger

On Monday I wrote to three dealers, asking for a copy of the 1931 printing. On Wednesday I played golf.

Seven o'clock, dew on everything, Buddy in the cart with his ears in the breeze, and up ahead the four retirees on the third, where they've been since 1998. That's the whole point of a Wednesday. Nobody on the tee, nobody behind me, and four hours in which the only witness is a dog who has seen worse.

Except the man who runs the shed came out with his mug and said there was a single on the first already, a guest on a day ticket, and would I go round with him, because two singles plus the retirees was more traffic than the course could carry. I said of course. I say of course to everything. It's faster. Inside I was already working out how much small talk I had in me, and the answer was about three holes.

He was on the tee, stretching. A few years older than me. A good coat, the kind that's been good for years. Clubs that weren't new and had been kept clean. He turned around when he heard the cart, and he didn't look at me.

"How old?" he said. To the cart.

"Twelve."

"Twelve." He crouched. He didn't put his hand out, which is the thing people do and the thing dogs hate. He just crouched where Buddy could see him and let Buddy decide. Buddy decided, after a while, that he could be looked at. "Hello, foreman," the man said, and I don't know how he knew that word, except that it's the only word if you've ever seen Buddy in a cart. "What's he supervising?"

"Me. It's a full-time job."

"I bet. What's his name?"

"Buddy."

"Buddy." He stood up, with the small noise men our age make. "Julian."

"James."

"Jim," he said, as if trying it on, and shook my hand. Nobody has ever called me Jim in my life. I let it go, because it was seven in the morning and the course was wet and gold, and I'd spent two months in a submarine.

---

Then he hit, and I stopped feeling sorry for myself and started feeling sorry for my swing. It was the kind of swing I would pay for. No effort in it. The ball left like it had somewhere to be.

I put mine into the trees, as per the club's website. He helped me look, and he was funny about it the whole way, and never once offered advice, which in a man who can play is close to sainthood. On the second green I missed from four feet and he said, "Oh, that's a relief," with such obvious pleasure that I laughed out loud.

He asked about the course. He asked about the retirees. He asked where the dog slept, and whether there was a second one at home, and when I said a Doberman he said he'd had a Doberman once and she'd eaten a sofa, and that it had been the best sofa he'd ever had and he hadn't missed it for a second. Somewhere around the fifth he asked if I always played alone, and I said Saturdays with my wife, who'd had a rough spring, her grandmother. Half a sentence. He nodded and didn't say he was sorry, and I liked him for that too.

My three holes of small talk were long gone by then. I hadn't noticed them go.

On the seventh he missed the green, which I enjoyed, and then from the fringe, from a lie I wouldn't have given a man money to try, he chipped it in.

It went up, and it landed, and it rolled, and it went in with the little sound. He stood there with the club still up and his mouth open, like a man who'd been handed a baby. Then he looked at me and said, "I'm never going to be able to explain that to anyone," and I laughed so hard that Buddy barked.

If you want to know when I decided I liked him, it was then, and it was the sentence, more than the chip. He'd done the one impossible thing of his year and his first thought was that nobody would believe him.

I knew that feeling. I'd been living in it since the funeral.

---

We had coffee in the clubhouse. It's a shed with a machine, but they call it the clubhouse, and at half past nine on a Wednesday it's empty. We sat by the window with Buddy under the table and the retirees visible on the fifth, having made two holes of progress in two and a half hours.

"What do you do, Jim?"

"IT."

"Ah." He nodded, the way everyone nods. Then, instead of the next thing everyone says: "What kind? I ask because my nephew says IT and means he fixes printers, and my brother-in-law says IT and means he's a millionaire. I never know which."

"Neither. I watch things. Software that watches other software. When something's dying in the night I'm the one they call."

"The night shift of the night shift."

"That's actually good. Can I have that?"

"It's yours." He drank. "I'm in continuity, for my sins. Insurance, basically, for things that can't be insured. If your building burns down, who does your payroll on Monday. If your records go, what's the copy. Very dull. Nobody at a party has ever asked a second question."

"What's the copy," I said.

"Sorry?"

"You said, if your records go, what's the copy. That's the whole job, isn't it. Everybody thinks the job is the thing. The job is the copy." And then I heard myself keep going, which I don't do. "It's my hobby too, if I'm honest. Old books. The same book printed twice doesn't always say the same thing, and nobody tells you. I put the two printings side by side and look for the difference."

He looked at me over his cup a second longer than a man looks at you over a cup. Then he smiled, properly, like somebody who has found something at a market.

"And is there one? A difference?"

"Mostly no. Mostly it's four hours with a ruler."

"And when there is?"

"Then I write it down, and my wife asks what size I want my tinfoil hat."

He laughed. He didn't ask which books. I noticed that he didn't, and I was grateful, the way you're grateful when somebody doesn't ask what you earn.

I have a rule about what I tell people at tables. I'd kept it for thirty years. That morning I gave a man I'd known for two and a half hours my dog's name, my wife's bad spring, and the one thing I do that I actually care about, and it didn't feel like breaking anything. It felt like a good coffee, which it wasn't.

"So who does it?" he said. "Your payroll. On the Monday."

"If the building burns down?" I thought about it. "There's a plan. It's in a binder. Somewhere there's a list of everything that has to run."

"Have you read it?"

"Nobody's read it."

"Somebody should," he said. "I say that as a man who sells binders." He stood and collected his cup. "If I'm round this way again I'll bring a flask. I'm a flask man. You'll find that out about me."

"I'm here every Wednesday."

"Then I know where to find you," he said, and patted Buddy, and put his good coat on, and went.

---

Ana noticed at dinner.

"You're in a mood," she said. "A good one. It's disturbing. Did something happen?"

"I played golf with a man who chipped in from the fringe."

"That's it?"

"He said he'd never be able to explain it to anyone."

She looked at me for a bit. "Okay," she said. "I'll take it. I'll take Wednesday golf man. Bring him round."

I told her he was only a guest, a day ticket, some work thing in the area, and she said that was a shame, and I said yes, and meant it more than I expected to. I'd driven home with the window down and Buddy asleep in the back, giddy, like a kid who's been picked for a team by the one who's good.

He'd asked about the dog first. I kept coming back to that. Everybody asks about the dog eventually. He asked before he asked my name.

The other thing I kept coming back to was smaller and more like me. It was the payroll. I've watched that bank's systems for eleven years and I could not have told you, honestly, what would run on the Monday.

So on Thursday morning, before the coffee, I went and found the binder.
