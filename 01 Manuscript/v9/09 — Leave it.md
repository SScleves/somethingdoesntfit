# Leave it

The binder turned out to be a file, four hundred pages long, and nobody had opened it in nineteen months. I know because files keep a list of who opens them, and I looked. That's the kind of thing I look at.

It answered Julian's question on page nine. If the building burns down, payroll is done on Monday from a recovery site in a business park, by people who get there on a bus. Fine. The part that concerned me was an appendix at the back: the inventory. Every scheduled job the bank would need to bring back, in order, each with a name, an owner and a reason.

My job, in one sentence, is to look at screens that look at other things. Ten thousand systems, every night, breathing, and my programs count the breaths and put the counts on a wall. Green is fine. Amber is a meeting. Red is my phone at four in the morning. But green doesn't mean nothing's wrong. Green means nothing's wrong that we decided to look for. So I don't look at the colors. I look at the list under the colors, all the little jobs that run on timers, the housekeeping. In a company our size it's a few thousand lines long and nobody reads it, the way nobody reads the ingredients.

So I had two lists of the same thing. What the plan says we run, and what we run. I did what I do with two copies of anything. I put them side by side.

They matched for three thousand eight hundred and twelve lines. The scheduler had three thousand eight hundred and thirteen.

The extra one was called `rcl-agg`.

Seven characters, which is the kind of name you give something when you're in a hurry or when you don't want it read. No owner. The owner field is mandatory. You literally cannot save the form without it, and it was blank. No ticket. No description. It ran every night at fourteen minutes past two, for about nine seconds, and sent roughly forty kilobytes of something to an address. When I looked the address up at ten in the morning it didn't exist. As far as I could tell it existed for nine seconds a night.

I took a screenshot. I screenshot things. It's the Record's fault.

Then I did the obvious checks, and they took me a week, because I do them properly and because I was hoping one of them would make it go away. Who created it, and when? There was no history. The first entry in the history of `rcl-agg` was `rcl-agg`, already running, as if it had been there before the list was. The other offices: same job, same minute, same forty kilobytes. The backups, as far back as the backups go, which is a long way, because the man who set them up was paranoid in the useful way. It was in all of them. Every night. Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds.

I'd been sleeping badly since the sentence. I don't know whether that's why I saw it. I'd like to think I'd have seen it anyway.

On the Friday I put it in the stand-up.

---

Stand-up is fifteen minutes in a glass box, standing, so that nobody gets comfortable enough to talk. Pieter runs it. Pieter is my manager, and a decent man in the specific way that managers are decent, which is that he'll defend you to the end of the earth about anything that doesn't cost him anything.

There were nine people in the box. I went last. I always go last. The persona likes to see the room first.

"There's a job with no owner," I said. "I want to know why."

"Which job?"

I told him.

Something happened in Pieter's face. I don't want to make it bigger than it was. It was the size of a man remembering an appointment. He looked at his screen, and for about as long as it takes to find a card in your wallet he said nothing at all.

"That one's old," he said. "It's a diagnostic artifact. From the migration."

"Which migration?"

"The big one. Before your time." He was still looking at his screen. "Leave it."

"It sends data somewhere."

"It sends diagnostics somewhere. It's been fine for years. Leave it, James, honestly. We've got the payments thing and the Antwerp thing and I'd rather have you on either of those than on a timer from the Stone Age." He smiled, and it was a real smile, and the meeting moved on. Frank caught my eye across the box and made a little skull with his hand, which is our thing now, apparently. I've decided to find it charming.

Here's the problem. The big migration wasn't before my time. I did the big migration, a lot of it, two years before Pieter joined. There wasn't a `rcl-agg` in it. I'd have known. I read the ingredients.

I didn't say that. It wasn't courage and it wasn't cunning. Pieter had said *leave it* in the tone you use for something that's already been decided, and I've spent thirty years learning that when my memory and the paperwork disagree, I'm the error. So I nodded, and went back to my desk, and felt the thing I'd felt in the kitchen with the two books. Only this time I was feeling it about a person.

Because people who don't know what a thing is say *I'll look into it*. Pieter hadn't said that. He'd gone quiet, and then he'd come back with a whole sentence, with a cause in it and a date and an instruction. Nobody has that sentence lying around about a seven-character job. Either he'd needed the pause to remember it, or he'd needed the pause to decide to use it, and I sat at my desk until seven with the list open and couldn't work out which I liked less.

---

Ana was in the kitchen with the dogs when I got home, and the dogs were in the kitchen with Ana's dinner, strategically.

"You're late."

"Payments thing."

"Mm." She was reading something on her phone, and she lifted it without looking at me, the way she lifts it when she wants me to see that she's not looking. "You've got a letter. Actual paper. It's on the side."

It was a good envelope, the kind with a lining, and my name was on it in a hand I didn't know. Of the three dealers I'd written to, one never answered and one had sent me four words, *not in stock, sorry.* The third was a shop in Groningen with a website from 2004, and he'd answered an email with a fountain pen.

*Dear Mr. Klippe,* it began, and I stood in my kitchen feeling oddly caught, as if I'd been spotted in a shop I'd told everyone I didn't go to. It was the first time I'd seen my name written down by somebody who had anything to do with the book.

He had a copy. The second impression, Leiden, 1931, sound, a little foxed. The price was three hundred and forty euros, which he was aware was a great deal for a book nobody read. They had become hard to find and he couldn't tell me why. He'd last sold one in 2011, to a university man in Deventer who had been very particular about the impression. If I wanted it, I could send the money and he'd send the book.

Then a postscript, in slightly different ink, as though he'd come back to the letter after a cup of tea. *I would gently say that this title attracts a certain kind of inquiry, and that I have found, over forty years, that it is wisest to be plain about one's reasons.*

I read that twice. Then I read it to Buddy, who was under the table in case of a chicken event.

"'Plain about one's reasons,'" I said. "What does that mean?"

"It means he thinks you're a nutter," said Ana, not looking up. "What's the book?"

"The one with the two copies."

"The one you measured."

"The one I measured."

She put the phone down then. "And a man in a shop in, where?"

"Groningen."

"A man in a shop in Groningen has written to you on nice paper to tell you to be careful."

"To be *plain.*"

"That's what careful sounds like in a shop." She picked the phone back up. "Did you know Mora ate half a sponge today? Just so we're all plain about our reasons."

I laughed. She has better lines than I do and always has. Mine are built and hers just arrive. I sat down and ate with them, and told her a did-you-know about the Ethiopian calendar being seven years behind everybody else's, because they counted the start of things differently and never saw a reason to stop. She said that was a good one, and asked if that meant she was thirty-one in Ethiopia, and I said it did, and she said she'd think about moving.

It was a good evening. There were a lot of good evenings and I was there for all of them.

---

At one in the morning I was at the kitchen table with the laptop, because of course I was.

I wasn't thinking about Tresidder. I was thinking about Pieter's face, and the pause, and a thing I'd read years ago and filed under interesting-wrong: that the easiest way to hide something is to give it a boring name and a boring reason and put it on a list nobody reads.

Two options, I told the dog, who was asleep and did not care.

One. It's exactly what Pieter said. An old diagnostic job that somebody forgot to document, sending its little report into the void every night, like a dog waiting at a door for a man who isn't coming home. Sad, boring, common as dirt. I've seen fifty of them.

Two. Somebody knows exactly what it is, and has told Pieter what to say.

I'm a reasonable man. I gave option one eighty-five percent, out of habit, and then I looked at that number and thought about where I'd last used it.

Then I did a small thing, the kind you can walk back from. I opened a ticket. Low priority, plain language. *Unowned scheduled job `rcl-agg`: request owner and purpose for the inventory.* It came back as number 41790. I assigned it to nobody, which is how you assign a thing to the system itself, and left it in the queue where the dull tickets go to be ignored for a year.

Before I went up I sent the man in Groningen his three hundred and forty euros. I hadn't told Ana the price. I'd noticed myself not telling her.

---

Three weeks later Pieter stopped at my desk on his way to something else and said, "Continuity want you. The program. They asked for the person who reads things."

I said thank you. It's what you say. I was on the stairs before I noticed that I hadn't asked who *they* were, or who had told them that I read things.

By then I knew a few things Pieter didn't know I knew, because I hadn't left it. I'd said my eleven words in a glass box and been told to leave it by a man whose one sentence about it was false, and I know exactly one honest way to find out what a job is when the paperwork won't tell you. You don't open it and you don't stop it. You stand next to it while it runs, and you watch what comes out.

I'd started that same Friday night.
