# The sheets

A sheet looked like this. I'm going to show you one, because I made two hundred and ninety of them in thirteen days and they were all the same shape, and the shape is the argument.

At the top, a name. Then four short sections, with a line between each.

**What you were told.** One sentence, in the words from the phone. *The nightly job is a security honeypot; leave it in place.* Or: *Certain editions are tracked for conservation; please telephone if asked for.* Or: *Your cellar holds heritage material; access is restricted for its protection.*

**What your own paper did.** Their records, not mine. The ticket they'd closed, with its number and date. The call they'd logged. The invoice line they'd approved, with their initials. The access they'd granted. Taken from systems they had the right to look in and never had, and printed with the reference numbers, so they could check. Beside it, one line from the schedule, their line: the date their ticket closed or their call was made, set against the date on the schedule it had moved, and the address, and in my handwriting one word for what the public record said had happened there. *Heart. Sold. Fire. Quiet.*

**Who is kept.** The seat list, for the four companies and ours. Names, dependents per allocation, the line about animals. Printed small, two columns, a page and a half. And at the top of it the only sentence on the whole sheet that was mine:

*Your name is not on this list. Please check.*

**Your desk.** This box was empty on most sheets, because most people had no desk. For the ones who did, it had four lines. Our annex, their desk number, their name. The shipping firm's annex, the same desk number, somebody else's name. The hospital. The energy company. Desk twelve, four times.

That was it. No theory. Nothing about books or sentences or what happens when we die. I'd learned that from Frank in my own kitchen: don't give them the story, give them the invoice. A sheet made no claim they could argue with. Here is what you were told, here is what you did, here is who's kept, here is who else has your chair, and the space between the boxes was theirs to fall into.

Two hundred and ninety. Thirteen days, the seventh to the nineteenth of December. Ana drove.

One more thing, and I'll say it once because I said it to myself every morning. The job at 02:14 counts people who ask and people who are told. Every person I handed a sheet to was a row the next night, if they weren't one already. I was doing to two hundred and ninety people the thing that had been done to me in June, and I knew it, and I did it anyway, because the alternative was to leave them in the dark where the counting doesn't matter. I'd like that written down against my name as well.

Pieter first, because he had to be, and because I owed it to him.

I did it at his desk on the Monday, after seven, when the floor was empty and the cleaners had gone through. He was still there because he's always still there; Pieter works late the way other men drink, steadily and without enjoyment. I put the sheet on his keyboard and sat down in the chair beside his desk, the one people sit in when they've come to resign, and I didn't say anything.

He read it. I watched him read the first box and recognize the sentence, because it was his, from a meeting I'd never been in, and I watched his ears go red. I watched him read the second and nod, because the ticket was mine, #41790, closed by him as a duplicate of #41822, which hadn't existed when I opened mine, and the date was right, and the closing note was his. He read the word in the margin, which for his line was *fire*.

Then he turned the page and found the list, and his own name was on it, which I'd left there on purpose, and I watched his face start to clear, and then I watched him read the number beside it.

*Dependents per allocation: 3.*

"I've got two," he said. It came out very quietly. "Two kids. Lotte and Bas. And Marieke. That's three." He looked up. "That's three with Marieke."

"Yes."

"So that's us. That's all of us. Why does it say three like it's a—" He couldn't find the word. I had it; I'd had it since the autumn. *Allocation.* I didn't give it to him. He'd get there.

He read the names around his. He knew most of them. Heads of department. Two board members. Anneke. The forty from the drill, and the people he reported to and the people they reported to, and nobody, not one person, from the floor he managed. Not Frank. Not the six in the glass box. Not the woman at the next desk who'd been at the bank twenty-two years.

"My team's not on it."

"No. You are. Because you said *leave it.* That's what the seat's for, Pieter. It's not a reward. It's a retainer."

Then he got to the last box, and I watched him read his own desk number four times.

"Who's this?" His finger was on the second line. "Rotterdam. Who's he?"

"Somebody who was told the same thing you were, by somebody with the same job as Julian."

"He's got my desk."

"You've got his. There's one floor. I've sat on it."

He sat there for a long time with his finger on the number three. Then he said, in a voice I'd never heard him use, "What do I do," and I said the thing I'd decided to say to all of them, because it was true and because it was the only instruction that couldn't be reported upward as a threat.

"Nothing. That's the point. Next time, don't."

I left him there. The next morning he said good morning to me in the glass box exactly as he always had, and I thought I'd lost him, and then, four days later, a ticket I'd opened against page 212 as a test came back not closed. Not actioned either. Just open. Sitting there. For the first time in eleven years Pieter had failed to tidy something, and I knew what it had cost him, and I said nothing, and neither did he.

Saskia I did on the Tuesday, at her desk, in daylight, because she's a woman who distrusts anything that happens after six.

Her sheet was short. She'd been told nothing; she wasn't on the helper list at all. What she had was the grant log, which is hers the way the visitor book is Aylin's, and in it one line she hadn't written. My program role, ticked on the eighteenth of June, by a service account, three weeks before anybody had asked for me.

She'd told me about that line herself in the autumn, with a sigh, and then she'd filed it under the things that are wrong in a bank and don't matter. Now it was on a page beside a schedule.

"I didn't write this," she said.

"I know. That's why it's on your sheet. Everything else on there is something somebody did. This is the one thing nobody did."

She looked at it for a while. Saskia owns the access reviews; a grant she didn't make is, to her, a stain on a tablecloth. She'd already written me up once, in the summer, two words in her log, *seemed tired*, and I think she thought about doing it again. She'd been told the rule on page 212 cut down the noise, and nobody had ever said the word honeypot to her, which is why she wasn't on the list: they hadn't needed to tell her anything. She'd done her job. Then she turned to her screen and typed for a minute and wrote something on the back of my sheet and turned it round.

A service account name. The system it belonged to. The vendor whose address it was registered to, which was the address on the invoice, which was the address that had fixed my access in eleven minutes.

"That's who ticked you," she said. "I'd like it back when you're done with it. It's mine."

The dealer in Groningen I did by post, because he'd earned the courtesy of not having me in his shop again. I put a note in with it. *You asked who sent me. Nobody. This is what the number in your ledger does.* Two weeks later a parcel came. It was the ledger, cloth spine, fourteen years of his handwriting, with the telephone number cut out of the back cover with a scalpel, cleanly, the way a conservator would do it, and a card that said, in the neat old hand, *I find I no longer need it.* The receipt from 2011 was still in it, marked *Second impression*, which he'd left for me to find.

The professor's neighbor in Deventer got hers through the door. I don't know how it landed. I know she didn't ring anyone, because the watcher would have told me what happened next, and nothing did.

Marijke I took by hand, on the Wednesday, with Buddy, because she'd opened her door to a man with an old dog once and I wasn't going to send her an envelope.

Her sheet had almost nothing on it. She'd been told *distracted*, by a policeman and a minister, and that was in the first box in their words. The second box was one line from the schedule, her address, *pension*, and the date. And clipped to the sheet, the one photograph I'd printed for anyone: a man asleep at my kitchen table with his mouth a little open, a blanket over his shoulders, and on the shelf behind his head, between the cookbooks, a steel flask.

She looked at the photograph for a long time at her kitchen table, with Buddy under it, and the flask's twin not there, because she'd given it to me.

"That's him," she said. "He's younger than I thought. Henk said he was about fifty."

"He's forty-four."

"Henk always made people older." She put the photograph face down and then turned it back up again. "Is he asleep?"

"Yes."

"Did you do that?"

"My wife did."

She nodded, slowly, as if that was the correct answer. "Good," she said. "Tell her from me."

So she got hers, in the curtain shop, between a customer and a delivery, and she read it standing up with her reading glasses on, and the first box said *conservation*, because the foundation had rung her in March about her father's papers, kindly, and she had let them photograph the notebook.

"Everybody let them in. That's what the word's for."

She folded it in four and put it in her apron. "He'd have liked this," she said. "A sheet. He'd have said it was the only honest thing anyone's done since." She looked at the list. "His name's not on it either."

"No."

"Good. He'd have hated the company."

Frank's I gave him in his own kitchen, with Mora under the table, because Buddy can't do his stairs, and because it was the one sheet I was ashamed of.

He wasn't on any list. He'd done nothing. What he had was one word against his name in the honeypot dozen, because he'd been in the room when Pieter said it, and he'd repeated it to me on these stairs in the spring.

He read it and laughed. "I'm a helper."

"You're on the list."

"For saying *honeypot* on a staircase."

"For saying it to me. That's what a helper is, Frank. Somebody who passes a word along without checking it." I didn't enjoy saying it. "I did the same thing at a funeral once."

He looked at the seat list for a while, the way he looks at a dashboard he doesn't trust, top to bottom and then back up the right-hand side.

"Pieter's got three," he said. "Who's the third?"

"Marieke."

"They counted his *wife*?" He put the sheet down. "Okay. Fine. I still don't believe any of it. But that's an insane spreadsheet and I'd like a copy."

Beard Man I did on the Sunday, at the stall, because that's where he is on Sundays and I wasn't going to ask for his address and ruin something.

He was mid-sentence. He's always mid-sentence. I put the sheet down on a box of Reader's Digest condensed books and he read it without stopping talking, which I've seen him do with a newspaper, and then he stopped talking, which I'd never seen.

The first box said *conservation*. The second was a telephone number in a ledger he'd never kept, because the polite man who came round in the spring had asked him to ring if anyone wanted a single title from the professor's lot, and he'd said he would, and he hadn't, because he'd sold the whole lot to me in two crates on a Sunday morning before the van came, to save himself the trouble.

"I was supposed to ring," he said.

"Yes."

"I never ring anyone. I've got a theory about that." He did. It took eleven minutes and had a comet in it. The short version was that the universe divides people into those who ring and those who get rung, and that the ones who get rung are the ones who've got something, and that he'd sold me everything at once because somewhere in him he'd known that a man who wanted a single title was a man who wanted it for someone else. "Nobody buys one book," he said. "Not off the ground. You buy the lot or you're working."

Ninety percent nonsense. I've said that before. But he'd sold me the thin book to avoid a phone call, and it was the only mistake they'd made in the whole east of the country, and he'd made it out of laziness, and I stood there in the market with the sheet on the condensed books and thought: that's the hole in it. That's the whole hole. A helper who couldn't be bothered.

"Did I do something?" he said.

"You didn't ring."

He thought about that. "I'll put that on my stone," he said, and went back to the comet.

The brewery man went to them.

I knew somebody would. I'd told Ana one in ten would, and it was closer to one in twenty, and he was the first, and I want to describe him because he wasn't a bad man.

He had a family firm in a small town, four generations, with a cellar under the brewhouse that went back to the seventeenth century, and twenty years ago a foundation had come to him with a grant and a request: the cellar was heritage, it was dry and cool and stable, would he allow part of it to be used for the storage of archival material, under seal. He'd said yes. He'd have said yes to anyone with a grant and a kind word about his great-grandfather. His sheet said *heritage*, and his record was a key register with his signature on it four times a year, and his last box was the full one: five seats in our annex under a V-number, dependents four, and the same desk in three other annexes under three other names.

He read it standing in his own yard with the smell of malt coming off the brewhouse. He read it carefully, all of it. He read the last box twice. Then he looked up with an expression I'd expected to be anger and wasn't.

"You've got this wrong," he said.

"The desk number's from your annex. I reconciled it."

"I don't care about the desk number." He tapped the box. "These three. They've been told they're in. They're not in. I'm in." He said it the way you'd correct somebody's spelling. "I've met the people. I've been to the building. There was a dinner. You've been given a list by someone who wants you to think it's all the same, and you've believed him because you're the kind who believes paper. You don't get it. I'm *in*."

"Four people have been to that dinner."

"Then four people think they're in and one of them is." He folded the sheet and handed it back to me, which nobody else did. "Thank you for coming out. I'll let them know you've been."

He rang them that afternoon. I know because of what happened on the Friday. And I want to say this about him: he wasn't betraying me. He was doing what twenty years of *heritage* had taught him to do with something strange in his yard, which was ring the number. He was being a good helper, one last time. It was the last time, because the people he rang could not explain the sheet to him either, and he'd run a business for forty years and knew what it sounded like when somebody couldn't explain an invoice.

Anneke de Wit I'd left till the Wednesday of the second week, because she was the one I was afraid of, and because her sheet was the longest.

Her record wasn't a ticket or a phone call. It was thirty quarterly approvals of a line that said *continuity reconciliation (recall index), per flagged person*, with her initials against each, going back to the day she'd got the job. Her sheet had all thirty, in a column. Beside the schedule's eleven addresses, what the public record said: the care home, the sale, the canal, the four that had gone quiet, checked by my own watcher against the family announcements and the house listings, nothing I'd been handed. And one more page nobody else got: seven nights of the reason column from the job at 02:14, one week in October, with the employee numbers blacked out and only the two words left standing in each row. *Asked (job). Wrote (dealer). Posted (forum). Asked (job).* Forty rows, so she could see what a flagged person was, and what kind of thing gets you flagged, and that it is nothing.

I asked for a meeting through her assistant, under the program's name, and I got twenty minutes, and I walked in and put the sheet on her desk and sat down, and she read it, and she did not say a word for eleven minutes. I counted. She read the invoice page, the one with her initials on it thirty times, and she turned it over and read the back, which was blank, and turned it back and read it again, and I understood that she was doing what I do. She was reading the middle. She was looking for the place where her own signature stopped being a formality and started being a thing.

Then she read the reason column, and something happened to her face that I didn't understand until a Monday morning on her step.

When she spoke, it was the first time I'd heard her voice.

"Thirty," she said.

"Thirty."

"I approved this thirty times." She looked at the red cell. "This is you."

"That's me. The week I opened a ticket."

She looked at me with the face from the reading room: not hostile, not frightened, assessing, the face of a woman whose job is to look at a thing and say what it will cost.

"You were in that room in December," she said. "He read your referral out loud. I thought that was for you. It was for me."

"Yes."

"I've been on the foundation's board for four years. I was asked to join because the bank funds it and someone from the bank should sit there. I've sat there. I've approved the minutes. I've never once been told what the reconciliation line reconciles." She stopped. "That's not an excuse. I'm head of risk. It's the opposite of an excuse."

"I'm not here for an excuse."

"What are you here for?"

"For you to read the rest. There's a schedule. There are four other companies and one floor of desks. There are two hundred and ninety people in this region who were each told one small thing and the word continuity, and not one of them is on the list you're on. I've given them their sheets. I've got eleven left, and they're the board. Yours." I looked at her. "I'd like you to read the rest before you sign next quarter's schedule, because I'd like you to know what a visit is before you sign for one."

"All of it?"

"All of it. Tonight. Here." She looked at the reason column one more time, and her hand went to the second page and stopped. "I've approved thirty of these without reading. I'm going to read one."

And Aylin.

I'd nearly taken her off the list, because she was the one I liked and because her sheet was the thinnest: a woman who sat at a desk in a reading room and kept a card index and booked appointments for dead men. Her word was *heritage*. Her record was the catalog, and on the sheet I'd put the one line in it that was hers and recent: a request slip dated the second of December, in her handwriting, for a shelfmark I'd written out on the reading-room table, marked *in conservation*. I'd seen the item on its shelf through the glass door at the drill. So had she, every working day for nine years.

I gave it to her on the Thursday, the nineteenth, at the desk, when I came up the stairs for the appointment she'd booked me into without my asking.

She read it. She read it the way she did everything, calmly, like a woman who's seen a lot of paper. When she got to her own handwriting she stopped.

"He told me to write that," she said. "In front of you. He said it was in conservation and I wrote it, because he's the head, and I know exactly where it is, and it's sound." She looked at the slip. "I've never written a false status on a card in my life, Mr Klippe. Not one. I wrote that one with you watching."

I said I knew.

"No. You don't." She got up. She went to the card index, the wooden one, and she didn't open a drawer. She reached behind it and took out a book. A big one, bound, with a ribbon, and she brought it to the desk and opened it and turned it round to face me.

It was the visitor book. Every person who had ever sat at the long table, in her handwriting and the handwriting of whoever had kept it before her, going back, when I turned to the front, to 1931. Name. Date. Shelfmark requested. And in a final column, headed *Outcome*, a word.

I read down the page she'd opened it at. The professor was there, in March, with the shelfmark that was on the last page of his thin book, and the word in the last column was *Deceased*. Above him, a name I didn't know and the word *Seated*. Above that, *Quiet*. *Quiet*. *Seated*. *Deceased*. *Declined*. *Quiet*.

And at the bottom, in December, in Aylin's hand: my name. The date. The shelfmark, because I had asked for one. And in the outcome column, one word.

*Pending.*

"I keep the book," said Aylin. "That's what I conserve. I've kept it for nine years and I've never been told what the last column means and I've never asked, because I was told it was heritage, and because it's very old, and because somebody has to." She looked at the sheet, still on the desk. "You've just told me what it means."

"I didn't tell you anything. I gave you a list."

"You gave me a list I'm not on." She closed the visitor book. "Copy it. Not now. He's upstairs. Come at midnight on Sunday; the door's on my badge. Copy it and give it to whoever you're giving things to." She looked at the slip on my sheet, in her own hand. "And bring that. I'd like to correct a card."

And the forum, which I did last, on the Thursday night, from the kitchen floor.

I didn't post a sheet. A sheet needs a name, and the forum is where people go not to have one. What I posted was a line. Half of one.

*Looking for anyone who's ever heard this said, anywhere, by anyone. Not read it. Heard it. "Don't be afraid. Hold on to"*

And nothing more. No question mark. I'd learned at least that much from a reading room: you don't argue, you ask.

I didn't think it would work. The forum doesn't finish sentences; it corrects them. By the morning there were two replies, one of which was about bloodlines, which I'd expected. By Friday night there were nine more, and I read them with Buddy's head on my foot and my own hand under my leg, which I noticed, and left there.

They finished it. Not all of them the same. *Hold on to whoever's got your hand.* *Hold on to the one holding you.* *Hold on to whoever is holding your hand*, twice, word for word. A man whose grandfather had said it in a hospital in Groningen in 1988. A woman whose mother was an archivist and had said it to her, once, drunk, and never again. A bridge. A beach. A corridor, which I read three times. A kitchen in Hengelo. Eleven people, in a week, who'd heard it said, by someone who was dying, to someone who wasn't, and had never found anyone else who had, and had filed it where you file that.

Henk's version was in my coat. *Said not to be frightened, only to hold on.* I put the notebook on the floor next to the laptop and read the two together, and then I read Els's father's line, which Beard Man had been improving for years. *The ones who held on come out together.* Three versions and eleven strangers, and none of them had met.

That's a challenge and a response. That's what you do at work when you think two systems are reading the same clock: you send half a thing to one and see if the other knows the end. I'd sent half a sentence to the internet and the internet had given me the rest, eleven times.

The watcher ran at 02:14. In the morning it showed me two of the eleven replies with a sentence added each, in a voice that wasn't theirs, about who really controls the hospitals. By Saturday one of the two had deleted his account.

So the eleven were counted now too. I'd done that. I sat on the floor and wrote down the eleven handles in the notebook under Henk, because a copy is the only thing I know how to make, and then I wrote the whole sentence out once, in full, and looked at it, and put the notebook back in my coat.
