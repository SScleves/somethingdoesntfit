# Frank's kitchen

Frank asked me round on a Saturday, which should have told me something, because Frank is twenty-nine and single and lives in a flat where the kitchen is also the living room and the bedroom is a mezzanine, and men like that don't ask colleagues round. They ask *friends* round. I'd apparently crossed a line without noticing, the way I cross most lines.

"Beer," he said, at the door. "And I need you to look at something. Properly. With the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely have a face. Pieter calls it the audit face." He looked down. "You brought the horse."

I'd brought Mora. Frank lives up three flights and Buddy doesn't do one. She went past him into the flat, found the table, got under it, and began a slow negotiation with something that had fallen there during an earlier period of Frank's life.

I'd also brought a sheet of paper, folded in my jacket, and that was the real reason I'd said yes. On Monday I was moving to continuity. The job I'd asked about in June had a new name, an owner and a ticket older than my question, and the only thing left on earth that said otherwise was a screenshot I'd taken on a Thursday without knowing why. I wanted somebody to try to kill it. Not Ana, who'd be kind. Somebody who'd enjoy it.

There was a television on with the sound down. A hearing, somewhere, men in suits behind a long table, a caption with the word *phenomena* in it. Frank had it on the way you have the weather on. Neither of us looked at it all evening.

---

He went first. It was his kitchen.

The warm-up was flat earth, which a cousin of his had gone in for, and which took about as long as it deserves. Ships go down over the horizon hull first. The shadow on the moon in an eclipse is always round, and only one shape throws a round shadow from every side. "Also," I said, "it's four in the morning in Tokyo right now. Ring someone." He ticked something on his laptop.

The second thing was a video.

Somebody had sent it to him, somebody who sends him things, and he wanted it killed and he wanted to watch me do it, and I'll admit the persona enjoyed that. It was a montage. Dates of earthquakes against phases of the moon, with a voice over it that had learned to sound like a documentary and a graph that went up and to the right with the confidence of a man who has never had to show his working.

"Right," I said. "Pause it. Go back. The graph."

He went back.

"It's not a graph. There's no axis. It's a line somebody drew on a picture of a graph. Second thing: what's the baseline? How many earthquakes happen on days that *aren't* full moons? He's never going to tell you, because the answer is *the same number*, and if you've got no baseline you've got no correlation, you've got a list of nights that something happened, and every night something happens." I drank. "Third thing, and this is the one that matters. Look at the dates he picked. He's got fourteen. There were two hundred quakes above magnitude six in that period. He picked the fourteen that fit. That's a man with a drawer full of socks telling you every sock is blue, having thrown the red ones away first."

Frank was grinning. "Go on."

"That's it. It's noise. It's beautiful noise, somebody spent a week on the voice, but you don't need me for it. You need a sock drawer."

"I needed to hear you say it." He killed the video. "Okay. Round three. This one's got a *book.*"

---

Round three was the one about the missing three hundred years.

You might know it. There's a theory, and it has a book, and the book has footnotes, and the footnotes are what get you, because they look like the real thing. The claim is that a stretch of the early Middle Ages never happened: that a few hundred years were inserted into the calendar by people with reasons, and that's why there's so little from that period, and that's why a certain emperor seems to have done too much in one lifetime.

"I don't believe it," Frank said. "Obviously. But I can't *kill* it. Every time I go to kill it the footnotes come up."

"Tree rings," I said.

"What?"

"You don't need the footnotes. Trees. There's a continuous record of tree rings going back twelve thousand years, overlapping, logs out of bogs matched to logs out of roofs matched to living oaks. Year by year. No gaps. If somebody had inserted three hundred years into the calendar, there'd be three hundred years of rings missing, and there aren't. The trees don't know what year the pope said it was." I drank. "Also the Chinese kept their own calendar and their own eclipses and never got the memo. Astronomers can run the sky backward; you can't fake where the moon was. It dies on tree rings in four minutes."

Frank looked at me for a while.

"Then why is there a documentary?"

And that's the thing about Frank. He doesn't want to be told a thing is stupid. He wants to be told *why someone made a film about it.*

"I don't know," I said. And then, because it was Frank's kitchen and the beer was good and I'd had a bad two weeks: "Here's what I notice, for what it's worth. A theory that dies on oak needs a footnote. Somebody gave it drone shots. When I see an explanation working harder than the mystery, I notice. When I see the *ridicule* working harder than the mystery, I notice that too."

"You think it's a plant."

"I think it's a sock drawer. People get shown the blue ones so that when a red one turns up they say *oh, that's one of those.*" I shrugged. "Or a man with a book needed a second mortgage. It's usually that."

Under the table Mora sneezed, twice, at whatever she'd won.

---

Then he showed me how he sleeps.

He has a list. I should have guessed; he's that kind of mind. On his laptop, a document, years old, headed *Things people believe* and under it two columns: *Still standing* and *Dead.* He's kept it since university. When he hears a claim he puts it in the left column, and when it dies he moves it to the right, with the date and the reason, and he's never once moved anything back.

"Everything goes in. Everything gets a trial. Crashed saucers, dead: the metal was pots and pans, there's a magazine article from 1952. The leaked government documents from the eighties, dead: wrong typewriter, the signature was traced off a letter from 1947. The man who said he'd worked on the alien craft, dead: he lied about his degrees. The water memory thing, dead: a magician was in the room when they ran it blind." He scrolled. It was a long document. "And here's what I've got for your lot. The believers. You know what the single most damaging thing is, in the whole history of it?"

"Tell me."

He turned the laptop round. A scanned page, declassified, 1953, a panel of scientists convened by people who had reasons.

"They *recommended* it," Frank said, with real pleasure. "The debunking. They sat in a room for four days and wrote, I'm quoting, that the subject should be stripped of its special status and its aura of mystery, through a program of, hang on, *training and debunking*, using mass media, psychologists, and, I love this, Disney. Walt Disney. It's not a conspiracy, James. It's a *memo.* Whether anyone ever ran the program is disputed, I'll give you that for free. But every time one of your forum people says they're being laughed at on purpose, somebody did once write that down as a plan, and it's the most bureaucratic thing I've ever read. That's my trump card. It's not sinister. It's a committee."

I read the page. I took my time, because I take my time, and because I'd seen something, and I wanted to make sure it was there before I said anything, and it was.

"Read the next bit," I said. "Under the part you read. Out loud."

He turned the laptop back round. He read it. He stopped halfway and read it again, and I watched his face do the thing mine does when I put a finger on the right line of page 118.

"'The burden of proof is on the sighter,'" Frank said, slowly, "'not the explainer.'"

"Yeah."

"That's the opposite of science."

"It's a *rule.* Written down, by scientists, in a government document. Whoever sees the thing has to prove it, and whoever explains it away doesn't. That's a committee writing a standard of proof, Frank. And you've been using it for ten years, in that list, and so have I. I'm not saying the saucers are real. I think the saucers are mostly pots and pans. I'm saying somebody wrote down the rule for how we'd be allowed to check, and then it got taught, and then everybody forgot it had been taught."

Frank didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "Huh," and moved his cursor to the left column, and I watched him *not* move the 1953 memo to the right.

"It's still dead," he said. "The saucers."

"I know."

"I'm not moving it."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But the memo's—" He looked at it. "The memo's not in either column, is it. The memo's the *table.*"

I didn't say anything. He'd got there on his own, and I've learned to shut up when that happens.

---

"My turn," I said, and took the sheet out of my jacket.

Two pictures of the same job list, printed one above the other. The top one from June: a job at 02:14 with a name, nine seconds, forty kilobytes, and nothing in the owner column. The bottom one from last week: same minute, same nine seconds, a different name, an owner, and a ticket dated in May.

"Kill it."

Frank looked at it for about as long as he'd looked at flat earth.

"It's a screenshot."

"Yes."

"I can make you a screenshot of Pieter approving my raise. Give me ten minutes and I'll make you one of Pieter in a dress." He held up a finger. "Second. Jobs get renamed. Tickets get backdated; I backdated two on Thursday, because the form won't let you close what you never opened. Third, and this is the one that matters." He was doing me, and doing it well. "Who else has the top picture?"

"Nobody."

"Then it's your picture against the system, and the system has an owner and a ticket, and you have a JPEG." He slid it back across the table. "Dead. Sorry. Is that what's been up with you?"

"It's the part I can carry in a jacket."

"Get a better part."

He was right, and I'd known he would be, which is why I'd brought it to him and not to someone who likes me more. It took him under a minute. Eleven weeks I'd been carrying that picture around like a relic, and a man with a beer had just put it in the *Dead* column without getting up.

"Can I borrow your method?" I said.

I turned the sheet over and drew a line down the middle. Frank's headings were no use to me; I wasn't trying to decide what was true. I wrote *could show a stranger* on the left and *only I have seen* on the right, and I filled it in with my arm round it like a boy in an exam.

Left: two printings of a 1926 book, page 118. Anyone with both copies and a ruler gets what I got. A third copy, with a dealer's receipt.

Right: the screenshot, as of four minutes ago. A capture file from work that I could show a stranger exactly once, on my last day of employment. And the thin book.

That one I looked at for a while. Forty-one entries in two hands, bought off the ground from a man who has never told me his name. Anybody could have written it. I could have written it. I'd been treating it for two months as the middle of everything, and on Frank's kitchen table, in Frank's columns, it was a notebook from a market with a story attached, and the story was mine.

"What's the thin book?" Frank said. He reads upside down too. Everyone does but me.

"A notebook. From the market."

"Whose?"

"That's the problem."

"Right column," Frank said. "Anything with *from the market* in it is right column. I don't make the rules." He considered. "I do make the rules."

---

He walked us down. Mora took the stairs the way she takes everything.

"Was that the 02:14 thing?" he said, behind me. "On your sheet. The one from stand-up."

"Yes."

"You know that's a honeypot, right?"

I stopped on the stair. He was behind me and couldn't see my face, which is the only reason I let it do what it did.

"A what?"

"A trap. Security leaves a thing lying about with no owner and waits to see who pokes it. Pieter told us Thursday. You'd gone down for your new badge." He laughed. "Years it's been sitting there, apparently, and nobody ever asked. Then you poke it at stand-up in front of nine people. You're the only one who passed, mate. That's probably why continuity wanted you. I wasn't meant to say. I'm drunk."

"Pieter told you that."

"Pieter told all of us. He said not to make a thing of it."

I said goodnight. I said it normally. The persona does stairs.

It's a twenty-minute walk and I did the sum on the way, with Mora pulling toward every bin. Pieter had now described that job three ways. In June, to my face: left over from the migration. Two weeks ago: continuity's, which is you. On Thursday, to nine people, once I was out of the room: a security trap. They couldn't all be right, and the last one I could test from where I stood, because I'd read what the job sends. A security trap watches who touches the trap. It doesn't keep a row that says a man wrote to a book dealer eleven days before he touched anything. Security doesn't read my post.

So either Pieter had lied to the team, or somebody had told Pieter that and he'd passed it on. I gave the second one better odds. Pieter is a bad liar. He pauses. And *honeypot* is a good thing to be told: small, flattering to nobody, and it explains why you shouldn't ask.

I'd also shown a colleague that I kept a picture of the old name, and Frank repeats things on staircases. I'd known that when I took it out of my jacket.

That left the right-hand column. The thin book would stay there until somebody who wasn't me said whose it was, and I could think of one person alive who might. Beard Man had mentioned her once, in passing, between a comet and a prophet. The family kept the furniture. There was a daughter.

Mora ate something off the pavement outside the kebab place, and I let her, which tells you where my head was.
