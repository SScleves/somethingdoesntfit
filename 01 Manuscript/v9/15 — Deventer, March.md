# Deventer. A Thursday in March.

The old man had written to them on paper, with a stamp, to an address that was a foundation's registered office and nothing else. Two pages, in a hand that was not what it had been. Most of it was argument. The last four lines gave a shelfmark, asked to consult the item, and asked whether, before that, somebody might come and talk to him about his notebook.

So somebody came.

He parked two streets away, because he always did, and walked, because the walk told him things: the bin at the gate, not yet out; the curtains, good ones, chosen by a woman; the bicycle with a child's seat on it that belonged to a neighbor. He rang. He waited longer than a younger man would have waited. The door opened on a face that had been expecting him for a long time and had not expected him to look like this.

"You're not what I thought," the professor said.

"People say that." He wiped his feet. "May I?"

The chair was blue and the professor lowered himself into it the way you lower something you're afraid of dropping. The notebook was on the side table, closed, under the man's hand, and the man did not look at it and did not look away from it, and the professor noticed both, and smiled.

"Tea?"

"Let me," the man said. "You sit."

He made tea in a kitchen that had a woman's handwriting on the calendar and a man's on the shopping list. He brought it through. He sat where he was told to sit, which was the chair opposite, the one with the better light on it, and he understood that he had been placed, and he let it happen, because there was no harm in it.

"I'll say what I've got," the professor said, "and you'll say nothing, and then you'll go. That's how it works, isn't it?"

"More or less."

"Forty-one," the professor said. "My grandfather found the first eleven. I found the rest. I've never published. I've never told anyone but my daughter that the book exists, and I told her it was a bibliography, which it is." His hand on the cover. "I'm not asking you to confirm anything. I'm old and I'm not sleeping and I'd like, before I stop, to have asked somebody. That's all."

The man drank his tea.

"They're all the same edit," the professor said. "Did you know that? I expect you know that. Forty-one books and it's always the same correction, in different clothes. A hand comes out. A giver goes. *Early* becomes *first*. Nobody ever adds anything. You only ever take one thing away." He was looking at the window now. "It's very disciplined. I've admired it, God help me. Thirty years I've admired it."

"You wrote us two pages of that," the man said. "Nobody answers that part. You also asked to consult an item, and you gave the shelfmark."

"A student of mine saw it on a crate. I've had it nine years."

"The answer to that part is yes. The room sits once a year. The second of December. You'd come in person and ask at the desk."

"December." The professor looked at his own hand for a while. "You people have a sense of humor."

"It's the date there is."

The professor opened the notebook at the last page, where the reference already was, and wrote the date under it, slowly, and one word under the date, and closed it and put his hand back on the cover.

"There," he said. "I wanted to have done it."

"You should rest," the man said, gently.

"Is that what you came to say?"

"I came because you asked." He put his cup down. "Nobody comes if you don't. That's the only rule I know for certain."

The professor laughed, which turned into something else, and he waited it out with his hand over his mouth, and then he said, "My daughter's going to be alright. Tell me that, at least. You can say that."

"Your daughter's going to be alright."

"You say it like you mean it."

"I do mean it." He stood. He took the two cups to the kitchen and rinsed them and left them on the rack, the way a guest does. When he came back the professor's eyes were closed and his hand was still on the notebook.

He lifted the hand, which weighed very little, and took the notebook to the window, and photographed every page, flat and square and without shadow, the way he had been taught. Then he put it back and put the hand back on it. The hand closed. He stood for a moment in the good light looking at an old man asleep in a blue chair, and then he let himself out, and pulled the door until it clicked, and walked the two streets to the car.

He made one call. "He's asked. Yes. In person. He's been given December; he won't be there. I have the pages, all of them. The original's on the side table. I'd say weeks." A pause. "I'd rather wait. A body's a fingerprint, you've said so yourself." Another pause, longer. "Understood."

*

Eleven days later the bin was not put out on a Monday and the daughter let herself in with her key.

*

He came back for the furniture in June, with a van and a man he paid in cash, because the daughter had put a card in the window and the card had been reported to him and it was cleaner to be the buyer than to be anything else. He bought the shelves and the blue chair and he did not haggle, and when he asked about the dresser she said no, that one was her mother's, she was keeping that, and he said of course, and the daughter, who had her father's hands, said she was glad the shelves were going to somebody who wanted them.

"And the books?" he said, lightly, as he had planned to say it.

"Oh, those went," she said. "Weeks ago. Man from the market. He takes anything, by the crate. I couldn't look at them."

He stood in the emptied room and let his face do nothing at all.

"Of course," he said. "That's the best thing for them."

Before he left the street he rang the bell of the neighbor with the bicycle and gave her a card with a foundation's name on it and a telephone number. The professor's papers were of some interest, he said, and the foundation was concerned for their conservation; if anybody came asking after them she would be doing the old man a kindness to ring. She asked how Hendrik had seemed to them, at the end. He said the professor had written some rather confused letters, and had not been sleeping, and that everybody had been very fond of him. She said that was just what she had thought herself.

He drove the chair and the shelves to a lock-up near Zwolle, and he went through the chair, the lining and the cushions and the frame, properly, and found nothing, and the following Sunday he went to the market in a good coat and stood in the cold at a stall where a man with a beard was talking about a comet, and he looked at the ground, where the crates were, and there were two, and they were full of Reader's Digest.

"Professor's books," he said. "Were there any left?"

"Sold the lot," Beard Man said, not unkindly, mid-sentence. "One chap. A fellow left me a number, said to ring if anyone asked after one title in particular. Nobody asked after one. He wanted all of them. I don't ring for a lot."

He thanked him. He bought a condensed Hemingway for fifty cents, because a man who buys nothing is remembered. And he went and sat in the car, and he did not call anybody for eleven minutes, and then he did.

*He was not blamed. It had been a question of weeks, and he had been right to wait. His standing was confirmed at the review. He had the photographs. The only thing that changed was that the original was now in a stranger's kitchen, and he would have to find the chap who'd taken the lot, and he had always, in his way, enjoyed that part.*
