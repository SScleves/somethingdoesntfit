# A market, Sunday

The market was the one James went to, which Kalder knew, and which was the reason he had put it off for five weeks.

He'd been living in a rented room over a bicycle shop two towns east, under a name that was not his and not the last one either. He'd cut his hair. He'd let his beard come in, gray at the chin, which he hadn't expected and didn't mind. He paid cash. He ran in the mornings, along the water, not too far, not too fast. He had been careful in all the ways he had been trained to be careful, and in two or three he had invented, and he was good at it. He had always been good at things.

But the grips were going. He'd noticed it in the kitchen on the Friday, turning the seven iron in his hands the way other men turn a glass, and the leather had gone shiny under his right thumb where it always went first. There were three men in the country he'd let regrip them. One was dead. One had a shop in Amsterdam he couldn't go near. The third was an old man with a folding table at a Sunday market in the east, between a cheese van and a man who sold books off the ground, and he'd been going to him for nine years, and he'd said so, at a dinner, to a man who remembered paper.

He knew that. He went anyway. He told himself it was because the grips mattered, and that was true, and it was also true that he wanted to see the stall again, and the square, and possibly, from a distance, a gray dog in a cart.

He parked three streets away and walked in with the bag on his shoulder, because you don't leave those in a car.

---

The old man with the folding table was glad to see him and didn't ask about the beard. He took the seven iron, turned it, ran his thumb where Kalder's thumb went, and said what he always said, which was that the leather was good leather and the problem was the owner. He said it would take an hour and Kalder said he'd walk.

He bought a coffee from the van and stood with it by the cheese, because from there he could see both ends of the square, and because the cheese man talked about the weather in a dialect he enjoyed.

He saw the first one at ten past eleven.

A man in a good coat at the flower stall, choosing tulips. Mid-fifties, a scarf, a face Kalder knew from a room in Leiden with a long table, nine years ago. A man who had once told him, kindly, that the hardest part of the work was the paperwork. The man did not look at him. The man was very interested in tulips.

Kalder turned, unhurried, the way you'd turn to check the time on the church, and saw the second.

A woman by the bakery van. Red hat. He'd sat next to her at a dinner in Antwerp, the one where the brewer had been so pleased. She had a paper bag in one hand and nothing in the other, and she was not looking at him either.

He finished his coffee.

There was a third by the church steps, and probably a fourth behind him where he couldn't see without making it obvious that he was looking, and he didn't look. He stood by the cheese and did the arithmetic, the way he'd been taught, and it came out the way it does when people who know the method use it on you. He'd said *three men in the country* at a table, and the man at the table had written everything down for a year. They hadn't found him. They'd found the bag. They'd waited at the three places the bag could go, and one of them was dead, and one was in Amsterdam, and here he was in the east with a coffee, exactly where a man who loved his clubs would be.

He almost admired it. He'd have done the same.

Across the square, at the far end, a man with a beard was mid-sentence at a stall of books on the ground, and in front of him, with a phone in his hand and an old gray dog sitting against his leg, was James.

Not coming over. Not hiding. Standing where Kalder could see him, which was a kindness, or a message, or both. The dog had seen him too. The dog had not stopped looking since.

His phone rang. The old one, the one he'd kept in the lining of the jacket for no reason he'd ever said out loud. He'd switched it on that morning to look at something. He hadn't told himself what.

He looked at the screen and almost laughed.

"James."

"Julian." The market behind the voice, the same market. "You can see them?"

"I can see three."

"I don't know how many there are. I didn't find you. I want you to know that. I'm not that guy. They asked me what you'd carry and I told them, and they asked where it could go and I told them that too. You said it at my table." A pause. "They're much better at it than me."

"They are." The tulip man had bought his tulips. He was standing with them by the fountain. "Why ring? You're forty meters away."

"Because I don't want to be near you. And I wanted to say one thing, and I wanted it to be me."

"Go on."

"You shouldn't have touched my dog."

The cheese man asked if he wanted the usual. He nodded and gave him a note and waved away the change.

"No," he said into the phone. "I shouldn't."

He meant it. Not as an apology; he had never in his life done anything as an apology. As an error. The one entry in nine years he would go back and change. Everything else had been necessary, or clever, or both. The dog had been quick. Quick was the mistake. A man doesn't forgive you a thing like that, and a man who doesn't forgive you remembers what you said about your clubs.

"Is he all right?"

"He lay down on the sixth green on the Monday. Right next to the hole."

Kalder smiled at the fountain. He couldn't help it. "They'll have hated that."

"The greenkeeper pretended not to see."

"Good man."

The red hat had moved. Not closer. Just to a place that was closer to where he would have to walk.

"I liked you," Kalder said. "I'd like that on whatever you're keeping. All of it was work. The golf, the barbecue, Ana. And I liked you anyway. That wasn't the plan."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Yeah," James said. "That's why it was so fucking hard."

Kalder laughed then, properly, the one from the back nine, the one people turned round for. The cheese man glanced up. The tulip man did not.

"Tell Ana it was very good. The pills. I'd have bet on you."

"I'll tell her."

"And James—"

But the line had gone. He looked at the phone. James had hung up on him. Across the square the man put the phone in his pocket and put his hand on the dog's head, and the man with the beard went on talking as if nobody had stopped listening, which perhaps nobody had.

---

The woman in the red hat reached him first. She said his name, the real one, quite warmly, as if they'd run into each other by chance.

"Hello," Kalder said. "Isn't it a beautiful day."

"It is." She looked at the folding table, where the old man was wrapping the seven iron. "We'll wait for those. There's no hurry."

"There isn't?"

"You asked for a seat," she said. "Nine years ago, in a kitchen. It's been kept. A desk on the lower floor, with your name on the card, and a bed. You'll have the generator for company." She smiled. "You'll be the first person who ever actually sat in one. They're quite excited about it upstairs."

He thought about that. A hundred desks, under a reading room, humming.

"Is there a course?"

"No."

"Dogs?"

"Companion animals are not accommodated," she said, and he could tell she'd been looking forward to saying it.

The old man held out the parcel. Kalder took it and gave him a note and waved away the change, and the old man said the leather was good leather, and Kalder said he knew.

He slid the seven iron into the bag, in its place, between the six and the eight. Then he did what he'd done at the start of every round of his life, which was count. Driver. Three wood. Hybrid. Four through nine. Wedges, three. Putter. He touched each head as he went, the way you'd touch the back of a chair in a dark room.

Fourteen. The legal maximum. All there.

He shouldered the bag. The tulip man had come up on his other side, and the third one from the church steps was behind him now, close enough that he could hear him breathing, and across the square an old dog was still watching him go.

"Right," Kalder said. "Lead on."
