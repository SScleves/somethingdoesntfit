# The gorilla mask

Ana wanted a barbecue, and I said yes in about two seconds, which should have warned her.

She'd been asking since September. *You've been nicer since Wednesday golf man, bring him round.* Then October happened and I went back into the submarine and she said it again, less nicely: *bring him round, Mulder, I want to see the person who makes you normal.*

I had a reason and I didn't give it to her. At the pub I'd named a book that doesn't exist and Julian had said he knew it. That's one reading. At work you don't wake anyone up for one reading. You want the same value again, from somewhere else, next to something you know is healthy. So I wanted Julian in a garden with other people in it, and I wanted Frank there, because Frank is the healthiest control I know. He has never once pretended to have read anything.

So: the last Saturday of October, a heater on the patio, coats on. Frank. Gert from two doors down, who is fine, and who turned out to have opinions about my grip. A man from the forum, a lonely one, who'd worked out where I lived from a photograph and turned up with a bottle of wine and theories, and whom Ana, to her enormous credit, fed.

And Julian, with a six-pack of something craft and his flask, because he's a flask man.

Everybody came round the back. That was my doing. Buddy sleeps across the front hall and I didn't want twelve people stepping over him, so I'd sent a message that morning: *back lane, fourth gate, mind the bowl.* Julian came early to help with the ice and I met him at the gate. He looked at the latch, which is the kind that lifts, and at the steel bowl just inside, and asked whose it was.

"Buddy's. He won't drink from the one in the kitchen if he's been out. Don't ask me why."

"I wouldn't dream of asking him," Julian said, and stepped over it carefully, the way you step over something that belongs to somebody.

---

Frank told the gorilla story with tongs. Ana had given him tongs to stop him talking with his hands and it hadn't worked.

"Forty-three," he said. "California. Desert. They're testing the first American jet, properly secret, nobody's supposed to know jets exist. And the test pilots, who are bored, because test pilots are always bored, get fed up with the other pilots at the base radioing in going *what the hell was that, no propeller, going like hell*, right, and the brass going *you saw nothing.* So one of them puts on a gorilla mask. And a bowler hat. And a cigar. And he flies the jet right up alongside some poor bastard in a propeller plane, and *waves.*"

"That's not true," said Ana.

"Every aviation book I've ever read says it's true, which is almost the same thing." Frank turned a sausage. "Point is, the poor bastard lands and goes *I saw a plane with no propeller flown by a gorilla in a hat*, and what happens?"

"They laugh at him."

"They laugh at him *forever.* He's gorilla guy. He was *right*, James. He saw a jet. He was the first man in his squadron to see the future, and he spent the rest of his life being the man who saw a gorilla. And nobody had to deny anything. They just stuck something stupid to the truth and let him carry it." He pointed the tongs at me. "That's your lot. That's the whole technique. You don't hide the plane. You put a gorilla in it."

"Handle with humor," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something I read."

Julian had been by the heater with a beer, quiet, very good at being quiet. "That's a good story, Frank," he said, in the tone of a man who collects them, and Frank beamed, and the sausages burned.

That was when I did it. Frank was still glowing. Julian had come over to top up Ana's glass and was a step and a half behind him.

"There's a book you'd like," I said to Frank. "*The Quiet Presses.* Van der Berg, I think. Dutch, fifties." The same words, in the same order, that I'd used at the pub. "It's all in there. The gorilla, the lot."

"Never heard of it."

"Out of print."

"Sounds made up." He said it happily, to the sausage. "Sounds like a book you'd invent to win an argument."

That's what a healthy system returns. No pause, no nod. Never heard of it, sounds made up, next.

I didn't look at Julian. I looked at Ana's glass. The wine stopped going into it for about as long as it takes to read a line, and then it went on.

So I had my second reading. I also understood, a little late, that a test with a witness is a test the witness gets to watch. He'd heard me run it.

---

I went in for more plates, and somebody had sent me a link. *is this you??* Two question marks. The link was to my own thread.

I should say which thread. In the summer I'd posted the reply I got about page 118 next to the wording in Els's father's folder, paragraph against paragraph, and a woman in Leiden had told me to take it down before it got me visited, and I had. I'd watched it go.

It was back. Under my name, with my photographs, dated the day I'd first posted it. And it was longer. After my last dull sentence there were two more, in my voice. *Almost.* The same dryness, and then a turn at the end, about who had really ordered the second impression, and which old families stood behind the Leiden printing houses. Bloodlines. A word I have never typed in my life.

Nobody had removed anything. They'd added. Two sentences. Enough that anyone who went looking for the man who'd asked about page 118 would find a man who also had views on bloodlines, and would file him, courteously and fully, under the usual thing.

I deleted it again, right there, plates on the counter. Then I posted a short dull note: *A post under this name about page 118 was deleted by me in the summer. Someone else restored it and added two sentences I did not write.*

Eleven people saw it. The forum shows a little number. Eleven. The version with the bloodlines had been seen by six thousand.

That's the arithmetic. I'd spent my life believing that the truth, said plainly, in the right place, would do its own work. Here was the right place and here was the truth and here was the number eleven.

I didn't think it was him. I need to say that. I thought it was a *them*, some pleasant man at a desk with a template. I put the phone away. I took the plates out. Julian looked up from the heater and said, "You alright, Jim? You look like a man whose bank's being difficult," and I laughed, because it was our bit by then, and said, "It's not the bank."

---

He was, I'm going to say it plainly, *wonderful.*

He was good with Gert. He let the forum man talk at him for twenty minutes about the Sphinx and said "huh" in all the right places and never once let his face do what mine was doing. And he was good with Ana. Better than good. He asked her about her grandmother. Not *sorry for your loss*. He asked what she'd been *like*, and Ana told him about the knitting, the undoing and redoing, and he listened the way almost nobody listens, with his whole body turned to her and his beer forgotten, and when she'd finished he said, "She sounds like somebody who knew the doing was the point," and Ana's eyes went bright and she went inside for a minute.

She found me in the kitchen later. "I like him."

"I know."

"You never bring anyone home. Eleven years. You've never once brought a person home that you liked." She put her hand on my chest, flat, the way she does. "He's kind. He's actually kind. Keep him."

---

It was nearly dark when he said it. We were by the fence, the three of us, Frank on his third telling of the gorilla, and the forum man asked where I found my books.

"The market," I said. "A man with a beard."

"Estate sales, mostly, isn't it," Julian said. "That's where the good ones come from. There was that professor's place near Deventer, last spring. A whole house of books."

He said it lightly. He'd said everything lightly all afternoon.

I have never said Deventer to him. Not on the course, not at the pub, not once. I have a face for tables and it held. "Before my time," I said. "I just buy what the man with the beard puts on the ground."

"Lucky man," said Julian, and I didn't know which of us he meant.

I filed it, and I want to be exact about where. Under *he sells to half the east of the country.* Under *estate sales are in the paper.* Under *I ran a test on him an hour ago, and this is what a clever man does when he's been tested: he runs one back.* The third one was the right folder. I put it in all three anyway.

Buddy never went near him.

All afternoon. Buddy goes near everyone, eventually, slowly, paying for every step. It's his whole policy. He went to Gert. He went to the forum man, which was a kindness. Then he lay down in the kitchen doorway and watched Julian for four hours and did not come out, and Julian, who'd asked about him before he asked my name, who'd crouched on a green to say hello, never once tried.

Mora went over once. Sniffed his shoe. Came back. Didn't go again.

"Does it bother you?" I asked him, when I brought him a beer. "He usually does the rounds."

Julian looked at the doorway for a moment. "Nobody's ever asked me that," he said. "People ask why the dog doesn't like *them*." He took the beer. "No. He's allowed."

Old dog, I thought. Tired. Too many people.

---

That night, after the forum man had been put in a taxi with his theories and his empty bottle, Ana fell asleep on my chest at ten and I lay there with one arm dead, looking at the ceiling.

I'd got what I wanted. Two readings and a control. And I knew what it had cost, in one sentence: Julian had now walked my back lane, counted to the fourth gate, and stepped over the bowl my dog drinks from, and I'd sent him the directions myself.

Ana shifted. "You're thinking," she said, asleep.

"No."

"You're always thinking. It's like sleeping on a fridge." She settled. "He's nice, though. Golf man."

"Yeah," I said. "He's nice."

I couldn't sleep, so I thought about the number eleven instead, because eleven was at least a number.

Here's what the gorilla does, and it took me a barbecue to see it. It doesn't only make you laughable. It makes *correcting it* laughable. The man who says *I never wrote that about bloodlines* is a man talking about bloodlines. I could delete the post every morning and they could put it back every night with one more sentence, and each round I'd look a little more like the forum man in the taxi.

I couldn't win that. But I do know what to do when somebody changes a thing and says nothing changed. It's the only thing I know. You keep a copy from before.

So at one in the morning I went down to the kitchen and built what I build at work. It isn't clever. Once a night it copies my own threads and every public page of the forum, keeps each night's copy, and in the morning shows me what's different: what was added, what was taken out, and under whose name. A watcher. I've built forty of them for other people's systems. This was the first one I'd built for me, on my own laptop, with a dog asleep on my feet.

It needed a time to run. I gave it 02:14. I'd like to call that strategy. It was spite.

I ran it once by hand to see that it worked. It reported no changes, because it had nothing to compare with yet. One copy is no use to anybody.

Tomorrow there'd be two.
