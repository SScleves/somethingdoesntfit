# The night he stops being afraid

I got home from Zwolle with a flask I didn't want and one sentence in a notebook, and I put them both on the kitchen table and looked at them until it got dark.

I know what to do with one copy of a thing. You file it. One odd reading from a tired system gets logged and ignored, and that's not laziness, that's procedure. I'd had a place, some weather and a hand, at a grave, on four days of bad sleep, and I'd put it under no sleep, and I'd have told anyone on my team to do the same.

But now there were two. A retired engineer in Zwolle whom I'd never met, after a funeral, with a bridge where I had mud. In my work, when two systems that have never spoken to each other report the same odd thing, you stop saying noise. You go back to the first one and read it again, properly.

The trouble was that the first one was the length of a camera flash, eight months old, and inside my own head, and I'd read it the way I read page 118 the first three times: fast, by its shape, looking for permission to stop. I wanted to read it slowly. There's no shelf to fetch it from.

So that night I sat down on the kitchen floor and tried to get it back.

That's all it was. Every door in the house shut. Phone in the drawer. Lights off except the one over the cooker, which Buddy likes, because it means food is possible. I sat with my back against the cupboard where his biscuits are, legs out, hands on my knees, and tried to remember a cemetery.

What I got was a memory of a memory. Gold light, crows, and a patch of cold mud I'd described to myself so many times it had gone flat, like a postcard of mud. I sat there for an hour remembering so hard that I got a headache, and Buddy watched me from the blankets with an expression I'd have described, in a review, as *concern for a colleague.*

The second night, the same. I tried a breathing thing from the forum and fell asleep and woke at three with my neck in the shape of the cupboard handle.

The third night I nearly didn't. I sat down, and Noor was working and not answering, and my wife was at her mother's with a Doberman, and there was a woman in Hengelo with a date against her name, and I thought: *this is the stupidest thing you have ever done. You're a man on a floor. This happens to other people, the ones on the forums, the ones with the glow. You check paper. Go and check some paper.*

And I'd checked all the paper there was. That was the thing. So I sat.

On the fourth night I stopped trying.

I don't mean I gave up. I mean something smaller and I can't make it bigger. I'd been trying to get it back for three nights the way you try to fall asleep, which is the one thing trying prevents, and on the fourth night, around two, with the cooker light on and the dog breathing, I thought: *fine. It's not coming. Sit here anyway, because the floor's warm and the dog's here and there's nothing on upstairs.* And I stopped waiting.

---

It came back.

Not the memory. The thing itself, the same as at the grave, except that it didn't stop after a flash, because this time I didn't stop it.

My cheek was against cold mud. I could tell you there were small stones in it. The sky was gray and low and wrong, and I was lying down in a way you don't choose, and I wasn't frightened of that, which I noticed. And the hand was there. Cracked at the knuckles. Reaching for mine.

At the grave that's where I'd cut it off. This time it arrived. It took hold.

And I knew it. At the funeral I'd said *almost* knew, like a word in a language you've forgotten. There was no almost. I couldn't have given you a name, or a face; there wasn't a face. I knew it the way I know my own front door in the dark, the weight of it, where it sticks. I had known that hand all my life and I had never once seen it in this one.

Whoever it belonged to said something. I'm sure of that the way you're sure someone's talking in the next room. I didn't get the words. Henk got words. I got a hand, and it held on.

Then it was the kitchen.

---

I checked. Of course I checked.

I sat on the floor and looked at the kitchen on purpose, the way you look at a room after you've been told a system's fine, to see if it is. The cooker light was on. The clock on the oven said 02:14, which I'd like to say I made up, and didn't. The dog's bowl was where the dog's bowl is. The fridge was humming the hum it has. Buddy was on the blankets with his head on his paws, watching me, awake, and had been, I think, the whole time. I was not glowing. I was a forty-one-year-old man in socks on a kitchen floor, and if Ana had walked in at that moment she'd have seen a tired man and a dog and said *come to bed*, and she'd have been right.

One thing was different. My left hand was on my knee. My right one I was sitting on.

I hadn't put it there. It was cold to the wrist, the cold you get from carrying shopping home in January, and I'd tucked it under my leg like a boy who's been told not to touch something. I looked at that for a while. A woman in Leiden at her kitchen table, with one hand under her arm. A pair of gloves, bought in May. It lasted about a minute. Then it was just my hand.

Nothing else in the room had changed.

What had changed was that I wasn't afraid.

Not brave. I don't mean brave. I mean that a thing that had been running in me since a corridor when I was sixteen, a thing I'd never once heard because it had always been there, the way you don't hear the fridge, had stopped. And in the quiet I could hear what it had been. Not the men in vans. The other thing. The one under the other ones. The one I'd asked a nurse about with my mouth shut at sixteen, and a grave about with my mouth shut at forty, and had built a hobby and a job and a persona and a folder called Reading in order never to hear myself ask out loud.

I hadn't been told what happens. I want to be clear about how little I'd been given: mud, a sky, a hand. No words. But the thing I'd been afraid of for twenty-five years turned out not to be the mud. It was a room where everything is done right and nobody takes hold of anybody. And somebody had.

It was gone. Like a bath. I sat there and listened to it not be there.

---

"Buddy," I said.

He lifted his head.

"I think I've got a soul."

And I was *embarrassed.* On the floor, alone, to a dog. My face went hot. I heard the sentence in the kitchen and it sounded like the forums, like the glow, like a man in a tinfoil hat, and I nearly took it back, and then I thought: *he's a dog. He's a professional. He's not going to tell anyone.*

"Sorry," I said. "I know. Bit much."

Two thumps of the tail. He put his head back down. He's heard worse.

---

Then the list read itself back to me. I didn't get up and fetch it. I couldn't have; it was gone off my table two weeks ago. But I'd read it thirty times, and I had it, and sitting there I understood I'd been reading it wrong, the way I'd read page 118 wrong: by its shape.

Forty-one entries. I'd had them in two piles. Forty about the beginning, a hand taken out of a story about where things came from. And one, twenty-six, about two rooms in Utrecht and something that passed between them. I'd asked Noor why the same people would care about both, and she'd said it was the only question.

They weren't two kinds. *Transmitted* became *arisen.* *Shared* became *coincident.* In another, about what small children say, *remembered* became *imagined.* In every one of the forty-one, somebody had received something, or handed it on, and a dull, trustworthy book had said so in a plain sentence. And in every one, after the second impression, nobody had. The thing just stood up by itself.

They never touched the wild versions. Noor had told me that in her kitchen and I hadn't known what to do with it: it's in every bookshop, with a crystal on the cover. What they took out was the sober one. The page a sensible person could check himself against.

Because that's what I'd been missing for eight months. I'd had a hand in the mud and nothing plain to put beside it, so I filed it, four seconds at a time. One person with that is a man who needs sleep. He needs a second copy before he's anything else: a page, or another person. Somebody had spent a century removing the pages. The people, I was starting to understand, they handled by appointment.

---

I stayed on the floor till it was light. Not doing anything. Just sitting with it, and with the dog, the way you sit with a system that's finally reporting properly and you don't want to touch it in case it stops.

Somewhere in there I went back to a corridor. I knew I would. Sixteen, a coin, four machines with four sounds. A man saying *leave it for now.* A nurse saying *he's in a better place*, and a boy turning the sentence over once and putting it where there was nowhere. And a hand on a sheet that I didn't take until it couldn't mean anything.

I couldn't go back and take it. I'm not telling you I did. I sat on a kitchen floor twenty-five years later and knew what it would have been for.

At seven I got up, slowly, with the noise men my age make, and let the dog out, and made coffee, and stood at the back door watching him drink from the bowl by the gate. I'd scrubbed that bowl four times. He hadn't held it against the bowl.

At nine I rang Ana.

"I'm okay," I said.

Silence on the line. Then, in a voice I'd never heard from her, careful, the voice you use near something that might be real: "You sound okay."

"I am."

"What happened?"

"I sat on the floor."

"James."

"I sat on the floor for four nights, and on the fourth one I stopped being afraid of the thing. The big thing. The one from the funeral. I can't tell you how and I'm not going to pretend I can. But I'm not afraid of it anymore, and I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it in my voice." I had my hand on the dog's head. "And I've been keeping things from you in folders. I'd like to come to your mum's. Not to bring you back. To tell you. All of it, once, on a table. Twenty minutes. You can decide after."

A very long quiet.

"Bring the hat," Ana said.
