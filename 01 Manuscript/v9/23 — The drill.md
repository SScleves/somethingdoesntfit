# The drill

The continuity program tests itself once a year, on a bus.

I'd been on the program six weeks by then. Pieter had announced it in his nice voice, the way you'd tell someone they'd been upgraded: *they asked for the person who reads things.* A woman called Saskia had walked me through a lobby telling me to notice everything, and I'd noticed that she said it like a line she'd been given, and I'd noticed that my badge, when I touched it to the reader on the new floor, made a different sound. Higher. One note up. I don't know who tunes those things, but somebody does, and somebody had decided I was a semitone more important than I'd been on the Friday.

Continuity is the department that plans for the building not being there. Floods, fire, the thing nobody says out loud. If the bank can't open its doors on a Tuesday, continuity knows where everyone sits on Wednesday. It's a department made entirely of lists.

The test works like this. Forty named staff, chosen in advance, get on a bus at seven in the morning and are driven to the recovery site, and they sit at desks that have their names on them, and they log in, and they pretend the building has burned down, and at four o'clock they get back on the bus. It's called a drill. I was on the sheet. Number forty, the last name on it. Pieter was twelve.

I'd have gone without being told. Since Leiden I'd wanted to see that building, and here was the bank offering to drive me there and give me lunch. I had a card in my shirt pocket with the reference from the last page of the thin book copied onto it, letters, dash, numbers, in case I got near anything to hold it up to.

---

The bus went east, out past the ring road and into the flat country where the business parks are, the kind of landscape that's nothing but sheds with company names on them, each one the size of a village and containing, as far as anyone can tell, three cars. The recovery site was one of these. Low, long, gray, behind a car park with a sign at the entrance that said the name of a foundation and, underneath, smaller, the name of a company I knew from a blue lanyard at a golf day.

That part was no surprise. He sells the bank its recovery seats; this was where the seats were. I'd have been more surprised if his company's name hadn't been on the sign. I got off the bus with the other thirty-nine and collected a lanyard of my own, a temporary one, white, with my name printed on it and a number.

Forty.

They gave us a sandwich. At eight in the morning. I've never understood the sandwich at eight; I think it's less about hunger than about giving people something to hold.

---

Inside, it was an office. Nobody tells you that about the end of the world: it has carpet tiles.

A big open floor, a hundred and fifty desks maybe, in rows, each with a screen and a phone and a laminated card with a name on it. Some of the cards had our logo in the corner. The rest were other banks, other companies, other people's Tuesdays. Everyone shares the site; that's how the economics work. You don't buy a building for a fire that might never come. You buy a seat in a building somebody else owns, and you hope that when your fire comes, theirs hasn't.

My desk was at the back by the window. Desk forty. My name was on the card, spelled right, which almost nobody manages. The card was whiter than its neighbors. I sat down and logged in and the screen came up exactly as it would at work, same wallpaper, same six things I'd left open, and for a second I was at work, and then I looked up and saw people I half knew sitting in rows under strip lights eating sandwiches. I looked under the desk, out of habit. No dog. Nobody had thought to allocate one.

Pieter came past. He was in the second row, and he'd walked the length of the floor to find me, which he didn't need to do.

"Settling in?"

"It's a desk."

"It is a desk." He looked at the window. "Good spot, actually. You can see the car park." He said it as if the car park were a view, and then he said, in exactly the same voice, "How's the program treating you?"

"Lots of lists."

"You like lists."

"I do."

He nodded, and stood there a moment longer than the conversation needed, like a man who wants to say something and can't find the sentence in the building. Then someone called him, and he went.

I did the drill. You log in, you open the things you'd open, you ring a number and tell a voice that you're operational, and the voice says thank you and you hear it typing. By eleven I'd done everything the laminated card asked of me and I had five hours left.

So I did what I do at any recovery test, on anybody's system. I counted.

It's the whole discipline, really. You count what was supposed to come over, you count what arrived, and if the two numbers differ you've got a job for the afternoon. Forty of us had got off the bus; I'd counted lanyards at the door because I can't not. I took my sandwich for a walk along the rows and counted cards with our logo on them.

Forty-nine.

I did it again from the other end, in case I'd counted a pillar. Forty-nine. Forty of them had a person behind them, eating. Nine had a card, a screen, a phone, and a chair pushed in.

It's a bank of five thousand people and I don't know most of them, so an unfamiliar name means nothing. But a drill has a sheet, and the sheet had forty names, and I'd read it, twice, because I was on it. None of these nine were. I went round again and wrote them on the back of my own laminated card with the pen they'd given us. Nine names. Eight I'd never seen. The ninth I knew from the label of a beer.

Nobody asked what I was doing. A man reading desk cards at a continuity test looks like the test.

---

Then I read the rest of the building, since it was there.

The fire plan by the door. The evacuation routes. A floor map, laminated, with our floor in blue and a second floor above us in gray, labeled, in a smaller font, ARCHIVE (FOUNDATION), NO ACCESS. A list of extensions, and at the bottom one that said *Reading Room.*

There was a staircase at the far end, behind the kitchenette, with a tall window on the landing and a glass door at the top. I went up. I touched my badge to the reader, and it made no sound at all, which is a sound in itself. I didn't try the handle. I stood there like a man who's wandered into the wrong part of a hospital, and looked through.

A corridor. Carpet, better than ours. A door at the end with a window in it, and through that a room with a long table and a lamp and shelves, and a desk at the near end with a woman at it, her back to me, doing something with a card index. An actual card index, wooden, with drawers. I hadn't seen one since I was a child.

And in the corridor, against the wall, a trolley of gray boxes, spines out. Each one had a label.

I took the card out of my shirt pocket and held it up against the glass, next to the nearest box, like a man checking a lottery ticket.

Same letters at the front. The dash in the same place. The same number of digits after it. The box on the trolley and the line on the professor's last page were about a shelf and a half apart, by my arithmetic, and the same people had numbered both.

So Noor was right, and I'd now seen it myself, through a door, which put it in the left-hand column. The appointment the professor hadn't lived to keep was up one flight of stairs from desk forty.

"You're not badged for up there."

A man in a high-visibility jacket, on the stairs below me, friendly. The site manager; it said so on him.

"I know. I was being nosy. Who is it, upstairs?"

"Heritage." He said it the way you'd say *accounts.* "Old papers. Different tenant, same generator."

"Who pays for the generator?"

"Not me, mate." He laughed. "There's more sandwiches if you want one."

I went back down and sat at desk forty and looked at the car park, which Pieter had called a view.

Julian was in it. A good coat, one hand in his pocket, standing by something German and quiet. He wasn't talking to anyone. He was looking up at the tall window on the stair landing, where a minute earlier a man had stood holding a card against a glass door. Then he looked along the building to my window, which from out there should have been a sheet of gray, and he found the right pane, and he waved.

I waved back. It's what you do.

So that was seen. I'd come to look at their building and been watched doing it by the one person there who knew what I read, and I couldn't even be annoyed, because he sells the seats and it was his shed. He got in the car and drove away.

---

The bus went back at four. Pieter sat near the front. I sat at the back with the laminated card in my pocket, nine names on one side and my own on the other, and watched the sheds go by.

At home, Buddy was at the window. Ana said he'd been there since three.

"How was the end of the world?"

"It had a sandwich."

"Of course it did."

I had two questions when I got on the bus home and I still had them at midnight. Who were nine desks for, in our colors, if not for anyone on our sheet? And who pays for a generator that a bank's emergency floor shares with an archive? Both of them were questions about a contract, and contracts I could get. I was on the program. Reading them was my job.
