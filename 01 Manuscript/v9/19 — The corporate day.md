# The corporate day

The bank has a golf day, which it has never once had.

I need to explain that, because it's the kind of sentence that gets you a look. Every spring an email goes round about the golf day. Every spring there's a form, and a date, and a course an hour away with a name like a retirement home, and every spring, two weeks before, a second email goes round saying that in light of the current environment the golf day has been postponed, and nobody is surprised, because the current environment is always in light of something. I've worked there eleven years. I've filled in the form eleven times. I don't know why. I think it's the same reason people buy lottery tickets, which is that filling in the form is the enjoyable part and you'd rather not have it taken from you by realism.

This year they postponed it to September, and then nobody postponed it again.

I found out on a Thursday, when a calendar invitation arrived with a tee time on it, an actual tee time, 09:40, and a note from the organizer saying I was in a three-ball and to please confirm my handicap. I sat at my desk and read it twice. Then I got up and went to the window and looked out at the car park for a while, the way you do when something good has happened and you don't want anyone to see your face.

It had been eleven days since Els's folder and nine since I'd posted page 118. Three people had looked at it. Nobody had answered. I'd started checking the thread the way Mora checks an empty bowl, often and with the same result. I wanted one day in which the only document was a scorecard, which makes no claims about anything except how badly you played.

I confirmed my handicap. I rounded it down, which is a lie, but a traditional one.

---

The course was an hour north, flat and windy, the kind of Dutch course that's a polder with ambitions. Buddy wasn't allowed, which I'd known and had still, on the morning, half thought about arguing. Ana walked me to the car with Mora at her heel and said, "Try to speak to a human," and I said I'd try, and she said, "A human, James, not a man who wants to tell you about his new wedges," and I said that was a very narrow definition of human, and she kissed me and said go.

Pieter was in the car park when I pulled in. He was wearing golf clothes he had clearly bought that week, all of them, in one shop. He waited for me to get out, and then he came over holding a plastic sleeve the way you'd hold a dish that belongs to someone else.

"I was going to do this Monday," he said. "Then I thought, not in the office."

It was a form. *Wellbeing referral (line manager).* My name, my employee number, and a box headed *Reason for referral*, which had been typed:

*Recent bereavement (family). Disturbed sleep. Preoccupation with historical documents.*

Under that, a line for the employee to sign.

"You don't have to see anyone," Pieter said. "Nobody rings you. It goes in the file, and then if you want a quieter quarter there's a reason for it on paper. That's how they explained it to me. It's a kindness, James. It's meant as one."

"Who wrote the reason?"

"It came like that. They usually do."

"I've never told anyone at work what I read."

Pieter looked at his new shoes. "You've seemed tired. Since the spring. People notice." Which wasn't an answer to what I'd said, and he knew it, and I let him have it.

Bereavement, fine. I'd taken a day for the funeral. Sleep, Ana could have told them, and nobody else. But at the bank I'm a man who plays golf badly and asks dull questions. The only places on earth that knew about the books were a dealer's telephone, a neighbor's telephone, a forum, and a list that went out at 02:14.

I'd read the sentence eleven days before, standing in my coat. *The preferred response to a correct reader is agreement, followed by reclassification.* Here was the second half, in a plastic sleeve, with a line for my signature.

At work, when a vendor sends you a diagnosis you think is wrong, you don't argue with the diagnosis. You sign for receipt and you keep your copy, because the diagnosis tells you what they can see. Also, a man who refuses a kind form in a car park is a man with something to protect, and they'd write that down too.

"Is there a copy for me?"

"The yellow one."

I signed it on the hood of the car, with Pieter's pen. The hood was still warm. He said, "Good man," with more relief than the occasion needed, and tore off the yellow sheet for me, and went to find his three-ball.

So that's in my file. A form that says I'm a grieving man with a hobby, signed by me. Anything I raised at work from then on would be read through it, and I knew that when I picked up the pen.

Across the car park a man was standing at the open trunk of something German, pulling on a glove, looking our way. I didn't know him at that distance. I folded the yellow sheet into my back pocket and went to play golf.

---

I was in a three-ball with two men from Treasury. I'd never met them. I knew within forty seconds that I wasn't going to meet them now either, because the first thing one of them said, as the cart girl went past with the drinks, was something about her that I'm not going to write down, and the second one laughed, and they both looked at me to see if I'd laugh too, and I did the thing I do, which is smile in a way that could be read either way and look at my glove.

Fine. I've played with worse. You play your own ball. I teed off, badly, and walked after it, and the Treasury men talked to each other about a boat, and the wind came across the flat land with nothing to stop it, and for about six holes I had a form in my pocket and didn't think about it.

Then I saw him on the practice green.

It was Julian. The stranger the starter had paired me with on a Wednesday in June, the one with the swing I'd have paid for, who'd asked about Buddy before he asked my name. We'd played most Wednesdays since. I'd told Ana about him, which I never do; she'd said, "You've made a friend," in the voice she uses for the dogs when they've done something clever.

He was in a company polo, putting, with a vendor's lanyard round his neck, the blue ones they give the sponsors. He looked up as my three-ball came past on the way to the seventh. He saw me, and his face lit up.

"James!"

He came over. He shook my hand with both of his. He asked after the dogs, by name, both of them, and I felt the Treasury men behind me recalibrating something.

"What are you doing here?"

"Working, I'm afraid." He lifted the lanyard like it was a joke. "I told you I was in continuity. I didn't say whose. I sell you people your seats. If your building burns down on a Tuesday, where do you all sit on Wednesday. I talk to people like your head of risk about things that will never happen until they do. It's a living. The golf's the only good part."

I said he'd never mentioned it was us. He said I'd never asked, and that he'd found the less you said about continuity the more people trusted you to provide it. Everyone laughed. I laughed.

He knew the organizer, it turned out. When I came off the ninth the three-ball had been rearranged, the Treasury men paired off with each other and a sponsor, and I was walking to the tenth tee with Julian, whose surname I didn't catch that day because he said it the way people say surnames they expect you to forget.

---

The back nine was the best golf I've ever played. I'd like to say that was the company and it probably was.

He was good, properly good, and generous with it the way good players sometimes are and usually aren't. He didn't give me tips. He watched me hit a seven iron thin into a bunker and said, "I'd have done exactly the same," which was a lie and a kindness in the same breath. He talked about nothing. The wind. A client in Antwerp who'd insisted on a recovery site with a view.

"You were doing paperwork on a hood," he said, on the eleventh. "On a golf day. I nearly came over and confiscated it."

"A wellbeing form."

"Ah. They gave me one of those once." He lined up a putt. "Sign everything, is my advice. It's the ones who won't sign that people worry about." He holed it.

On the twelfth I asked him my one question of the day. I was on the continuity program now, as of the first, and I'd been reading its lists for a month, and there was a thing I hadn't found in any of them.

"The seats. Who decides who gets one?"

"The client." He didn't look up from his ball. "You tell us how many, we make sure there's a desk and a phone and a sandwich. We count chairs, James. Who sits in them is your lot's business, thank God."

It was a dull answer, and I wrote it on the scorecard anyway, next to my six.

At the fourteenth he asked about Ana.

Not *how's the wife.* He asked how she was doing, since her grandmother. He remembered. I'd mentioned it once, in June, on a tee box, in the half-sentence you give a stranger, and he'd kept it, and here it was, three months later, offered back to me carefully like something I'd lent him.

I told him more than I'd told anyone. Not about the book; I had enough sense left for that. About Ana. About how the grief had changed shape, from a thing that lived with us to a thing that visited, and about the knitting she hadn't finished, and about how I'd been bad at it, at the funeral, at saying the thing people say. He listened. He didn't fill the silences. And when I was done he said, "You're hard on yourself, James. Most people never notice they're lying at a funeral. You noticed and it's still bothering you in September. I wouldn't call that a defect."

I three-putted the fourteenth. I didn't care.

---

There was a dinner afterward, in the clubhouse, the kind with a raffle. I didn't stay for it and neither did he; he had another client, he said, the glamorous life. We stood in the car park in the last of the light with the wind dropping and he said we should play here again, properly, not at a corporate thing, and I said yes before he'd finished the sentence.

"Bring the old boy," he said. "Buddy. He can ride in the cart."

I said he'd like that.

"And James." He had his car keys in his hand and he turned them over once. "If work's ever not fine. You've got my other number now." He nodded at my phone, where he'd put it, on the thirteenth, while I was looking for a ball.

He drove off in something German and quiet. I stood there a while longer than I needed to.

Ana rang while I was still in the car park.

"Did you speak to a human?"

"I did."

"A real one?"

"Julian. He was there. It turns out it's us he sells to. Disaster planning. He asked after you. He remembered about your grandmother."

There was a pause.

"That's nice," Ana said, and it was, and it was also the voice she uses when she's filing something. "Was he nice?"

"He was great. I played the best back nine of my life."

"Then I'm glad." Mora barked in the background at something only Mora could see. "Come home. Buddy's been at the window since four."

I didn't tell her about the form. I had it in my back pocket the whole call.

---

The answer came that evening. I was at the kitchen table with the laptop, Buddy on my feet, and I checked the thread the way I'd checked it for nine days, and there it was. A username I didn't know. The account was a week old.

It was a lovely reply. It was long and polite and it knew a great deal. Variants of this kind were common, it said. The author had very likely revised the sentence himself before his departure. The Leiden houses of the period had been notoriously casual about recording such things, as was the ordinary practice of the trade. It thanked me for the care I'd taken over the photographs. It agreed with every word I'd written, and nothing in it contradicted me.

I got the folder out and put page three beside the screen, and went through the first paragraph with a finger. *Courteously.* Yes. *Fully.* Four hundred words. *With reference to the ordinary practice of the trade.* He'd used the actual phrase. Then I opened the Record and looked at what I'd written before I posted. Three out of three.

I took the yellow sheet out of my pocket and laid it next to the photocopy. Paragraph one had arrived at nine in the evening from a stranger. Paragraph four had arrived at nine in the morning from my manager, in a plastic sleeve. Whoever they were, they'd answered me twice in one day, kindly, and one of the answers had my signature on it.

So I decided something, at that table, and it seemed sensible at the time. The form said I was tired. Fine. From Monday I'd be tired. No tickets, no questions at stand-up, nothing in a queue with my name on it. I'd be quiet at work and read what they sent me.

I wrote the stranger a short thank-you, because that's what a bibliographical inquiry does.
