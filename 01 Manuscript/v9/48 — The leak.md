# The leak

The brewery man rang them on the Wednesday afternoon, and by Friday night the date had moved, and I know that because of a calendar entry I'd had no business caring about.

The new schedule, the one I'd read on a phone with a flask ticking down beside it, had a date at the top: the day it came into force, the day Anneke was due to sign it. The thirty-first of December. The old man's review date, the one from the bottom of page 212, the one that had moved once already in the autumn, the week I signed a form and went quiet. And in our own systems, in the continuity program's own calendar, which I had every right to read, the thirty-first had an entry, blandly named, a quarterly meeting that had been in the calendar for eleven years and that I'd been invited to because I was on the program now.

On Friday night at eleven the meeting moved.

From the thirty-first to the twenty-third. Monday. This Monday. The entry changed the way entries do, with a little note that said *rescheduled by organizer*, and the organizer was a name I'd never heard of at an address that was the foundation's, and I was sitting at the kitchen table with eleven envelopes in a folder when my phone made the small sound that means a calendar has changed, and I looked, and I knew.

The review had come forward for the second time. Somebody had listened to a brewer, and somebody had looked at the number of helpers in the east of the country who'd stopped closing tickets that fortnight, which I imagine looked, on whatever they look at, like a dial being turned the wrong way, and somebody had done what I'd have done, which is pull the date in before the thing spreads.

Twice. The first time, in September, for a man going quiet. This time for a man being loud. There's no speed you can go at that they don't count.

"Ana."

She came down. She looked at my face.

"Monday," I said.

---

I'm going to tell you what I thought in the next ten minutes, because it was the clearest I've ever thought and I don't expect to think that clearly again.

Monday was the schedule. Anneke signs, the quarter starts, the visits begin. I'd given Anneke everything on Wednesday night and I didn't know yet what she'd do with it; she'd said *I'm going to read one*, and she'd taken the folder, and that was all. If she signed, the addresses on the new schedule, Hengelo among them, plus whatever had been added since the brewer, got their visits, and I had a very good idea now what a visit was.

The board met on Monday too. Not about this. About the foundation's trustees, once a year, a formality, twelve people in a room approving a list of names they'd never met. That was the quarterly meeting in the calendar; that was what had moved. They'd pulled the foundation's annual confirmation forward to the same morning as the schedule, so that whatever the board did, the schedule would already be signed.

Twelve board members. I had eleven sheets left and they were theirs, and I'd been going to deliver them next week, calmly, one a day, the way I'd done the others.

I had two days. And they knew about me properly now, not as a man with papers in a reading room but as a man with two hundred and ninety sheets in the east of the country, because the brewer had rung and I'd been in his yard.

"They'll come here," Ana said.

"Yes."

"Tonight?"

"I don't think so." I looked at the window, where the dog had been standing every evening for two months and where he was standing now. "They don't like fingerprints. A visit to the house of the man who's been handing out sheets, the week he hands them out, is a fingerprint. They'll go round me."

"Round you how?"

My phone rang.

---

It was Pieter. It was twenty past eleven on a Friday night and it was Pieter, who in eleven years had never once rung me outside working hours, and I could hear, before he spoke, that he was in his car.

"James."

"Pieter."

"There's a man at my door."

I put the phone on the table on speaker and Ana sat down across from me and we both looked at it.

"Tell me."

"I'm in the car. I was coming home. I'm at the end of my street and there's a car outside my house that isn't ours and there's a man on my step talking to Marieke." His voice was very steady, the steadiness of someone who's decided to be steady for the length of a phone call and will pay for it after. "He's got a folder. He's got the same kind of coat as the man at the drill. In the car park."

"Can you see his face?"

"No. He's turned away. Marieke's got the door on the chain. She does that. I told her to do that, years ago, I don't know why, she thought I was mad." A pause. "He's just standing there. He's not pushing. He's *waiting*."

Ana's hand had found mine on the table.

"Pieter, listen to me. I'm going to tell you what to do and I need you to do exactly this, and I'll explain afterwards, and you can be as angry as you want."

"Okay."

"Drive up to the house. Park where you always park. Get out. Walk up to him like it's nothing, because to him it is nothing; he doesn't know you've seen a sheet, and if he did he'd be surprised you were calm, so be calm. Say good evening. Say, can I help you."

"And then?"

"And then let him in."

Silence on the line.

"James."

"Let him in. Make him tea. Sit down with him in your kitchen with Marieke and the kids upstairs and listen to whatever he says. He'll have a reason. It'll be about the bank, or the program, or your access, or something about the migration. It'll be plausible. It'll be kind. He'll ask you things. He'll ask whether anyone's been in touch with you about your work, and whether anyone's given you anything."

"And I say—"

"You say nothing."

"I can't say *nothing*, he's—"

"You can. That's the one thing you can do. You answer every question with something true that isn't the answer. You've been busy. The program's a lot of lists. You don't really follow the politics. You're very happy to help. You let him talk, and you nod, and you make a second cup of tea, and you say nothing, and in forty minutes he'll leave, because he's not there to hurt you, Pieter. He's there to find out what you know. And if you give him nothing, then nothing is what he's got."

I could hear him breathing. I could hear the indicator ticking in his car.

"Why would he hurt me? I'm on the list. You showed me. I'm *on* it."

"You're on it because you said leave it. You stopped saying it on Monday. That's why he's on your step."

A long pause.

"Marieke's not on it," Pieter said. "Not separately. She's a number. She's one of my three."

"I know."

"And if I'd closed your ticket. Like always. He wouldn't be here."

"No."

"So this is you. This is because of your sheet."

"Yes," I said. "It is. And I'm sorry. And I'd do it again. And I need you to go and let him in and make him tea, because the alternative is that he waits on your step until he's been seen waiting, and then it's not a visit anymore, it's a thing with a fingerprint on it, and I don't know what they do about those and I don't want to find out at your house."

The indicator stopped. I heard the car move.

"He's turned round," Pieter said. "He's seen the headlights. He's—" A breath. "He's smiling. Jesus. He's *waving*."

"Wave back."

"James."

"Wave back, Pieter. Park the car. Say good evening. And ring me when he's gone, from the kitchen, with the kettle on, and I'll be here."

The line went quiet for a second, and then I heard a car door, and footsteps on gravel, and Pieter's voice, from a distance, saying "Evening. Can I help you?" in a tone I'd heard him use in a glass box for eleven years, the tone of a man who's never once lost his temper at work and has saved it all for somewhere, and then the call ended.

---

He rang back at ten past midnight.

"He's gone."

"Tell me."

"He was from the foundation. Compliance. Routine, he said. A review of access on the program, everyone added since the summer, nothing to worry about. He asked if anyone had approached me about the program's data. I said the program was mostly lists. He liked that. He said he was a list man himself." Pieter stopped. "He asked about you. By name. Whether you were settling in. I said you liked lists too. He wrote that down. He had a little book."

"Good."

"He asked after the kids. Their names. He knew their names, James. He asked if Lotte was still doing the hockey."

I didn't say anything.

"And then I gave him the sheet."

Ana's hand closed on mine.

"Pieter."

"I know what you said. I'm not you. I can't sit in my own kitchen with a man asking about my daughter's hockey and say *nothing*. You can. That's what you've been doing for a year and I don't know how you stand it." He breathed. "It was in the drawer by the kettle. I got it out and I put it on the table in front of him and I said, this came to me, and I don't know what it is, and I'd like you to tell me."

"What did he do?"

"He read it." Pieter's voice had gone strange. "All of it. He read it the way you read things, with his finger on the line. He read his own word, the first box, and he nodded, like it was correctly spelled. He read the ticket. He read the list and found my name and I watched him look at the three. And then he got to the last box."

"The desks."

"He read desk twelve four times. He read it out loud. Rotterdam, Nijmegen, the woman with two names. And he stopped, and he looked at me, and he said, 'Where did you get this?' Not angry. Like a man who's found a page in his own file he's never seen. I said it came to me. He said, 'Four,' and he said it again, 'Four,' and then he asked me, very politely, whether I'd been to the building, and I said yes, every year, I sit at desk twelve for the drill, there's a card with my name on it. And he said, 'Yes. There would be.'"

"And then?"

"And then he folded it in three and put it in his folder, with his own papers, and he thanked Marieke for the tea and shook my hand and said if I thought of anything I should ring, and he gave me a card. The foundation's name and a number. It's the general number. The one with the music."

"I know the one."

"James, what the fuck is this."

"I'll tell you. Properly. Not tonight." I looked at Ana. "Tonight I need to know one thing. Did you see his face properly, in the kitchen? Was it Julian?"

"No." Pieter sounded surprised at the question. "Nothing like. Sixty, maybe. Gray. A good jumper, the kind a wife picks. Nice voice. He drank the tea. Both cups." A pause. "He didn't know, James. About the desks. I'd swear to it. He read it like it was news."

I closed my eyes.

"Okay," I said. "Thank you. Go to bed. Lock the door. You did it exactly wrong and I think it might have been better than right."

"I gave him the sheet," said Pieter, in a voice of wonder. "He was in my kitchen asking about my daughter's hockey and I gave him the sheet and he *read* it." And then, differently: "He had the same face you had. In the autumn. When you put the annex on my desk. Like he'd found a thing that didn't reconcile."

"Something like that."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know," I said. "I've got a dog," which wasn't an answer, and Pieter didn't understand it, and said goodnight.

---

Ana took her hand off mine.

"Three addresses," she said. She'd been counting on her fingers; I'd seen her. "You said they'd go round you. Pieter's one."

"Pieter's one. There'll be two more tonight. People who got sheets and stopped. They're checking the ones nearest the top." I pulled the laptop over and opened the list, the two hundred and ninety, with ticks beside all of them and the eleven at the bottom with no ticks that were the board. "And on Monday Anneke signs the schedule, and the board confirms the trustees, and all of this goes back to being nothing."

"Unless."

"Unless the board doesn't confirm them. Unless twelve people walk into a room on Monday morning each holding a sheet that says what the foundation they're about to approve has been charging their bank, per flagged person, for thirty-one years. And who's kept. And who else has the chair."

"Eleven sheets."

"Eleven. Anneke's got hers."

"By Monday."

"By Sunday night. Monday morning's too late; they need to have read it."

Ana looked at the list. She looked at the dog at the window. She looked at the clock on the oven, which said twenty to one on a Saturday morning, and she did the thing she does, which is put the kettle on, and while it boiled she stood at the counter with her back to me and said, "Where are they? The eleven."

"All over. Two in the city. Three in The Hague. One past Woerden. One in Bunnik. The rest in between."

"That's a long night."

"About that."

The kettle clicked. She didn't move.

"Buddy goes to Frank's," she said. "Mum's is two flights and he can't, and I'm not leaving him in this house for a night with nobody in it. Mora comes with us; she's good in a car and she's a Doberman and I'd like a Doberman in the car. We leave Sunday after dark, because if they're watching the house they're watching it in daylight like normal people. We do the far ones first and work back. You read, I drive, and nobody reads me any signs about pumping stations."

"Ana."

"And on Monday morning you're not anywhere near that building, you're somewhere with a scorecard, because if this works I don't want you in the room when it does, and if it doesn't I want to know where you are."

I looked at my wife, in her dressing gown, at one in the morning, with a kettle behind her and a list of eleven strangers in front of her, planning a night's driving to deliver the only thing that could stop a machine she'd told a man to his face she didn't believe in.

"You don't believe any of this," I said.

"Not a word." She poured the tea. "Drink that. Then print the eleven again, the long version. Then sleep. I'll ring Frank."

Buddy got down from the window at last and came and lay across my feet, and I sat at the table and printed eleven sheets, one at a time, while my wife rang a man who'd never been responsible for anything that breathes, and the Doberman watched the door, and somewhere in the east of the country two more people were making tea for a nice man with a folder and saying nothing, because I'd told them to, and one man hadn't, and I didn't know yet which of them had done me the favor.
