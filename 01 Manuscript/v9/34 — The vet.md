# The vet

It started at two. Of course it did.

The sound woke me. Not a bark. Buddy hadn't barked since the summer; he'd stopped bothering, the way he'd stopped the stairs. It was the other sound, the one every dog owner knows from the inside of their own sleep, and I was on the landing before I was awake and in the kitchen before I'd turned a light on, and he was on the floor by the radiator, on his side, with his legs going.

I got the blanket under him. I got the car keys. I rang the number on the fridge, the one Ana had put there in June in her careful hand, *EMERGENCY VET*, with a little heart, because she puts hearts on things, and a woman answered on the second ring and said *bring him*, and I carried a twelve-year-old dog to a gray car in the rain in my socks.

The girl on the night desk typed his name while I stood there holding him.

"Buddy Klippe," she said. "We've got him booked in for Thursday."

"Yes."

"The kennel. Thursday to Monday."

"Yes," I said. "He's early."

The vet was young and kind and unhurried, and *unhurried* was the thing. She didn't rush. She looked at him and listened to him and put a hand flat on his chest the way Ana puts a hand on mine, and she said, "Okay. Okay. Not tonight, I think." And then, because I must have had the face: "I mean it. I don't say that. He's old and something's upset him badly and he's dehydrated, and I'm going to keep him for a few hours and give him fluids, and you're going to go and sit in that chair, and it's not tonight."

"What upset him?"

She looked at me the way vets look at you when the real answer is *I don't know* and they've been trained not to say it. "Something he ate or drank, maybe. Old dogs get into things. Did he get into anything?"

"He can't get into anything. He can't do the stairs. He drinks from a bowl by the back gate and eats what I give him."

"Then I don't know," she said, which I liked her for. "Sit down. I'll come and get you."

I sat down. There's a chair in every vet's waiting room at three in the morning and I've now sat in it. I had my phone in my hand and I did nothing with it for a long time.

Then I looked at the date. It was Wednesday. The schedule said Saturday.

---

They let me take him home at six, on the condition that I brought him back on Thursday as planned, which the vet said with a straight face. I carried him in through the front, under the camera, and the first thing I did, before the blankets, was look at what the camera had seen. One clip. Me, at a quarter past two, in socks, with a dog. Then four hours of a path.

The kitchen was the kitchen. The light was on, because I'd left it on. The back door was shut and not locked, because I'd gone out the front with my arms full. My coat was on the table where it had been for a week.

I don't know why I lifted it. I'd like to say instinct. I think I just wanted my coat.

The page from the schedule was there. The two green books were there, and Els's father's folder, and the laptop, which is worth more than everything else on that table put together. The thin book wasn't.

I looked for it the way you look for keys: properly, twice, in places it had never been. Then I stopped and stood in my own kitchen and said it out loud, to the dog, because it needed saying plainly.

"I watched the date and the door."

A date somebody had printed for me, and the one door with a camera on it. I'd booked a kennel for the Saturday. I'd sat in this kitchen for a week so the house would never be empty by day. And someone had put a hand under the back gate on a Tuesday night, and waited for me to carry my dog out the front, and walked in the other way. I'd shown him the lane. I'd told him which bowl.

I thought about the police for as long as it takes to hear the sentence. *Someone took a notebook with no title and left the laptop.* I had photographs of every page; I'd taken them the night I first opened it, for the Record. A photograph of a notebook is a thing anyone could have made. I could have made it. Frank had already told me which column that goes in.

I made a bed on the kitchen floor out of every blanket in the house and lay down next to Buddy, in my coat, and slept badly. I woke twice, and both times he was awake, watching me, his head on his paws, the way he watches me go up the stairs from the bottom step.

Neither of us could help the other. Neither of us left.

At nine in the morning I rang Ana. I didn't wait for nine at night.

"He's okay," I said, first, before hello, because she'd hear it in my voice otherwise. "He had a bad night. He's okay. The vet said not tonight. She meant it."

Silence. Then: "What happened?"

"Something in his water. I think. The bowl by the gate." I had my hand on his head. "The date's Saturday. I had everything ready for Saturday. It was last night, round the back, and while I was at the vet somebody came in and took the professor's notebook off the kitchen table. Nothing else."

A long breath. "Are you saying Julian—"

"I'm saying what happened. I don't know who. I know who's walked that lane."

She didn't say anything for a while. When she did, her voice was different. "Send me a photo of him."

I did. Asleep, on the blankets, gray to the eyebrows.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Nine tonight."

"Nine tonight."

---

It was a photo that finally got me to check a person.

I had the barbecue pictures on my phone, the ones Ana had sent me afterward, *look at your face*, *look at the surveyor's hat*. In two of them, in the background, by the heater, there was Julian. And that afternoon I sent one of those pictures to Noor, in Leiden, because I was asking her about the forum man. That was all. The lonely one with the theories, who'd found our house from a picture; I wanted to know if he was a known thing, if he was harmless. I cropped it badly. I left in the heater.

She rang me that night. She'd never rung me. We'd messaged and I'd sat in her kitchen, and she had never once used a telephone, and it rang, and I knew before I answered that it wasn't about the forum man.

"The man by the heater."

"Julian. He's a golf friend. He sells the bank its—"

"His name's Kalder."

I was standing in the kitchen. I sat down on the floor, next to the dog.

"Julian Kalder. He was in the department the month after Hendrik retired, overseeing the transfer of the collection. He was in and out for three weeks, with a clipboard, and he was *very* kind about it. He learned everyone's names. He asked about my mother. He brought his own coffee, in a flask." Her voice was perfectly steady and I could hear what it cost. "I haven't seen him for nine years and I'd know him in the dark. Why is he in your garden?"

I'd like to say it took me a minute. It took no time at all. I'd known since the pub, the way you know a disk is going: every reading fine, and a noise. Julian K. on the invoice, who fixed my access in eleven minutes. The lanyard. The word from the professor's margin. Deventer. I'd had all of it, and all of it was mine, things I'd noticed, alone. What Noor had just given me was the one thing I'd never had on him, which was somebody else. A second person who'd seen that face nine years before it asked about my dog.

"He asked about Buddy first," I said. "On the course. Before he asked my name."

"Yes," said Noor. "That's what he does."

---

I went to Leiden on Thursday. I took Buddy to the kennel first, since he had a reservation, and the girl on the desk said *there he is* as if he'd been away on business.

On the train I built the thing I was going to say to her. It went: *you know him. You can place him. You were there, in the department, and you're a scholar, and you've got a name, and if you say it, publicly, with mine, then there are two of us, and two is a pattern, and a pattern is something a style guide can't reclassify.*

I'd built her into a witness. On a train. Without asking.

She let me say it, the whole thing, standing in her hall with my coat on, and when I'd finished she said, "Your post is back up."

"I know."

"You told me you'd deleted it. I gave you my address because you'd deleted it."

I got the laptop out of my bag, in her hall, and showed her. It isn't much to look at. A list of nights. The first four: no post, only my dull note saying I'd deleted it. The fifth night: the post, back, dated the summer like always, with a third sentence that hadn't been there at the barbecue. My deletion the next morning. Two nights of nothing. Then the post again, and a fourth sentence.

"I take it down. They put it back, a little longer each time. This shows which is which." I turned the screen toward her. "It's the only proof I've got that I did what you asked."

She read it the way she reads paper, all of it, including the dull columns. "That's well made," she said. Then: "No."

"Noor—"

"No to the other thing. And you knew I'd say no, you've known since my kitchen, and you built it anyway, on a train, because you needed somebody to *believe* you. I'm not going to be that, James. The reason I'm alive is that I never once needed it." She wasn't angry. That was the thing. "A reader who persists is to be offered the opportunity to publish. You've just offered me one. In my own hall. You've done their job for them, and you've done it because you're frightened, and I understand, and no."

I stood there.

"Ask me something else," she said. "You're good at it when you're not frightened. Ask."

So I asked the thing I'd actually come with. "The notebook's gone. They took it on Tuesday night. I've got photographs of the last page, but a photograph is mine, I could have written anything and photographed it. The reference. Is there anyone else alive who's seen it?"

She looked at me for a moment. Then she went into the kitchen and came back with a slip of paper and a pencil, and wrote, against the wall, in a conservator's hand, without looking anything up. A letter, four digits, a slash, two more.

It matched. I didn't need to check. I checked anyway, against the photograph on my phone, one character at a time.

"It was on a crate," she said. "Nine years ago, in the transfer. I saw it for about four seconds and I told Hendrik that evening, and he wrote it at the back of his book and sat on it for nine years. And then last winter he wrote to them. Two pages about his rights and four lines asking to see it. That's not asking, that's arguing with a request attached. They gave him the second of December, and he was dead in March." She put the slip in my hand. "Nobody has ever simply asked. Go on the day. In person. Write it on their slip like any reader and put it on the table. And don't argue. You'll want to. You'll have a bag full of things. Ask first."

"Why are you giving me this?"

"Because you asked instead of argued," Noor said. "For once. Go home to your dog."

---

I sent her one message on Friday morning. *Thank you.*

An hour later she answered. She'd never been quick before. One line.

*There's a van on my street.*

I rang. It rang out. I sent *are you all right? ring me*, and watched the screen for a while, like a man waiting for a kettle.
