# The math

Hendrik Maasen's address was on a letter. One of his books had a note in it from a librarian about a renewal, typed, dull, sent to his house, and he'd used it as a bookmark and left it at page sixty. So I had a street in Deventer, and on the Saturday after Frank's kitchen I put Buddy in the car and went to look at it.

I told Ana I was going to see a man about a book. That was true of most Saturdays and not of this one.

The house had a for-sale sign. Good curtains, still up. I stood at the gate for a while like the worst client a realtor ever had, and then the door of the next house opened and a woman came out to move a bicycle with a child's seat on it, and saw me, and saw the dog, and decided I was all right.

"He's not there, love. He died in March."

"I know. I'm sorry. I bought some of his books."

"Oh, the *books.*" She said it the way you'd say *the drinking.* She was sixty-something and kind, properly kind, the sort who takes the bins in for the whole row. "Did Hendrik write to you as well?"

"Write to me?"

"He wrote to everybody, at the end. Libraries. Foundations. He wasn't well, you know. He wasn't sleeping, and he'd got it into his head about his books, that somebody had been changing them." She bent and let Buddy smell her hand. "The foundation were very good about it. The gentleman who came for the furniture said he'd sent them some very confused letters, and that everyone had been fond of him. They're concerned for the papers. For their conservation. He left a card, in case anyone came asking." She straightened up. "I suppose that's you. Shall I tell them you called?"

Conservation. It was the second time in a month I'd heard that word from a decent person holding a telephone number. The first one, in Groningen, had stopped ringing because I'd told him what the call was for, and I'd been pleased with myself all the way down the highway. I looked at this one. She wanted to have been a good neighbor, and she had been, and I had nothing to put in front of her but my say-so. A week earlier Frank had killed a screenshot in under a minute.

"Tell them a man with an old dog asked where the daughter's shop is," I said.

Four streets from the station. Curtains. I couldn't miss it. She said she'd ring that afternoon, and she was pleased to have something to tell them, and I thanked her. So that was my name on something again, or my dog's. I'd known it when I asked.

---

Els Maasen had her father's hands. I'd never met her father and I knew it the second she put them on the counter.

Buddy went under the cutting table and lay down on an offcut of something expensive.

"I bought your father's books at the market," I said. "There was a letter in one. It's nothing, a library renewal, but it's his, so it's yours." I'd put it in an envelope and written my number on the envelope. "And I wanted to ask you about him."

"You're the one who took them." She looked at the envelope and didn't pick it up. "The man with the beard said somebody went through them spine by spine. Like Dad."

That was an opening. I had it right there. And what came out of me, warm and steady, in the voice I keep for these occasions, was:

"I'm sorry. I understand he wasn't well at the end. That he wasn't sleeping."

I watched her face shut.

"Who told you that?"

"His neighbor."

"Of course she did." She came round the counter. She wasn't angry. It would have been easier. "My father did the crossword in ink until the week he died. He was eighty-one and he could tell what you'd had for breakfast from your tie. He wasn't unwell. He was old, and he was right about something, and I don't know what. And since March every single person who has come through that door about him has been *so polite.* The man for the furniture. The foundation. Her next door, with a casserole. Now you." She held the door open. "Thank you for the letter."

I'd done it again. A stranger had handed me a sentence on a doorstep and I'd carried it four streets and delivered it, unchecked, in a kind voice, to the one person in Deventer who knew it was wrong. It's what I'd done at the grave. Apparently I'd learned nothing since the grave.

"Buddy. Come on."

He looked at me from under the table. He'd found the best floor in the province and he had no plans.

"Buddy."

Nothing. Els stood there holding the door. A bus went past. So I got down on one knee on a curtain-shop floor, with a woman waiting for me to leave, and said the first thing in my head, which was a piece of market nonsense I'd been told once over two crates of books.

"Listen. We all go back in the one bucket. The ones who held on come out together. That's the arrangement. So I'm not going without you, and you might as well get up."

He got up. Front legs, back legs, the grunt. We went out past her. She was looking at me oddly, and I took it for wanting me gone, and I said sorry again, which was polite, and she shut the door.

I told Ana the man hadn't had the book.

---

She rang the following Sunday. Unknown number, eight in the evening, me at the sink.

"Why did you say that?"

"Who is this?"

"To the dog. In my shop. Why did you say that?"

It took me a second. "It's something a man at the market says. The one with the beard. He's got a theory about a bucket. I'm sorry, I wasn't—"

"My father said that." Her voice was very even. "Three dogs we buried in that garden. He'd stand there with the spade and say it, the same words, every time, and then he'd go in and not talk until supper. I've never heard anyone else say it in my life."

"I thought it was his. The market man's."

"Dad talked at that man every Sunday for twenty years. He must have kept some of it." A pause. "You weren't polite. You were on the floor talking to a dog. Come on Saturday. Bring him."

On Saturday she was gone ten minutes into the back, and when she returned she had a folder, cardboard, the kind with an elastic band, and she held it the way you hold something you've decided to be rid of.

"The furniture went to a man with a van. I kept the dresser. Mum's." She put the folder on the counter. "This was in the bottom drawer, under the lining paper. I found it in May. I've looked at it maybe four times. I don't know what it is and I don't want it in the house and I can't throw it away, because he hid it, and he never hid anything." She pushed it an inch toward me. "You seem like someone who checks things."

"I am."

"Then check it. And don't tell me."

Buddy was under the cutting table again. She'd put the offcut down for him before we arrived.

---

Three pages. Photocopies of photocopies; you could see the gray ghost of a staple in the corner, copied along with the text, three generations deep. The top of the first page said, in a typeface nobody had ever chosen:

*PREFERRED FORMULATIONS. Fourth revision. For internal circulation to readers at level 2 and above.*

Below that, two columns. *Deprecated* on the left. *Preferred* on the right.

I read it standing at my own kitchen table with my coat still on and the dogs waiting to be let out.

*Deprecated:* was given to. *Preferred:* arose among.
*Deprecated:* taught by. *Preferred:* developed by.
*Deprecated:* the earlier peoples. *Preferred:* the first peoples.
*Deprecated:* brought. *Preferred:* discovered.
*Deprecated:* shared. *Preferred:* coincident.
*Deprecated:* remembered. *Preferred:* imagined.

Thirty-odd pairs, and every pair did what I'd found on the kitchen floor with a wet rope on my chest: it took the hand out. Nothing was ever added. Nothing was ever *denied.* If you read the right-hand column on its own it was perfectly respectable. It was how every careful scholar I'd ever read already wrote. Nobody had told a lie. They'd written a house style, for the house that decides what careful sounds like.

The last row on the page sat a little apart from the others, under the word *Consolation.* The right-hand side said: *a better place.*

The left-hand side had no words in it at all. It had a reference. Letters, a dash, numbers.

I sat down then, in my coat. I'd said that phrase at a grave in the spring, or one of its cousins, in a warm voice, along with sixty other people. And I knew the reference. I got the thin book out and opened it at the last page and put it beside the photocopy, and it was the same one, character for character. Whatever people used to say before they said *a better place*, somebody had thought it safer not to type it even on a page for level 2 and above. They'd filed it and given the shelf. And Hendrik Maasen had written that shelf on the last page of his notebook, with a date and the word *Ask.*

The second page was about dates. *Where a date is contested, prefer the later.* *Where a sequence is contested, prefer the one in which the simpler precedes the more complex.* Each with a line of justification that sounded like every methods section I'd ever skimmed.

The third page had a heading.

*WHEN A READER NOTICES.*

Four paragraphs. The first said that a reader who notices a change between printings is to be treated as a bibliographical inquiry and answered as such: courteously, fully, with reference to the ordinary practice of the trade. The second said that a reader who *persists* is to be offered the opportunity to publish, in an appropriate venue, with an appropriate framing. The third said that a reader who persists after publication is to be *reviewed.*

The fourth paragraph was two lines long.

*In no case is a reader to be contradicted. Contradiction confirms. The preferred response to a correct reader is agreement, followed by reclassification.*

I read that one four times. Then I let the dogs out, because they'd been very patient, and stood in the garden in my coat watching Mora try to eat a leaf, and thought about Pieter in June, in his nice voice. *They asked for the person who reads things.* I'd been agreed with at stand-up and reclassified by the first of the month.

---

The math came the way everything comes to me, slowly, at a kitchen table, while I was trying to do something else.

Page one said *level 2 and above.* So there's a level one. So there are levels. And a document doesn't get a fourth revision unless it's been used, by enough people for enough years to wear out the first three. I've written fourth revisions. They're what happens when a thing is boring and necessary and has been for a decade.

So: how many people?

I'm not a statistician. I'm a man who counts breaths. I started with the ones I'd actually met. A dealer in Groningen with a telephone number. A neighbor in Deventer with a telephone number. A manager who told nine people a job was a trap, and nine people who believed him. Somebody who could rename a job. Somebody who could date a ticket in May.

None of them knew each other. That part I'd expected. What took me longer, and it's the only clever thing I worked out that month, was that they hadn't been told the same thing. The dealer and the neighbor got *conservation*, because you tell people who like old paper about old paper. Frank got *honeypot*, because you tell an engineer about security. Each reason was small, and true enough, and each one explained why you needn't ask anything further. Nobody in that list would need to know what the others were for. Nobody would even need to know there were others.

You couldn't do it with a cabal. A cabal can't keep a secret in a town. You'd do it with a handful of people who know and a great many who've each been given one task and one reason. In a company of five thousand you'd want somebody for the tickets, somebody for the job, somebody to tell a manager what to say. Call it ten, to be safe. One in five hundred.

In a town this size that's a hundred and sixty people, and most of them were asked to do one small thing once by somebody pleasant, and did it, and have never thought of it since. The woman with the bicycle is one. She took the bins in for him.

I could feel what the number wanted from me. It wanted me in a submarine. Stop ringing my mother, skip the beer after work, give Beard Man short answers, and treat everyone on the surface as possibly the man with the van. I got as far as not answering one text from Frank. But going quiet isn't a check. It's just being frightened with a reason, and page three had handed me something I could actually test.

---

The thing with Ana happened over breakfast on the Monday, and it was my fault.

"You were up at four," she said. Not a question.

"The payments thing."

She buttered toast. She's a careful butterer, corner to corner, and she did the whole slice before she said anything else.

"The payments thing finished in June. You told me. You said *good catch* in a voice and did the thing with your hands."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything.

"I don't mind that you're up at four," Ana said. "I mind that you've started having a thing you tell me and a thing you don't. I can see the join. I've always been able to see the join, that's the entire reason you married me, you said so, on the third date, you said *you're the only person who's ever caught me.*" She put the knife down. "So. Caught. What's the four o'clock thing?"

And I had enough by then. I could have put the three pages on the table and the thin book next to them and said: *I think somebody keeps the wording of things, and I think they've noticed me.* She'd have said *okay, Mulder* and asked a hundred questions, and whatever came after would have come with two of us in it.

"Reading," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her toast and ate it, corner to corner, and went to work, and didn't kiss the top of my head. She was quiet for a day. One day. She was perfectly nice the whole time, and by the evening I'd have paid anything to be shouted at.

One in five hundred. And the only person I was certain wasn't in, I'd just put on the outside.

---

That night I set up the test the way I'd set one up at work. When you want to know what a system does with a certain kind of input, you send it that input, and you write down what you expect before you look.

So I wrote it in the Record first, with the date. If page three was real, the answer would be courteous and full. It would refer to the ordinary practice of the trade. It would agree with me, and it would not contradict a single word. If instead I got two people saying *interesting* and a third calling me an idiot, then Hendrik Maasen had hidden a joke under his wife's lining paper, and I could go back to golf.

Then I opened the forum, under the name they already had, and posted the plain version. This time it was a question about a sentence, with nobody dead in it.

*Has anyone compared the two impressions of this 1926 survey, p. 118? First impression: "transmitted to the early." Second, 1931: "arisen among the first." Two typos and a plate flaw are uncorrected in both. Photographs attached.*

Dry as toast. Buddy was asleep on my foot under the table. I knew it would be on a list by a quarter past two, with my number beside it and a two-word reason, and for once I'd picked the two words myself.

I pressed the button and went to bed.
