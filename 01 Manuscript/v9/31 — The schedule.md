# The schedule

It came to me because I'd signed for it. That's the only reason.

Earlier that autumn I'd reconciled Annex C and put my initials at the bottom, and in a bank your initials are a forwarding address. Whoever signs off the seat annex gets the program's quarterly paperwork to acknowledge. So on a Tuesday, between a capacity report and somebody's leaving card, there was an item in my queue called *Continuity transfer schedule, Q4*, thirty pages, with a button that said *Acknowledge*.

I'd expected a list of bunkers. I'd been reading too much. It was a list of *transfers.* Dates in the right-hand column, through the end of the quarter. In the left column, what was being transferred: records, mostly, by category, from the bank's own collections to the foundation's depot, each with a reference number, the way a careful institution moves things. *Deaccessioned.* *For conservation.* *For review.* Four hundred years of never throwing anything away, and here was the bank quietly sending things out of the building on a timetable.

And in the middle column, under a heading that just said *Visit*, addresses.

Not many. Eleven in the quarter. People who'd be *visited* in connection with the transfers, I assumed: readers of the records being moved, academics, dealers, the kind of people who'd notice a shelfmark had gone. One in Zwolle. One in Apeldoorn. One on a farm road I had to look up.

And one that was ours.

Our street. Our number. In a column headed *Visit*, with a date eleven days off, a Saturday, and in the notes field, where the other lines said things like *pension* and *estate*, one word:

*Companion.*

I knew the word. I'd initialed it. *Companion animals are not accommodated*, Annex C, a third of the way down, and I'd thought of Buddy in the car and signed anyway.

I printed the page. I acknowledged the item, because not acknowledging it would have been the louder thing to do. Then I went home.

---

I didn't show her. I asked her to go to her mother's for a couple of weeks and I didn't say why, which is the stupidest way I know to ask Ana anything.

"No."

"Just until the—"

"No. You don't get to send me to Mum's like a parcel. Is this the thing from the kitchen floor? The run thing?"

"Not yet."

"Then no."

That was dinner. Afterward I went to let the dogs out, and I left my coat on the kitchen table, over the page, because I am apparently a man who hides things under coats. When I came back in Ana was standing at the table with the coat in one hand and the page in the other, reading. She'd read it upside down first, through the gap. She reads better upside down than most people the right way up.

She didn't say anything for a long time. Then she put her finger on a line.

"Why is our address on this?"

"Ana."

"It's a work thing. It's got the bank's name on it. Why is our *house* on a work thing, in a column that says visit." Her finger moved. "What's companion."

I could have said I didn't know. I'd been saying it for months, each time lying by exactly the width of a word I was too frightened to say.

"It's the dog," I said.

Ana looked at me.

"It's a word from a contract at work. A list of people who get a desk if the building burns down, and their families. There's a line in it that says companion animals aren't included. I signed it." I heard my own voice and it was very flat. "Somebody has put that word next to our house, next to a date. I don't know who. I don't know what a visit is. I think it's Julian. I don't know that either."

"Julian sells fire drills."

"Julian asked about Buddy before he asked my name."

She sat down. Not in a chair. On the floor, by the radiator, where Buddy was, and she put her hand on his head the way I do when I'm thinking. I'd never seen her do it before. She'd got it from me, the way I'd got the chin-on-the-head from her. We'd been trading gestures for eleven years without noticing, and this was the first one I'd given her that she didn't want.

"James," she said.

That's the first time. Eleven years. Mulder, Scully, *you*, *hey*, *dickhead*, with love. Never James. It landed in the kitchen like a plate.

"I'm not leaving you. I want to be really clear about that, because you're going to do the thing where you decide what I meant. I'm not leaving *you.*" She stroked the dog. "I'm leaving the *address.* The address is on a list. I'm not stupid, I can read a list. And I'm taking Mora, because Mora barks at the postman and if somebody's coming to visit a dog I'd rather it was the one that bites." She looked at Buddy. "I can't take him. Mum's flat is up two flights. You know he can't."

"I know."

"So you keep him. And you keep *that*" — the page — "and whatever's in the folder called Reading, and you sort it out, or you don't, and either way you ring me every night at nine, and if you don't ring I'm coming back with my brother and a bat." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I don't believe any of it. I want to say that too. I don't believe there's a *they.* I think you've found a thing that doesn't fit, like you always do, and you've pulled it, like you always do, and this time something on the other end pulled back, and it's a *person*, not a *they*, and he was in our garden drinking our beer, and I liked him, and I'm so angry about that I can't see."

"Ana—"

"*Don't.* Don't do the voice. Don't tell me a did-you-know. You haven't told me one since the pub and I noticed. It's the first thing you've ever stopped doing without telling me, and I've been waiting for you to *see* that I'd noticed." She stood up. "Help me pack the dog."

---

We packed the dog. Mora's bowl, Mora's lead, Mora's toy, which she carried to the car herself, wet, in a state of joy, because the car means the course or the vet and both are the best thing that has ever happened. Ana's bag took four minutes. She's always been able to leave a house in four minutes. Her grandmother taught her, she said once. It's what a woman learns when the situation at home isn't ideal: where the bag is.

At the door she stopped. The hat was on its hook. She looked at it for a second and didn't touch it, and that was somehow the worst part.

"Nine o'clock," she said.

"Nine o'clock."

"Every night."

"Every night."

She put her hand on my face. Not squeezing. Then she took it away and went down the path with the Doberman pulling her toward the best thing that had ever happened, and I stood in the door with an old dog leaning on my leg and watched my wife leave the address.

The car went. The street was quiet. Somewhere, a bin.

---

So I did what I do when I'm given a date. I got ready for it.

I gave the watcher the eleven addresses. It's the same build, with one more thing to read: the public notices, which in this country means the family announcements in the regional papers, the houses going up for sale, the businesses being wound up. Everything a street says about itself in print. If anything was published about any of the eleven, it would tell me at 02:14 with the forum.

I rang the vet and booked Buddy into the kennel, Thursday to Monday, around the Saturday. The girl asked if we were going away. I said sort of. If somebody was coming to visit a dog that Saturday, the dog would be in a building with a night nurse and a locked door.

I told Pieter I'd be working from home for two weeks. He said fine a little too quickly, the way he says most things to me now.

And I put a camera over the front door. Forty euros, the size of an egg. It sends a picture to my phone when anything moves on the path, which so far has meant the postman, a cat, and me, looking up at it.

That was the plan. The house would never be empty by day, the car would be in the drive, the door would be watched, and on the one day that mattered the dog wouldn't be there.

I sat down on the floor by the radiator, where she'd sat, and put my hand on Buddy's head.

"Eleven days," I told him. "We can do eleven days."

He didn't have a theory. I had a date.
