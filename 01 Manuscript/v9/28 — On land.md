# On land

The pub by the course has a back terrace with heaters, and on a Saturday in November you can sit out till nine if you keep your coat on and lie to yourself about the temperature. Ana had come to meet us. Her idea. She'd said on the phone, *I'll come for the last hour, I want to see you two being idiots*, and here we were, and we'd been idiots for eighteen holes and she'd arrived in time for the beer.

Buddy was under the table on a folded towel the barman keeps for him. Julian had found that out before I did, which is the kind of thing he finds out.

Inside, over the bar, a television with the sound down was showing a hearing. Men in suits behind a long table, a caption with the word *phenomena* in it. It had been going for weeks. A pilot would describe something he'd seen with his own eyes, and then a man from an agency would say that a review had found nothing, and then another pilot, and then another review. Each one paired. Nobody in the pub was watching. I'd stopped watching it too, which I noticed, and which I'd have called progress if I hadn't just read what a style guide says about readers who go quiet.

Ana was on her second glass and telling him about the toaster.

"Three weekends," she said. "*Three.* Every wire in the house. I'm standing in my own kitchen holding a toaster like a woman in a hostage video."

"Did he find it?" Julian said.

"He found a *label.* Somebody had labeled a fuse wrong in nineteen ninety-something." She squeezed my arm. "He was so happy. I've never seen him happier. Not on our wedding day."

"Fair," I said.

"That's a good day," Julian said, and meant it, and smiled at me over his glass. "It's the same with old wiring as with old wine. You don't fix it in place, you decant it. Pour the whole house into new copper and leave the sediment in the walls."

Ana laughed. I laughed a beat late, and I'll tell you why.

*Decanting.* The professor had written it in a margin, in pencil, in the thin book, next to entry nineteen, and I'd had to look it up on the Tuesday, because he hadn't meant wine. He'd used it the way you'd use it for a library: taking a collection out of one building and pouring it into another, so that it keeps its order and loses its address. It's a word people in his trade use. I had never heard it said aloud in my life, and I'd now heard it twice in one week, once from a dead man and once from a man who sells desks.

That can happen. Words do that. You learn one on Tuesday and the world is suddenly full of it. I know that effect; it has a name, and I've used it on other people for years.

But I've also spent eleven years at a bank being paid to notice when two systems that shouldn't know each other start using the same word, and my job has one rule about it. You don't argue with the coincidence. You send it something and see what comes back.

---

So I set a trap, the way I'd set one at work. If you want to know whether a system is really reading a feed or just saying it does, you put something in the feed that isn't true and wait to see if it gets repeated.

I let the toaster story finish. I let him tell one about a client in Antwerp. Then, when Ana had gone in to find the bathroom, I said, as a man says things on a terrace:

"You read, don't you. Old stuff. You said once."

"When I can. Why?"

"I picked something up this week I think you'd like. Dutch, 1950s, a little book about the Leiden printing houses between the wars. *The Quiet Presses.* Van der Berg, I think. Hard to find."

There is no such book. I'd made it on the spot out of three words I liked, and I'd never say it twice, which is the point of a test input: you have to be able to recognize your own.

He didn't pause. That's what I keep coming back to. There wasn't the half-second a man takes to search his memory and come up empty and decide whether to admit it.

"Oh, that one," Julian said. "Yes. Dry as a biscuit. The chapter on the type foundries is the good bit." He drank. "You're going to need a lamp for that typeface."

I said I expected so.

Ana came back out and sat down and put her cold hands on my neck, and I made the noise you make, and Julian laughed, and we talked about a dog at the club that had eaten a scorecard. Nothing happened to his face. Nothing happened to mine either; I've had years of practice at a table. The heaters ticked. On the television behind the bar another man in uniform described a light and another man in a suit described a review.

Here's where I'd got to by the second heater cycle. Either a man had just been polite about a book, the way you nod at a title at a party, and I'd caught a friend in a kindness. Or he had a reason to be seen to have read what I read, and the reason was not that he liked old print. The first one I'd have believed of most people. He'd given me the chapter on type foundries, though. You don't invent a chapter to be polite. You invent a chapter when the question is a test and you know it is, and you'd rather pass loudly than fail quietly.

I didn't know which. I still didn't know in the car. What I knew was that I'd lied to a friend on a terrace, and made up a book, and that if I was wrong about him I'd just done something small and ugly to the one person in eleven years who'd asked about my dog before my name. And if I was right, then he knew I'd tested him, because a man who nods at a title that doesn't exist has either heard of it or hasn't, and he'd made his choice with his eyes open. That title was out of my hands now. I couldn't take it back and I couldn't use it twice.

"Same time Wednesday?" he said, standing, putting a note under his glass.

"Wednesday."

"Bring the biscuit book."

He kissed Ana on the cheek. She let him. He crouched by the table and said something to Buddy, who did not take his chin off his paws, and he laughed at that, the way you laugh at an old dog's dignity, and went.

---

"I like him," Ana said, in the car. "He listens. Men don't listen like that."

"No."

"You were quiet."

"Eighteen holes."

"You were quiet in the way where you've found a label." She looked at me. "Don't do the face. I'm just saying. You two have been playing for five months and I've met him once, on a terrace, in the dark, in a coat. I want to see him in daylight with other people. See if he's real."

"He's real."

"Then it'll be easy." She watched the ring road go past. "A barbecue. Next Saturday, before it's too cold. Frank. That man from your forum you keep saying you'll have round. Henk from two doors down, so it isn't weird. And golf man."

I'd been trying, since the second heater cycle, to think how I could get Julian in a room with other people and a control. Somebody who hadn't read the thin book. Somebody who'd hear a made-up title and say *never heard of it*, which is what honest people say. Frank, for instance, who'd put it in the right-hand column before the sausages were done.

"Yes," I said. "Good idea."

"That was fast."

"It's a good idea."

She looked at me a while longer, and then let it go, and turned the radio on. I drove. Buddy slept in the back. I didn't tell her why I'd said yes so quickly, and she knew I hadn't, and that was one more thing I hadn't told her, which by then was a list of its own.
