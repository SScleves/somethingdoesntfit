# Down House, 1860

The water cure at Ilkley involved being wrapped in wet sheets and left to think, which was the last thing he needed.

He had been there since October, in a rented house on the edge of the moor with the whole family, and the book had come out without him. Twelve hundred and fifty copies, and the booksellers had taken the lot before it reached a shop, which the publisher reported as a triumph and which Darwin received, in a wet sheet, as a kind of exposure. He had worked on it for twenty years and let it go in thirteen months, and now it was out in the shops in its green cloth, saying what it said, and people were reading it in railway carriages, and he was in Yorkshire with a rash.

The letters came up by the afternoon post. Some of them he had expected. Sedgwick, who had taught him geology and whom he loved, wrote that he had read the book with more pain than pleasure, and that parts of it had made him laugh until his sides ached, and other parts he had read with sorrow, because he thought them utterly false and grievously mischievous. *Mischievous* is a word for a boy who has broken something.

Murray wanted a second edition at once. The first was gone and the trade was asking. There was time for small corrections only. A few weeks.

So in a cold house, in the last days of November and the first of December 1859, between the sheets and the sitz baths, with his stomach doing what his stomach did, Charles Darwin sat down with a copy of his own book and a pencil and went looking for the places where he could give a little ground.

---

He did not change the argument. That should be said plainly, because the scholars have said it since, and it is true: the second edition is the first edition with its sleeves rolled down. Most of the corrections are the kind any author makes when he reads himself in print for the first time and winces. A misprint. A date. A sentence that had got away from him.

But there was one sentence, and it was the last one.

In the first edition it ends like this: *There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one; and that, whilst this planet has gone cycling on according to the fixed law of gravity, from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.*

*Breathed into a few forms or into one.* He had chosen that carefully. It let the reader supply the lungs.

In the second edition the sentence has three more words. *Having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one.*

That was all. Three words, in the last sentence of a five-hundred-page book, where a man reading quickly would take them as the kind of thing one says. If you had the two editions side by side you would need to be looking for it.

He also added, facing the title page, a third quotation to sit with the two already there. It was from Bishop Butler, on the meaning of the word *natural*: a bishop's worth of respectability opposite his own name.

The second edition was published on the seventh of January, 1860. Three thousand copies. He was back at Down by then, in his own study with the mirror angled at the window so he could see who was coming up the drive. And there it was on the last page, in type, as if it had always been there.

---

Here is the part that matters, and it is in a letter, and anyone can read it.

Three years later, in March 1863, he wrote to Joseph Hooker, his closest friend, about a review that had annoyed him. Partway through the letter he said this:

*But I have long regretted that I truckled to public opinion & used Pentateuchal term of creation, by which I really meant "appeared" by some wholly unknown process.*

Truckled. It is not a word you use about yourself unless you mean it.

He never took the three words out. Every edition after the second kept them, through the sixth and last in 1872, and the sixth is the one that went on being reprinted, the one in the cheap editions and the school libraries and the quotations. For most of the people who read the most famous last sentence in science over the next hundred years, the Creator was always in it. The first printing, the one without, survives in rare-book rooms, mostly behind glass.

So the record is this. A man wrote a sentence that said one thing. Sick, in a cold house, with the letters coming up by the afternoon post, he changed it to say something softer, and let it go out under the same title. And then, privately, to one friend, he said what he had really meant, and the word he used for what he had done was a word for giving way.

Nobody made him do it. That is the uncomfortable part, and it should not be smoothed over. No visitor came to Ilkley with a card. There was only a quiet, decent, frightened man with a pencil, who knew what the sentence should say, and knew what it would cost, and chose.

He said he regretted it once that we know of, in a letter, to a friend, and never in print.

It is worth being exact about the direction, because it is the opposite of the one on James's kitchen table. Darwin put a hand into his sentence: *by the Creator*. The printer in Leiden, seventy years later, took one out of Tresidder's. And Darwin did it himself, under his own name, in an edition openly sold as a new one, where anyone with both copies can see it. What the two cases share is smaller than that. A plain sentence in a careful book changed between printings, and nearly everyone since has read the second version without knowing there was a first.

The letter to Hooker is kept with Darwin's papers at Cambridge University Library, and the text is online, where anyone can read it. The first edition is in reading rooms around the world. If you have ever stood in one of those rooms and asked to see it, somebody wrote your name in a book.
