# The other ten

Saturday came, the date I'd been guarding, and nothing happened on it. I sat in the kitchen from seven in the morning with the camera open on my phone and my dog in a kennel across town, and the postman came, and a cat. On Monday I collected Buddy, who walked out slower than he'd gone in but under his own power, and the girl on the desk said he'd been no trouble, which I already knew.

Noor's phone rang out on Friday. On Saturday it went to a message. On Sunday it did nothing at all.

Then on Wednesday, at 02:14, my own phone buzzed on the kitchen floor, where I'd more or less moved since the vet.

The watcher isn't supposed to wake me. It leaves a report for the morning. But I'd given the addresses a louder setting than the forum, the way you do at work with the one system that matters, and this was the first time it had used it.

*1 new notice. Family announcements. Match: Boersma, Zwolle.*

It was a thank-you.

*For the many cards, the flowers and the kind words after the death of my dear husband Henk Boersma, my warmest thanks. They have been a great comfort. M. Boersma, Zwolle.*

People put those in the regional paper here, a few weeks after a funeral, when the cards have stopped coming and they've run out of ways to answer them one at a time. I lay on the floor and read it three times. Then I went looking for the notice it was thanking people for, which took four minutes. Henk Boersma, seventy-three. *Suddenly.* October. The date on the schedule against that address was three days before he died.

The watcher hadn't missed it. It had done exactly what I built it to do, which was look forward. I'd given it eleven addresses and told it to tell me about anything new, because I was guarding a date, and it had not occurred to me, the professional, to ask the first thing I ask about any system I'm handed. What happened before I got here?

I'd had the page for two weeks. I'd looked at it the way you look at a bill you can't pay, which is quickly, and only at the line with your own name on.

Eleven addresses, and one of them was mine. That left ten.

---

I did it the dull way, because the dull way is the only way I trust.

Public records. Death notices, which in this country are published in the regional papers and online, and are searchable if you know the format and have the patience. The land registry, which tells you who owns a house and when it changed hands, and charges you for each answer. The chamber of commerce, for anyone with a business. Houses for sale. Church newsletters, which are all online now and which nobody thinks of as a record. The dullest layer of a country, the layer that never makes the news, where a life leaves marks the way a glass leaves rings.

The schedule gave addresses, and addresses in this country are enough.

It took three evenings.

Zwolle I had. The regional paper gave it four paragraphs in October: the canal road toward Hasselt, a Sunday morning, an old green Volvo, no other vehicle. The police were quoted as saying the driver may have been distracted.

The second was in Apeldoorn. A woman in her fifties. Not dead. The chamber of commerce showed her translation agency dissolved in October. The land registry showed the house sold three weeks after the date on the schedule, quickly, for less than the neighbors had got. Then nothing. No new address in any record I could reach.

The third was a village outside Raalte. A retired teacher, a widower, seventy-eight. Not dead either. His church's newsletter wished him well in his new room at a care home forty kilometers away and said he would be glad of cards. The house was listed nine days after the date on the schedule. The photographs still had his books in them.

Three. Each one plausible. I said that to myself at the time and it's true. A man of seventy-three on a road he knew too well. A woman selling up. An old man who couldn't manage his stairs. If you handed those three to anyone, a policeman, a journalist, Frank, they'd shrug. Three things happened in three towns in the east of the country in one autumn. That's not a pattern. That's a region.

The fourth, fifth, sixth and seventh had gone quiet.

That's the only word I've got for it. No death notice. No sale. No dissolved company. But nothing new either, in any record, since the date on the schedule. In two of the four there was a last trace that bothered me: an account that had posted every few days for years and stopped; a sports club's newsletter that had listed a man as treasurer for a decade and in its November issue listed someone else, with no thanks and no farewell.

Four people I couldn't find and couldn't not find.

The eighth, ninth and tenth had dates still to come. The nearest was the twenty-eighth: a street in Hengelo, a woman's name alone on the land registry since 1987. I wrote the three dates down.

So: three resolved, four quiet, three to come. I count things for a living and I know what random looks like. It's clumpier than people think. You get runs that mean nothing. This wasn't clumpy. This was every past date on a list followed within three weeks by a plausible ending or by silence. That's not a run. That's a schedule being kept.

And it was the first thing in all of this that nobody had handed me. The thin book came in a crate. The folder came from Els. The job, the annex, the schedule itself: everything I had, somebody had put where I'd look. This I had pulled myself, out of records anyone can read. I'd have liked to think nobody knew I'd done it. The land registry sends a receipt for every lookup, with your name on.

---

On Thursday I rang the workshop. Noor rents a press at a bindery in Leiden and the number is on her invoices. A man answered.

"She's not taking private work anymore."

"Since when?"

"Friday. Institutions only. If it's a family Bible I can give you another name."

"Is she all right?"

"She's working." A pause. "She said somebody might ring and ask that, and that I was to say she's working. So I've said it."

I thanked him. I'd been to her house twice, with my face, and rung her from my own phone, and somebody had spent a Friday making sure she would stop answering it. That was the bill for the shelfmark, and it had gone to her.

At nine I rang Ana and told her Buddy was eating. She asked what I was doing tomorrow.

"Driving to Zwolle. To ask a woman a question."

"What question?"

"I'll tell you when I know if it was the right one."

She let that go, which she wouldn't have a month ago. "Take Buddy."

"He's barely out of the kennel."

"He's old and he sits in the car and he makes you look like a man with a dog instead of a man with a question. Take Buddy."

I took Buddy.

---

Zwolle is a nice town and the house was in the nice part of it, a decent house on a street of decent houses, and there was a green Volvo in the drive, old, immaculate, the kind of car that gets washed on Sundays. I hadn't expected the car. I don't know why. They'd pulled it out of a canal and somebody had brought it home.

I'd thought about what to say for the whole drive and thrown all of it away by the time I rang the bell. I'd decided to say I was from the pension fund. I'd decided to say I was a journalist. Standing on the step with an old dog sitting at my heel I found I was going to say the forty percent of the truth that fits in a doorway.

She was small and neat and seventy, and her name, she told me later, was Marijke, and she looked at me, and then at Buddy, and her face did the thing people's faces do when they see an old dog, which is soften before they've decided to.

"I'm sorry to bother you," I said. "My name's James. I saw your notice in the paper. I'd like to ask about something your husband used to say. I don't work for anyone. I've got a dog, in case that helps."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at the Volvo.

"You'd better come in," she said. "He likes a kitchen, does he? Henk's did."

Buddy went under her table and lay down against her feet, the way he does with people he's decided about. She put her hand down and found his head without looking.

---

She told me a good deal that I'm keeping, because she told it to me, and some of it was just a woman talking about her husband to a stranger with a dog.

Henk's father had been a draftsman, and he'd had a thing he said. Henk had heard it as a boy and taken it for a story about the war, and had thought about it, on and off, for sixty years. Then in the spring his brother died, and Henk came home from the funeral and sat in the car in the drive for an hour, and when he came in he said: it isn't Father's. It's mine.

"And after that he said it to everybody. The bowling club. The man who does the gutters. They'd started looking at him. I told him, Henk, leave it. Nobody wants to hear it."

"What did he say? The words. Can you remember how he put it?"

"I can say it in my sleep. He said it the same way every time. That's what frightened me."

"Would you mind if I wrote it down?"

She looked at the notebook, and at me, and I think that was the moment she decided. Nobody had written anything of Henk's down except a policeman.

She said it. I wrote it. I read it back to her and she stopped me on one word. I'd written *afraid*.

"Frightened," she said. "He said frightened."

I crossed it out and wrote it again and read her the whole thing.

*It was a bridge, in the rain. Somebody had hold of my hand and said not to be frightened, only to hold on.*

"That's it," Marijke said. "That's all of it. He never had anymore than that. A bridge, he didn't know where. Rain. And a hand." She turned her cup. "And then he'd sit on his own. His right hand. He'd put it under his leg like a boy who's been told not to touch something. He said it went cold. I bought him gloves, in May."

I kept my face still.

"Did anybody ask him about it? Apart from the bowling club."

"The man from the pension." She said it without any weight. "In October, while I was at my sister's. He rang ahead and he came when he said and he wiped his feet. Henk told me on the phone that night. *A nice man, he listened, he liked the car.* He'd brought his own coffee in a flask and apologized for it. He borrowed the Volvo for an hour to move a box, and brought it back washed. And Henk said, he was so pleased, he said: Marijke, he asked me if I recalled anything else. Nobody asks me that."

She got up and took something down from the shelf above the coats and put it on the table between us. Steel. Unmarked. The kind you can buy in any shop.

"He left that. Nobody came back for it. The pension people said they'd never sent anyone. I thought, well, somebody did." She looked at it. "Three days after, Henk went out along the canal road while I was at church. They said distracted. I had the car checked, afterward, by the man in Kampen that Henk trusted. He had it a whole day. He told me it was the best-kept Volvo he'd seen in twenty years, and he was sorry, he'd hoped to find something."

Buddy sighed under the table.

"He wasn't distracted," she said. "He was happy. That week. He'd stopped saying it for a while, because I'd asked him, and that week he started again, and I thought, oh, let him."

---

I had the page in my bag. Her address, a date, and in the notes field the word *pension*. I had two other addresses that had been tidied the way hers had, and four that had gone quiet, and a woman in Hengelo with two weeks. I could have put all of it on her kitchen table next to a flask.

I didn't, and for once I can say why. I have a rule. I tell nobody until I know what they were told and can put their own paper in front of them. I knew what she'd been told: *distracted*. But the page in my bag wasn't her paper. It was the bank's, with my acknowledgment on it. The last time I told somebody a true thing with nothing of their own to rest it on, she wrote a kind note about my sleep. So I told Marijke nothing, and it wasn't only cowardice this time.

At the door she said, "You wrote it down like it was worth something."

"It is."

"Then tell me one thing. Is he somewhere? Henk." She had her hand on the frame. "You came about what he said. Is he somewhere?"

She'd let me into her kitchen and given me her husband's sentence, and that was the question it cost her to ask, and I didn't have it.

"I don't know," I said.

"No," she said. "The minister didn't either, he just took longer." She went back in, and came out again with the flask, and pushed it at me. "Take it. I don't want it in the house now. You'll know what to do with it."

I didn't. I took it.

She looked at the Volvo, and at the dog, and at me. "You're not from anyone. I believe that. You've got the face of a man who's not from anyone." And she closed the door gently, the way you do when there's an old dog on the step.

---

I sat in the car for a while before I started it. Buddy was asleep on the back seat. The flask was on the passenger seat and the notebook was open on my knee.

A bridge, in the rain. A hand. Somebody saying not to be frightened.

I'd had cold mud, and a low gray sky, and a hand, at a funeral in the spring, for the length of a camera flash. I'd filed it under no sleep. I'd filed it more than once. And here was a retired engineer in Zwolle I'd never met, who'd had a place, and weather, and a hand, after a funeral, and had been asked very politely whether he recalled anything else.

One of those is a tired man at a grave. I didn't have a folder for two.

I closed the notebook and put it in the glovebox. Then I took it out again and put it in my coat.
