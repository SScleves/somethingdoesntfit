# The payload

There's a thing engineers do when someone tells them to leave something alone, and it isn't disobedience. Pieter would call it disobedience and he'd be wrong, and the difference is the only defense I've got.

If a job runs on your systems and you don't know what it does, you don't open it. Opening it is a decision. You don't stop it, you don't change it, you don't touch it. You stand next to it while it runs and you watch what comes out. I've done that a thousand times to things I was allowed to look at, and nobody ever called it anything but Tuesday.

So on the night of the stand-up I set an alarm for ten past two, and went downstairs in my socks, and opened the laptop on the kitchen table, where the two copies of page 118 still lived in their folder.

Buddy came and lay across my feet. He does that when I'm up at the wrong time. He doesn't ask why. He just makes sure that if I get up to do something stupid, I'll have to move a dog first.

What I did will satisfy nobody. The technical people will find it too vague and everyone else will find it too much. I watched the door the job sends things through, and when it sent something, I kept a copy. I didn't break anything. I didn't log in as anyone I'm not. The data went where it always goes. It also went into a file on my laptop called, I'm embarrassed to say, `nothing.txt`, because if Ana came down for water I wanted the screen to tell the truth.

Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds. Forty-one kilobytes, which is nothing. A photo of the dogs is a hundred times that. Forty-one kilobytes is about what a person could read in the time it takes to make coffee.

I opened it expecting noise. Diagnostics look like noise: timestamps, hexadecimal, the digestive sounds of a system talking to itself. Pieter had said *diagnostic artifact*, and the part of me that has spent thirty years losing arguments with paperwork expected to find exactly that and go back to bed the fool.

It was a list.

---

A hundred and thirteen lines. Each line was a date, then an employee number, the eight digits we all have on the back of our badges, then two words in lowercase, like a note somebody writes to himself. I'll leave the dates off.

`40217733	posted (forum)`
`40193058	wrote (dealer)`
`40221904	asked (hr)`
`40188120	searched (archive)`
`40209417	recalled (family)`

I read all hundred and thirteen with Buddy on my feet. Then I read them again, and the second time I wasn't reading the numbers. I was reading the verbs.

Posted. Wrote. Asked. Searched. Requested. Raised.

Every one of them was a thing a person does with words, to somebody else, on purpose. Nobody on the list had *thought* anything. You can't put a thought in a file. Every line was a moment when somebody in my company had stopped, and typed, or picked up a pen, or opened their mouth in a meeting, and put a question where it could be read. The thing in brackets was where.

There was one verb I couldn't place. *Recalled.* Seven lines of it, with *(family)* or *(colleague)* or *(hr)* after it. I work for a bank. In a bank a recall is a payment you sent by mistake and want back, and there's a whole team for it, with a form. So I decided it was a product term that had wandered into the wrong file, and I moved on. That's how good I am at this.

The dates went back thirty days. New lines go at the bottom. Whoever wrote the thing that writes it was tidy. So I scrolled to the bottom, and the last line had that day's date on it, and it was:

`40176502	asked (job)`

My number is 40176502. I know it the way you know your own phone number, because I've typed it into a hundred forms.

I didn't panic. I didn't feel watched, not yet. That came later, in a different room. What I felt, at my kitchen table with my own badge number on a list I wasn't supposed to have, was the cold, almost pleasant thing I get at work when a system finally tells me what it is. Because it had, in two words. Nobody on the list had broken a rule. The job didn't count what you did or what you knew or what you thought at four in the morning on a kitchen floor. It counted whether you'd turned it into words and handed the words to someone. I'd said eleven of them in a glass box that morning, and by a quarter past two the next morning there I was, with a reason beside my number that was exactly true.

I looked at the bracket. Not *(forum)*, not *(hr)*. Somebody had a category for people who asked about the thing itself. I was the only one in it.

---

That would have been enough for one night. I'd like to say I went to bed.

What I did was the thing I should have done first. You don't scroll for your own number. You search for it, because scrolling finds what you expect and searching finds what's there.

Two results.

The second was the one I'd just read. The first was most of a page higher up, dated the Monday of the week before. Eleven days before I opened my mouth in stand-up.

`40176502	wrote (dealer)`

I sat there for a while. Buddy's weight on my feet was the only thing in the kitchen I was sure of.

I'd written to three dealers on that Monday. From this table, on this laptop, from my own email address, with a dog under the chair. I hadn't mentioned it at work. I hadn't mentioned it to Ana until a letter came. There is no system in that bank that knows I own a book, and I should know, because I watch them.

So the job wasn't only reading the bank. Whatever made this list could be told things from outside. And somebody outside, on a Monday in June, had told it that an employee with my number had written to a dealer.

Years ago I read about a technician in San Francisco, an ordinary one, the kind of man who reads the wiring diagrams because somebody has to, who found a locked room on the sixth floor of his own building where the internet was being copied through a splitter to a government agency. He found it because he read the documents nobody read. I'd always liked that story, the way you like stories about people who are a bit like you, from a safe distance. I didn't like it that night. That night I thought about what happened to him after, which was lawyers, and a long time of being called a crank, and then being right, which as far as I can tell didn't help.

I closed the laptop. I didn't delete `nothing.txt`. I renamed it, which is the kind of brave I am.

---

I had one capture and a theory, and one capture isn't much.

At work, when you want to know what a monitor is really watching, you don't read its manual. You feed it something and see whether it shows up. A test input. It has to be something only you sent, at a time only you know, so that if it comes out the other end there's no arguing about where it came from.

I spent most of Saturday pretending I didn't know what my test input was.

In the spring I'd typed a question into a thread on a forum, looked at it for five minutes, and deleted it. I'd had ten reasons. The real one was that people who post questions like that get a label and the label doesn't come off, and I'd been lurking on that forum for twelve years under a name that's a joke only I understand, posting the safe kind of did-you-know, precisely so that I'd never have one.

Nobody at the bank knows that name. Ana doesn't know that name.

At eleven that night, with Ana asleep and Mora upstairs in my place on the bed, which she takes the second I leave it and defends like a tenant with rights, I logged in at the kitchen table and typed it again. Same thread. Same words.

**Has anyone here had an NDE and is willing to talk about it?**

I looked at it for a good deal less than five minutes this time, because this time it wasn't a confession. It was a test input, and I'm a professional. Then I posted it, and it turned out to be a confession anyway. There it sat, under my name, where anyone could see me looking. Fuck.

By one o'clock it had three replies. One was kind. One said it was all the pineal gland. One was a link to a retreat in Portugal.

At fourteen minutes past two I kept a copy of what went through the door.

A hundred and fourteen lines. I searched.

`40176502	wrote (dealer)`
`40176502	asked (job)`
`40176502	posted (forum)`

Three hours and fourteen minutes, from my kitchen to their list. A forum name I've never typed on a bank machine, tied to my badge number before I'd gone to bed.

---

Here is what I had by Sunday morning, and what I'd paid for it.

I had three rows. I printed them on the home printer, the three lines and nothing else, and put the sheet in the folder with page 118. The first row had cost me nothing I knew about. The second had cost me eleven words. The third had cost me the one thing I'd been careful about for twelve years, and I'd handed it over myself, on purpose, to find out whether they'd take it. They took it. So the bank, or whoever the bank sends its forty-one kilobytes to, now knew what I call myself when I think nobody's looking, and what I ask about.

I knew what the list counted. I didn't know what it was for. A list of people who ask is a strange thing to want. It isn't thieves or leakers. It's the curious, a hundred-odd of them in one bank, counted nightly. Either that's the most pointless piece of software ever written or it's one gear of something.

And I had two questions, which is one more than I can usually carry.

The first was the letter. Three people on earth knew I'd written about that book, and all three sold books for a living. One never answered, one sent me four words, and one had told me, on good paper, to be plain about my reasons.

The second was the book itself. Nobody counts a man for buying a novel. If writing to a shop about one dull title from 1931 was worth a row, then that title mattered to somebody, and all I had to show for it was one changed sentence in one copy, found on a kitchen floor at four in the morning. Before I asked anybody anything I needed to know whether it was my copy or the whole run, and whether it was only this book.

The first question needed a drive. The second only needed a ruler, and the copy from Groningen was paid for and on its way.

I got into bed. Mora moved over by one dog's width, which is her maximum concession. Ana was asleep on her side with her hand under her cheek. I lay there next to her and didn't tell her, and I already knew that not telling her was going to be a bigger problem than the list.
