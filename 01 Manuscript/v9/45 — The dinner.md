# The dinner

Ana had been home four days and I wanted one ordinary Friday.

That was the whole plan. Pasta, the kind with the jar. A series she'd watched half of at her mother's and would have to explain to me. Mora upside down on the kitchen floor, and Buddy on his blanket under the table with his chin on my foot, which he does more since the vet. On Monday I'd sat in a reading room and said no to five seats. Nothing had happened on Tuesday, or Wednesday, or Thursday. I'd started to think no might be a thing you could just say.

The bell went at ten past seven.

Buddy lifted his head. He didn't get up. He made a sound I hadn't heard from him in twelve years, low and steady, like a fridge that's about to die.

"Is that the dog?" Ana said.

"That's the dog."

She went to the door. I heard it open, and I heard her say "Julian!" in the voice she keeps for people she's delighted to see, and I stood at the cooker with a wooden spoon and understood what you get when you turn down an old man's offer. You get the friend.

She knew who he was. I'd told her all of it at her mother's, with objects on a tablecloth, including the name Noor gave me and what had been in the bowl by the gate. She'd had three weeks to decide what she'd do if he ever stood on our step again, and what she did was take his coat.

I've thought about that since. If she'd shut the door he'd have learned that we knew. She opened it wide and he learned nothing. It took her about a second. I'd have stood there with the spoon.

"I was passing," he said, coming down the hall. Nobody passes our street. It doesn't go anywhere. "I've been a terrible friend, Jim. Three weeks. I brought a bottle to apologize, and then I thought, that's not enough for three weeks, so I brought two."

He looked the way he always looked, like a man who'd slept well in a good hotel. And he had his clubs on his shoulder.

"You brought your golf bag to dinner," Ana said.

"I came from the range. I'm not leaving these in a car, no offense to your street." He stood the bag by the front door on its two little legs and patted it like a horse. "They were built for me by a man in Scotland who's dead now. There are three people in this country I'd let regrip them, and one of those I'm not sure about."

It was a tan leather bag with a brass tag on the strap, and I'd wondered about it since our first Wednesday. I'm bad at golf, but I know what things cost. I looked at it by my front door the way you look at a car you don't know parked outside your house.

Under the table Buddy was still going.

"He's got a new noise," Kalder said.

"He's had a rough month," said Ana.

"He's allowed." He didn't crouch down this time. He sat where Ana pointed, and put his phone face down by his plate the way he always did, so you could see he wasn't looking at it. Mora, who has no principles at all, went and lay on his foot.

---

For twenty minutes he was just good company, and that was the hard part. He asked after Ana's mother by name. He told a story about a client in Antwerp who'd insured his wine cellar and not his wife. He ate the pasta from the jar and said it was exactly what he'd wanted, and I believed him, and Buddy growled through all of it at a volume you could talk over, like weather.

Then he turned his glass a quarter turn and said, "I hear you had a meeting on Monday."

"A short one."

"Short's fine. People think short means no." He smiled at Ana, to include her. "In my business nothing's a no until the renewal date. You'd be amazed how many clients say no in the first week of December and ring me on the thirtieth."

"What happens on the thirty-first?" I said.

"Allocation closes. After that it's whatever's left, and what's left is never by the window." He ate some pasta. "I'd hate that for you. You've got a nice desk."

"Is this golf?" Ana said.

"Work," we both said.

"Because it sounds like golf. It's got the same amount of nothing in it."

He laughed, properly, and topped up her glass. He's the only man I know who can pour for a woman and make it look like listening.

"The trouble with saying no in December," he said, "is the house. People don't think about the house. You've got an old place here, and it's lovely, but an old place in winter is a job. You can't leave it. Pipes. And you can't be in it every hour either, can you, not with work, not with the Wednesdays." He looked under the table, pleasantly. "Same with an old dog. You can't leave him and you can't watch him. They get into things."

Then he turned to Ana, and his whole face went kind.

"I hope Buddy's okay now," he said. "I should have asked that first."

Nobody in my house had told him Buddy was ill. I hadn't. Ana hadn't seen him since October. He knew that, and he knew I knew it, and he said it to my wife, gently, with her wine in his hand.

Ana didn't move a thing. "He's fine," she said. "He's eating like a horse. Do you want cheese on that?"

She got up for the cheese. On her way past the cupboard by the sink she stopped, for about as long as it takes to read a label, and went on.

---

I'm not good at code. I'd had eight months of other people's, and I was tired, and I answered in the only language where I don't have to think.

"Can I tell you what I do all day, Julian? You've never really asked."

"You watch things."

"I get paid to detect a problem. Not to fix it after. Anyone can fix it after, there's a whole floor for that. So the rule is that the slightest doubt gets looked at. You don't wait for a second one, and you don't decide the first one's nothing because you like the system." I put my fork down. "I've had one since the pub. The book you said you'd read."

He didn't pause. He's too good to pause. He just stopped chewing a little early.

"*The Quiet Presses*," I said. "Van der Berg. I made it up at the bar and you'd read it. You said the chapter on the type foundries was the good bit."

"Did I."

"Dry as a biscuit, you said. You were very fair about it."

"What book?" Ana said.

"Doesn't matter. It doesn't exist." I turned to her, because I couldn't keep looking at him. "Have you ever seen Burn Notice?"

"Is that the one with the yogurt?"

"He eats a lot of yogurt, yes."

"I watched it with Mum. She fancied the old one." She put the cheese down. "Why?"

"I was thinking about how it starts. He's a spy, a good one, and one day somebody finds out who he is. And his own side doesn't rescue him. They burn him. That's the word. No job, no accounts, nobody picks up. It's nothing personal. A spy with his face known is just a cost."

"That's sad," Ana said.

"It runs seven seasons."

"I never saw it," Kalder said. He was looking at me with real interest, the way he'd looked at the forum man talking about the Sphinx. "Is it any good?"

"Dry as a biscuit. The second half's the good bit."

He laughed. I'll give him that. He laughed with his head back, and Mora thumped her tail against his shoe, and under the table Buddy went up a note.

And I thought: that's it, that's everything I've got. I've told a man who poisons dogs that I know what he is, in my own kitchen, with my wife three feet from him. I'd run out of code. So I did the other thing, the one I'd asked her for on this floor at four in the morning, after the barbecue.

"Ana."

She looked at me.

"You remember what I asked you. On the floor. I said one day I'd tell you, and you'd know it was the day." I kept my voice the way it had been that night. "It's today."

It sounded mad. I'd told her it would.

She held my eyes for about a second. Then she looked at the bottle and said, "That's finished. I'll open the other one," and picked up his glass, and hers, and went to the counter with her back to us.

She hadn't heard. Or she'd heard and decided I was being dramatic in front of a guest. I sat there with my heart going and thought, I asked for one thing. One thing, in fifteen years.

Kalder watched her go. "She's very good," he said quietly. "I've always thought so."

She came back with three glasses, poured in the kitchen, and put his in front of him.

"To Buddy," she said. "Being okay now."

"To Buddy," said Kalder, and drank.

---

It took twenty-five minutes. He told another story. Partway through it he lost a word and went looking for it, and found it, and smiled at himself. He said the pasta had been perfect. He said, "That's a heavy red," and looked at the glass, and then at Ana, and I watched him get to the answer about four seconds too late to do anything with it.

"Oh," he said. "That's very good."

He put his head down on his arm, slowly, like a man listening to the table. His mouth came a little open. It made him look younger.

Buddy stopped growling. Just like that, mid-note. He came out from under the table, slower than he used to, and stood in the kitchen doorway looking at the sleeping man with an expression I can only call professional.

Ana was by the sink with her arms folded and her mother's pill bottle in her hand.

"How long?" I said.

"Mum takes one and she's out for seven hours. He's had two." She looked at the bottle. "I didn't plan it, James. He asked if Buddy was okay *now*, and I was standing at the cupboard, and Mum's bag was in there from Tuesday. And I thought, he's going to sit here being lovely until one of us says something we can't take back. I'd rather he slept."

"You improvised."

"I improvised." She put the bottle down very carefully. "Is that a crime?"

"Probably."

"Good," said Ana, and sat down, and put her face in her hands, and started to shake. Not crying. The other thing. I've seen it in people after an outage, when the system's back up and there's nothing left to do with the adrenaline.

I'd been paying for this in forms and initials. She'd just paid in something you can be arrested for, and it was my bill.

I got her up and into the hall, away from him. I don't know why. He couldn't hear anything.

"I told you to run," I said.

"I heard you."

"That was the sentence. That was the whole promise. You said okay."

"I said okay to believing you. I believed you. I never said I'd leave you in a kitchen with him." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "And run where? He's got my Doberman on his foot."

We both looked. She was still there. She'd gone to sleep on a drugged man's shoe.

"She's a terrible dog," I said.

"She's a terrible dog."

---

I took four photographs. It's what I do when something's in a state it won't be in again. I capture it first and work out what it's for later.

Him, from across the table, with the glass. His face, close. The whole kitchen from the doorway, so you could see it was a kitchen and whose. And one from the hall, which is the one that mattered, though I didn't know it yet: the front door, the tan bag standing beside it with the brass tag, and through the kitchen doorway behind it a man asleep at my table.

Then I sat down opposite him, and the phone was right there.

Face down by his plate. His right hand was on the cloth beside it, palm up, the thumb a little apart from the fingers.

"James," Ana said from the doorway. She'd seen where I was looking. "His thumb's *right there*."

"I know."

"So?"

I've read a few hundred incident reports in eleven years and a lot of them start exactly here. Nobody breaks into a phone. They don't need to. People leave them face down on tables. They fall asleep next to them. Every report has a line near the top where someone borrowed something for a minute because it was right there, and that's the line the report is about.

"If I open it with his hand, I've got something I took off a drugged man," I said. "I can't put that in front of anybody. I can't put it in front of Pieter. I couldn't have put it in front of you."

"I'd have looked."

"You'd have looked, and then you'd have asked where I got it."

She thought about that. "I drugged a man for you," she said, "and you won't borrow his thumb."

"No."

"Unbelievable." She went and got the blanket off the couch. I thought it was for her. She put it over his shoulders, tucked it in at the neck, and looked at what she'd done with real disgust. "Don't," she said. "I know."

So we waited. That's all I did that night, for seven hours, and it's harder than it sounds. Ana slept on the couch in her clothes. Mora was eventually persuaded off his foot with ham. Buddy lay down in the doorway and didn't close his eyes, and I sat at my own table across from a man with his mouth open and a phone I wasn't going to touch, and drank cold tea, and kept not touching it.

At one point I turned it a few degrees with one finger, so it was square to his plate. Then I turned it back. I'm not proud of either.

---

He woke at ten to five.

There was no start. His eyes opened, and he lay with his head on his arm and looked at what he could see from there. The glass. The clock on the oven. The blanket, which he touched. Ana sitting up on the couch. The bottle of pills on the counter, which she hadn't put away. My phone, on the table in front of me.

Then he sat up and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Zopiclone," he said. "It tastes like a coin. I thought it was the wine." He looked at Ana. "Your mother's?"

"Yes."

"I'd have bet on him." He nodded at me. "I'd have lost."

Nobody said anything. Buddy started up again from the doorway, quietly.

"How many did you take?" Kalder asked me.

"Four."

"Is the bag in any of them?"

"One."

"Sent anywhere?"

"No."

"Doesn't matter." He picked up his own phone, looked at where it was lying, and put it down in the same place. I think he could tell it hadn't been opened. I think that annoyed him more than the pills. "I'll do the sum out loud, Jim, since you like to see the working. Either I report tonight or I don't. If I report it, I'm the one who was put to sleep at a subject's table by the subject's wife, and photographed with his clubs by the door. If I don't, there's a seven-hour hole in my Friday, and in March that picture's on a widow's dresser in Zwolle and somebody asks me about the hole." He turned his hand over on the cloth. "For that photograph I'm retired. Either way. And we don't do a dinner and a clock."

"Burn Notice," Ana said.

"I told you, I never saw it." He almost smiled. "I'll take your word that it's dry."

He got up. He was steady, which I hadn't expected. He walked down the hall to the bag by the door, unzipped the long side pocket, and came back with a steel flask.

I knew it. The same make, the same dented shoulder where the cup screws on. The other one was on the shelf behind his chair, between the cookbooks, where it had been since Marijke pushed it at me in a doorway in Zwolle. He'd have seen it when he sat down.

He looked at it now, and then at me, and unscrewed his own.

"I never drink anyone's coffee," he said. "Old habit." He poured into the cup. It steamed. It had been in a golf bag for ten hours and it steamed, which told me what the flask cost too.

Then he turned his phone over, put his own thumb on it, typed something with his other hand, opened a gray folder with no name, and slid the phone across the tablecloth until it touched my cup.

"You've got as long as this lasts," he said. "It holds four cups, and I drink fast when I'm annoyed."

I didn't pick it up. "Why?"

"Not for you. Don't thank me, I'd find it embarrassing." He drank. "On Monday they have one problem, and it's me. If you're driving around the east of the country with what's on there, they've got two, and you're the loud one. I need about a week of them being slow."

"And you think I'll do that."

"You read things, Jim. You can't help it. It's the only reason any of us ever met you."

I looked at Ana. She was already coming back from the hall with my bag, the one with the annex in it.

He'd finished half the cup.
