# Enjoy it while it lasts

In the morning I went to look, and I couldn't.

The old job list was gone. Not the job; the list. That week the bank had switched on the new monitoring tools, the ones Frank had sent the link about in the spring. AI-driven. Finds the anomalies for you, no humans needed. He'd put it in the team channel again on the Monday, same words, same little skull. *It's here. Enjoy it while it lasts, boys.*

Everything I used to read now came through the new thing. It was faster, and it had graphs. I searched it for anything running at 02:14 and got nothing. I searched for the job's new name. Nothing. I listed every job owned by continuity, which is a short list, and it wasn't there.

My first thought was that they'd finally deleted it. My second was the invoice. You don't bill per person for a count nobody's making.

So I read the defaults.

When you buy a thing that watches, it comes with opinions. Out of the box, before anyone's touched it, it already knows what's normal and what isn't, what to wake you for and what to let through. Those opinions are called defaults, and nobody reads them, because they're long and they're boring and they've been chosen by experts, and the whole promise of the thing is that you don't have to. You install it. It watches. You sleep.

Four hundred pages. I read them at my desk over two days, like a man eating a phone book.

On page two hundred and twelve, in a section about scheduled jobs, there was a class of thing the tool had been told not to report. Not to log, not to display, not to alert on. A pattern, in the dry language of patterns: *jobs held by a continuity function, on a nightly schedule, emitting small payloads to unresolved addresses.* Classified, out of the box, as *noise.* Visible only to a role called vendor support.

My job. My forty kilobytes. Described in one sentence, in a default, in a product sold to ten thousand companies, as the one shape the watcher need never look at.

At the foot of the page: *Rev. 4. Next review: 31 December.*

It was the second document I'd held that autumn that was on its fourth revision. I'd have given a lot to see the third. In June the job had no owner. Now it had one, and here was a rule that said *held by a continuity function.* Somebody had kept the rule up to date.

Nobody had hidden anything from me in particular. I want to say that plainly. There was no locked room. Somebody had been boring in the right place. They'd written one sentence into the defaults of a tool that would be installed everywhere by people who'd never read it, and from then on every company that bought it had a watcher that had been told, before it opened its eyes, what it would never have to see. My access hadn't been taken away. It had been narrowed by exactly one shape, by a setting, and no person had needed to decide it.

---

I took page 212 to Saskia. Printed, with the sentence underlined.

I've gone back over the next four minutes more than I've gone over most things.

I told her. Standing at her desk, in the voice I use for incident reviews, I told her what the rule hides. There's a job at 02:14. It sends out a list of staff numbers with a reason beside each. The people on it are people who asked something, or wrote to someone, or posted. I'm on it. A foundation bills the bank for it, per person, and has done since 1994. And this page is why nobody sees it run.

She listened to all of it. She's a good listener; it's her job to hear why people want things.

Then I made it worse. I thought I knew what she'd been told, and I wanted her to know I knew.

"They'll have told you it's a security trap," I said. "A honeypot. It isn't one."

"A what?"

"That's what Pieter told the team."

"Nobody's told me anything about a trap." She wasn't defending herself. She was puzzled, and it was real. "James, it's a default. It came in the box. Every client has that rule. I've got the ticket for the ruleset right here, it says *accepted as shipped*, I didn't write a line of it and neither did anyone in this building." She turned her screen a little, as proof. "I was told it cuts down the noise. Which it does."

So that was my one guess, and it was wrong, and the first thing she'd learned about my theory was that it was wrong about her.

She looked at her screen again, at something that wasn't the ticket. She does the access reviews. She sees what's on a person's file. She didn't say the word *referral* and she didn't have to.

"Are you sleeping?"

"That's not—"

"I'm asking because I have to log this. Somebody queries a ruleset, I log it. I'm going to put that you asked about rule 212 and I explained it's a vendor default." She was already typing. "And I'm going to put that you seemed tired. Because you do. And because if anyone reads this later I'd sooner they read that than something worse."

"Could you leave that part out?"

"I log everything. That's why they trust the log." She looked up, and she was kind about it, and I'd have preferred almost anything else. "Go home. It's four hundred pages of settings. Nobody should read that."

I went back to my desk. I'd told a colleague the truth with nothing in my hands but a printout of a default and my own say-so. Frank would have had it in the right-hand column before I'd finished the first sentence. And now it was in her log, under my name, with a note about sleep. Three papers in a month, and this one I hadn't even been asked to sign.

---

That night I wrote a rule. Kitchen table, Buddy underneath, the Record open at a clean page. I don't make vows. But I write rules for systems all day, and it had become obvious that I needed one for me.

*I tell nobody until I know what they were told and can put their own paper in front of them.*

It looked sensible written down. Rules do. What they were told, because I'd just seen what happens when you guess. Their own paper, because mine was a market notebook and a dead screenshot, and Saskia had a ticket.

I didn't ask myself what it would cost to keep, or who'd be paying while I kept it. I should have. I underlined it instead.

---

I stayed narrowed for five days. I did my lists. I was tired, as agreed.

On the Wednesday, at seven, on the sixth green, Julian said, "You look like a man whose bank's being difficult."

"It's got new tools."

"There it is." He crouched for Buddy, who was in the cart and stayed in the cart. "Hello, old boy. Is he being a misery? He's being a misery." And then, standing, easily, the way he said everything: "If it's an access thing, and it's always an access thing, whoever sold you the tools will have someone. They always have someone. That's what you're paying them for. Ask."

"Ask who?"

"Whoever's name is on the invoice. That's who you always ask. The person who'd be embarrassed."

I knew whose name was on the invoice. I'd looked it up the week before: the tools had come through the same company whose name was on the sign outside the shed and on a blue lanyard at a golf day. So a man was standing on a green telling me to write to him, in the third person. He must have known I could see that. I think it was meant as a kindness I could see through, the way you let a child watch you hide the egg.

Fine. It was also a test I could run. If the man who sells the seats could open what page 212 shuts, that told me something about who'd shut it.

I wrote that afternoon. Two dull lines. *Could someone help me with a view that seems to be restricted by ruleset default 212.* The reply took eleven minutes. It was from a man who signed himself *Julian K.*, who was sorry to hear it, who'd had a word, and who thought it'd be sorted by close of play.

It was sorted by four. Saskia came past my desk and said, "Oh, that got through, did it, good," in a voice that meant it hadn't gone through her.

I wrote back to thank him. He wrote back that it was nothing, that these things were always a setting somebody had forgotten, and that if I needed anything else he was the person who'd be embarrassed.

Then I did the dull thing and put the two invoices side by side. The one for the tools that had narrowed me. The one for the support hours that had just fixed it. Same supplier. Same post box. The problem and the rescue had been billed from one address, eleven minutes apart. At work we have a word for a vendor who breaks something and then mends it while you watch. We call it a demo.

And there was the job, on my screen, under the vendor-support view. New name, owner continuity, a ticket. It had run that morning at 02:14 for nine seconds. Forty-one kilobytes. One more than in June.

I didn't capture it. I looked at the number for a while and closed the view.

So Julian could see my access. He'd known it was narrowed before I'd told him what the trouble was; I'd said *new tools* and he'd said *access.* If he could see that, what else could he see? My tickets? What I'd posted? What I read?

I'd never tested a person. I test systems, and paper. But I knew how I'd go about it with a system, and I decided to find out.
