# Leiden

I thanked the stranger on Wednesday night. On Friday I did the next thing, which is what I'd do at work with any message I'd seen twice: I put the two copies side by side and asked who else had received it.

Under his reply, in the same thread, I posted two photographs. On the left, his first paragraph. On the right, the first paragraph of page three, cropped, with the heading left on: *WHEN A READER NOTICES.* And one line from me. *Has anyone else had an answer in these words? The right-hand image is from a photocopy, date unknown.*

I should say what that name was. For twelve years, under a handle that was a joke only I understood, I'd posted did-you-knows. The light kind. The steam engine. The calendar. A door ten meters up a wall. Never a theory. A man standing at the edge of the strange and dark corner with his hands in his pockets, saying *huh, look at that*, and leaving. A few hundred people followed it. Ana knew about it and thought it was sweet.

By Saturday morning forty people had looked at the two photographs and nobody had said a word. At noon there was a private message, from an account that had been registered nine years and had never posted. I checked that before I read it twice.

*Take that down. Today, before tonight. You have put their own page on a public board under a name they can match to a house, and people who do that get visited. I was Hendrik Maasen's last student. He showed me that page on a photocopy in 2009. Take it down and I will tell you where I am.*

I looked at the post for a long time. It was the only thing I'd ever made that sat in Frank's left-hand column: two documents that shouldn't match, matching, in public, with a date on it. Without it I had a polite reply to a dull question and a folder from a curtain shop.

Also, Els had said *don't tell me*, and I'd had her father's hidden page on the internet for a day. I hadn't told her. I'm not sure that's better.

I deleted it. I took a screenshot first, and then remembered what Frank thinks of screenshots. Then I wrote back: *Done.*

Four minutes later, an address in Leiden. *Next Saturday, two o'clock. Drive. If you have a dog, bring it. People with dogs look like nothing.*

---

Her name was Noor Haverkamp, and she lived in Leiden, which is where the plates were.

She had been Hendrik Maasen's last doctoral student. She'd left the university in 2017, when the library's special collections were "restructured" under a digitization grant, which is a sentence that sounds like nothing and that she said in a tone I recognized, the tone I use for the word *migration.* Now she repaired paper for a living. Freelance.

Her kitchen was small and the table was covered in a sheet of glass, and under the glass, I understood after a while, was a page from something four hundred years old that she was keeping flat.

"Don't put your coffee on it."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Everyone dreams of it. It's a table." She looked at Buddy, who had come up her stairs one at a time with me behind him like a man moving a piano. "Is he clean?"

"No."

"Then he goes under." He went under, and lay down with his chin on her foot, and she let him. She was fifty, maybe, gray at the front, with the hands of a person who does one thing extremely carefully all day. "You want to know if you're mad."

"I want to know who else has seen that page."

"Better. Me. Hendrik. Whoever typed it. And as of yesterday week, forty people on a forum, thanks to you." She pulled a folder across the glass and didn't open it. "He showed it to me once, in his office, with the door shut. He said, Noor, if you ever see this phrasing in the wild, assume the person is not a scholar. Then he never mentioned it again, and I thought he'd gone a bit strange. Then they restructured the collections, and I understood what strange was for." She looked at me. "The man who answered you used the first paragraph nearly word for word. It's a template. They don't even change it."

"Why would they?"

"Exactly. Nobody has ever noticed it twice."

---

I'd brought the thin book. She put on gloves to touch it, which told me more about her than anything she'd said, and she went through it the way I'd have gone through it if I'd been a better man, slowly. She stopped where I'd stopped, at eleven. Then she stopped at twenty-six.

"You've looked this one up."

"It's not a book."

"No." Entry twenty-six was a paper. *Verhoef, A. Utrecht, 1993. Withdrawn before publication. Reclassified: instrumentation.* And then, in the professor's hand, the only entry in the list with a note on it: *two rooms, 40 m, correlated. Copy from the family, 2015. Same edit: "shared" → "coincident."*

"Somebody in the family kept the original," Noor said, "with the word *shared* in it. The version in the department's archive says *coincident.* They couldn't see why anyone would have bothered. Hendrik could." She turned the page with one gloved finger. "It's the only entry that isn't about the past. Everything else is a hand being taken out of a story about the beginning. This one's a hand being taken out of last Tuesday. Two people in two rooms, and something went between them."

"That pair's in the folder. Shared, coincident. Page one."

"Then the page is newer than 1993, or the paper was the reason for the pair. I've wondered." She almost smiled. "He'd have liked you. You read the list."

I asked her the thing I'd been stuck on since the kitchen floor. "Why bother? Go into any bookshop. There's a whole wall of it. Gods who came down and taught farming. Lost civilizations. The hand's on every cover, in gold. Why spend ninety years taking it out of a book about flood legends that nobody reads?"

"Because of the wall." She had a paperback on the glass, French, tabbed near the back, and she pushed it toward me without opening it. "That's a report by a private committee, 1999. Retired officers, engineers. It was sold in a magazine. One chapter is a thought experiment: if you were the ones visiting, how would you manage a population like us? Texts, they suggest. Films. Sects. It's been on sale for a quarter of a century and nobody has touched a word of it, because look where it's shelved. With the crystals." She took her hand off it. "It's in every bookshop, James. That's where it's allowed to be. Loud, and silly, and next to a book about angels. What they remove is the plain version. The dull book by the careful man. The one you'd believe."

"They don't stop you talking."

"They stop you being heard. And if they can't, there's a word for what happens next, and it's from a budget meeting."

"Reviewed."

"So you've read page three properly." She closed the thin book. "Hendrik had a rule. He gave it to me the week I left, in these words, and I've kept it nine years. *I'm fairly sure I'm already being watched. Until we're a hundred percent, never publish, never ask, always hide. If anything happens to me, know I was right.*" She took the gloves off. "I have never published a word of this. Not a footnote. I repair paper, and my mother's in a home I can pay for, and nobody has ever come to my door. I broke the rule last Saturday to write to you, because yours was the kind of post that gets a door knocked on, and it had his page in it."

I knew what my visit might be worth to somebody. A neighbor in Deventer had rung me in a month before. If anyone was keeping track of where the man with the old dog went, I'd just parked outside her house. I'd known that when I rang the bell.

---

Before I left she opened the thin book again, at the back.

She read the last page. The reference, the date, and the word *Ask* in her supervisor's hand. She didn't say anything. She sat back, and she put her right hand under her left arm, flat, tucked in, the way you'd carry a newspaper. It was a warm kitchen.

So I asked the dull question. "Is your hand cold?"

She looked down at it as if it were somebody else's.

"Hendrik asked me that," she said.

That was all I got. She took the hand out and pointed at the page with it.

"That's a depot shelfmark. When they took the collections apart, everything went to the foundation's depot. I packed crates for three weeks. I wrote that one on a lid myself, and I told Hendrik, which I shouldn't have." She tapped the date. "It's a shed in a business park, out east. There's a reading room, and it sits once a year. I've never been let in. The ground floor is rented to a bank, for emergencies. Desks, in case their own building burns down."

I didn't say anything. I'd been a month on a program whose whole purpose was desks in case the building burned down, and there was a welcome pack in my drawer with an address in it that I'd had no reason to read twice.

"And find the money," Noor said. "Hendrik never looked. He thought money was vulgar. But paper doesn't pay for itself. Somebody pays for that shed, and the grant that emptied my library, and whoever answers forum posts for a living. You work in a bank."

---

I asked her the math on the way out, standing in her hall with Buddy sitting on my foot. She was the only person I'd met who could take it.

"One in five hundred," I said. "That's what I get. One in five hundred people has been asked to do one small thing, once, and been given a reason."

"That sounds low."

"Low?"

"You're counting the wrong column." She had her hand on the door. "You've worked out how many it takes to run it. Fine. Now do the other number. How many have noticed? In a hundred years? Hendrik. His grandfather. Whoever kept that paper in Utrecht. Me. You. That's five I can name without trying, and I'm a woman who repairs paper in Leiden." She opened the door. The street was wet and ordinary. "And then ask yourself why there's a list of forty-one books and not a list of the people. Whether nobody made one, or somebody did."

"Somebody did," I said. "It goes out at a quarter past two every night."

She looked at me for a moment.

"Then you know something Hendrik didn't," she said. "Don't write to me again." And she shut the door, kindly.

I did the other column in the car, on the back of a parking ticket, with Buddy breathing on my neck. It wasn't low. It came out about the same as the first: for every person who'd been asked to do one small thing, there was roughly one who'd noticed something small and kept quiet. The list at work only had the ones who'd said so.

On Monday I took the welcome pack out of my drawer. *Recovery site.* An address in a business park, east of the ring road. Landlord, a foundation. And a line I'd skimmed in my first week because it had my name in it and nothing else of interest: the annual test was on Thursday the fifteenth, by bus, and I was on the list.
