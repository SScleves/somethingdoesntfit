# What was on the phone

He drank with his left hand and watched me with the rest of him. I've been read by monitoring tools for eleven years and none of them ever did it that calmly.

"You're going to want to photograph everything," he said. "Don't. Your wife's phone is in the hall and mine logs the screen. Read it. You're a man who remembers paper. That's what they were worried about."

"Who's they?"

"Three cups and a bit, Jim."

I read.

---

I'm going to give you the four things in the order they came, because the order is how I understood them, and because what I understood wasn't on any one screen.

No messages. That was the first thing. Not deleted; absent. The app was there and it was empty, the way a show house is empty. Whoever he talked to, he didn't talk to them here.

Then the gray folder. Three things that looked like spreadsheets and one that didn't.

The first was people. Not names. Codes: two letters and three digits, a region, a status, and beside each one a short note in the register I'd come to know from the schedule, the register of a man confirming a delivery. *Active. Active. Reassigned. Active. Retired (natural).* Forty-odd rows, and most of them for other regions, with a region code I didn't know. Ours had twelve. I didn't know what I was looking at until I found him: our region, *active*, and in the note column, the only one with anything personal in it, a single word.

*Hands.*

"That's me," he said, without looking. "Third from the bottom. You'll be looking for the 'Retired' column. There isn't one yet."

Handlers. The people who do the visiting. By code, by region, and I had a dozen of ours, and I didn't know a single name, and I understood that this was on purpose and that the purpose was that nobody, not even the man across the table, could give the names, because he'd never had them.

"You've met two of them," he said. "You wouldn't remember. One sold you the coffee at the drill."

The second sheet was the schedule. Next quarter's. The one after the one I'd acknowledged at work in October and carried in my bag since. Addresses, dates, a column headed *Visit*, a column of notes. I read it with my heart doing something I didn't like. Our address wasn't on it. I read it again. It wasn't. The addresses that had gone quiet were carried over, with new dates, and nine new ones, and beside three of them the word *pension*, and beside one the word *companion*.

I turned the phone so he could see it. "We're not on it."

"The dog lived and the book's back where it came from," he said. "The visit was about the book. They don't come back to a house for feelings." He drank. "Hengelo's still on it. I'd look at Hengelo if I were you, and I'm not."

The third was seats. Not ours. Four companies. Four annexes, laid out exactly like the one I'd reconciled in the autumn, with names and *dependents per allocation* and the line about animals. A shipping firm, a hospital group, an energy company and a university. The totals across the four, when I added the column in my head, were a number of people that would fill the recovery site in the business park three times over.

So there were other buildings. Of course there were.

---

And then the fourth thing, and I want to go slowly here, because it was the thing I hadn't expected and it was the thing that told me how to win.

It was a list of helpers.

Not handlers. The others. The people who make the phone calls and close the tickets and sign the invoices and catalog the records and drive the vans. Hundreds of them in our region alone, and they weren't by code. They were by name, because nobody had ever thought a helper needed protecting. They weren't the ones who knew anything.

And beside each name, in a column with no heading, was a word. One word per person. When I'd read enough of them I realized the column had a heading after all, it just wasn't written down, and the heading was *what we told them*.

Now here's where I stopped reading like a man being given something and started reading like a man at work. Because a gift from this man was worth exactly nothing until I'd checked it against something I had made myself, and for once I had plenty.

*Honeypot.* That was Pieter, and a dozen more at the bank, and it meant they'd been told the nightly job was a security trap for leakers and that they were helping by leaving it alone. I didn't need the phone for that one. Frank had told me on his stairs, in the spring, with Mora under his table: *Pieter told the team it's a honeypot.* Match.

*Conservation.* That was the dealer in Groningen, and a woman in Deventer who turned out to be the professor's neighbor, and a records clerk in Apeldoorn. I'd driven to Groningen and the dealer had said the word to me himself, over a receipt from 2011 marked *Second impression*. Match.

*Disaster recovery.* The site manager at the recovery site, the bus driver, the people who stock the canteen. True, every word of it, which is the genius of it. And *heritage*, for the depot upstairs, which the site manager had said to me through a shut glass door with his hands in his jacket: *different tenant, same generator.* Match, and match.

Three reasons I'd been given by three people in person, in three different towns, with no phone in sight, and they were sitting on this sheet in the right column against the right names. I don't believe lists. I believe lists I can check three times against things I was there for.

"You're checking it," Kalder said. He sounded pleased, which I'd have paid not to hear. "Of course you are."

"It's what I do."

"I know. I've watched you check a scorecard you knew was right." He turned the cup. "Do the desks."

---

I hadn't been going to. I did.

Our annex was in my bag, in Ana's hand, and she put it on the table beside the phone without being asked, open at the first page, with my initials at the foot of it.

Annex C. Forty-nine rows. Desk one, Anneke de Wit. Desk twelve, Pieter, dependents per allocation three. Desk forty, a man in Legal who'd been off sick since March. Nine vendor allocations with references beginning V, which Pieter had told me to leave, and I'd left, and signed.

The shipping firm's annex, on the phone. Desk twelve: a name I'd never heard of, in Rotterdam. Dependents per allocation, two.

The hospital group. Desk twelve: a consultant, Nijmegen. Four.

The energy company. Desk twelve: a woman with a double surname. Three.

I went back to ours. Desk twelve. Pieter. Lotte and Bas and Marieke, three.

I did it again for desk one. Four Annekes, in effect: four heads of something, each in the chair by the window. Then I did desk four, and desk twenty, and desk thirty-three, and stopped, because Ana had put her hand flat on the annex and I understood that I'd been saying the numbers out loud.

"It's one building," I said. "There's one floor of desks under the reading room. I've sat at one. I counted them at the drill. A hundred, maybe. And they've sold each desk to four companies."

"Four that I know of," Kalder said. "The university's new."

"So when it happens, whatever it is, four people turn up for Pieter's chair."

"Nobody turns up." He said it kindly, which was worse. "That's the part you're not getting yet, and I don't have the cups for it. The seats were never for sitting in. A seat is what you promise a man so that he'll leave a job alone for thirty years. You don't need a hundred desks for that. You need an annex, and a man like you to initial it."

"And the brewery."

"He's got a four in his column and he's in three annexes. He's the happiest man in the east of the country."

I sat back. Under the table Buddy had come in from the doorway at some point and put himself across my feet, and I hadn't noticed, which tells you something.

I'd signed that annex. I'd found nine names with no HR record and taken them to Pieter and been told leave it, and I'd written the exception in and initialed every page, because it was a real list of real desks in a real building and I'd eaten a sandwich at one of them. I'd thought the seats were the thing under everything. They were the lid. They were a lie with a floor plan, and I'd reconciled it.

That's where the lifeboat broke. Not on anything the old man said in December, not on anything this one said over coffee. On a desk number that appeared four times, in four documents, one of them with my initials on it.

---

I went back to the helpers once more, because there was one thing I hadn't looked for.

The fifth column. Against every name, down the whole list like a watermark, the same word: *Continuity.* That was the promise. Not saving the world; nobody believes that. The thing that goes on. The payroll on Monday. And every one of them had heard that word and taken it to mean, without once examining it, *you'll be all right.*

I looked for them on the four annexes, and on ours. Pieter's honeypot dozen. The dealer. The site manager. The clerk in Apeldoorn. Aylin, who was on the list as *heritage*.

Not one of them. Hundreds of people who'd been told continuity and heard *kept*, and the lists of who was kept were in the next folder along, and not one of their names was on any of them. Except Pieter, who was on ours. At a desk sold to three other men.

He put the cup down. It rang on the table, empty.

"That's four," he said, and reached across and took the phone out of my hand. Not roughly. The way you take a glass from someone who's done with it. He thumbed the folder shut and put the phone in his inside pocket, and then he stood and screwed the cup back onto the flask.

"Too bad," he said. "I liked you."

He meant it. That was the thing about him, start to finish. He'd meant all of it, and it hadn't changed anything he did.

Ana got his coat. She held it out at arm's length, the way you'd hold out something a dog had been sick on. He took it the same way and said thank you.

At the door he shouldered the bag. The brass tag caught the hall light. "Don't ring me, Jim. The number's gone by lunchtime." He looked down the hall at Buddy in the kitchen doorway, who'd started again, very low. "He was always right about me, you know. First day. On the green. I liked that about him too."

Then he went down the path with the clubs, and got into his car, and that was the last time Julian Kalder sat at a table of mine.

---

We stood at the door and watched his car go round the corner, and when it had gone Ana said, "I liked him," in the voice of someone identifying a body.

So I'd been given a gift by the man who poisoned my dog, and I'd checked it, and it was good. That's the cost of that chapter and I'd like it written down plainly. Everything I did for the next three weeks stood on four cups of his coffee.

I sat down at the table with the annex, which was mine, and the schedule, which was mine, and the three words I'd been given in Groningen and on Frank's stairs and through a glass door, which were mine, and what I'd read was already going where things go in my head when they've been checked. For a month I'd thought the thing I'd found was a machine, and you don't beat a machine, you go around it, you take the seat. It wasn't a machine. It was a few dozen people with codes, standing on a few hundred who'd each been told one small true-sounding thing and the word continuity, standing on everybody else. Each helper knew one task, and could tell a policeman the whole truth and it would come out as nothing, because it was nothing. That's not strength. It's width. And width only works as long as the people being stood on have never been shown the list they're not on. Nobody had ever put it in front of them. Nobody had ever needed to. They never asked.

"Say what you're going to do," Ana said. "Plainly. No systems."

"One page per person," I said. "What they were told. What they did, from their own records. What it was part of. And who's kept, with a space where their name should be." I looked at the kitchen clock, which said half past five on a Saturday morning in December. "And then I need a printer, and three weeks, and you're going to have to drive."

"Mora comes," said Ana, and went to look for the paper.
