# The week it vanished

Beard Man knew how the professor died, because Beard Man knows everything the way a river knows everything. It's all gone through him and none of it has stayed in order.

It was the Sunday after Groningen. I'd gone to the market with one question, which is one more than I usually bring.

"Chair," he said. He was pricing a box of Reader's Digest condensed novels that nobody on earth would buy, and he priced them anyway, lovingly, at fifty cents each. "Blue one. Daughter found him. Monday. Bin hadn't been put out, that's how she knew, he always put the bin out. Nice man. Didn't haggle."

"You met him?"

"Sold him things. Years. Particular." He held a condensed Hemingway up to the light, as if checking it for the parts they'd left out. "Wanted first printings. Not first editions, mind. Printings. Most people don't know the difference. I told him the difference once and he said he knew, thank you, and I liked him for it."

"And the daughter sold you the books."

"Curtains," said Beard Man. "She does curtains. Deventer, four streets from the station. Didn't want the books, wanted the shelves. Said he'd stopped sleeping at the end. Said he'd be up all night with them." He put the Hemingway down. "Said the last few months he kept asking her the same thing."

I waited. He let me wait. It's the only power he has and he enjoys it.

"What thing?"

"Whether she'd be all right." Fifty cents. Fifty cents. "Which is a normal thing for a dying man to ask, and I told her so, and she said no, not like that. She said he asked it like a man checking a *list.*"

Then he was off again, mid-sentence as always, the comet, the prophet. I stood there with my hands in my pockets and didn't hear a word of it.

"Who bought the furniture?" I said, when there was a gap.

"Man in a van." Beard Man shrugged. "Good coat. Polite. Didn't haggle either. Everyone's very polite about that house."

The week itself I have to give in order, because the order is all I've got.

Monday I looked at the job. I'd got into the habit, the way you touch your pocket for your keys. I didn't open it. I looked at its line in the list: `rcl-agg`, no owner, fourteen minutes past two, nine seconds. Still there, same as every morning for six weeks. My ticket was still in the queue where I'd left it, number 41790, dying quietly.

Tuesday, the same. Wednesday, the same.

Thursday morning it was gone.

Nothing in red, no failure. The line wasn't there. I searched for the name and the search came back empty, and for about a minute I was a man whose floor had been taken away. I'd watched that thing run for six weeks. I had three nights of what it sent in a folder in my bag.

Then I did the boring thing, which is the only thing I'm any good at. I stopped looking for the name and sorted the list by time.

Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds. Forty-one kilobytes. Same address, the one that's only there while you're sending to it.

It was called `bc-recon-nightly` now. It had an owner: *Business Continuity Program*. It had a description: *Nightly continuity reconciliation. Diagnostic. Introduced during platform migration. See #41822.* Every mandatory field was filled in. It looked like every other line on the list. If I'd been reading the ingredients for the first time that morning I'd have gone straight past it.

I opened my own ticket. It had been closed. *Resolved: duplicate of #41822.*

I looked up 41822. It existed. It was a perfectly good ticket, a dull one, registering an old diagnostic job and transferring it to continuity, and according to its date it had been raised years ago, in the last month of the migration. I worked that month. I worked most of its nights.

Tickets are numbered in the order they're born. Mine was 41790 and I'd opened it in June. So here was a ticket thirty-two numbers younger than mine and several years older. Whoever wrote it had done everything properly, and filled in every box, and typed a date into the one field that lets you, and had either not known about the number or not cared, because who checks a number.

Then the backups, because I had to. I know what I wanted to find: last month's snapshot still saying `rcl-agg`, a sealed room with the old name in it. It said `bc-recon-nightly`. They all did, as far back as they go. For a minute that frightened me more than anything else that week, until I remembered how the thing is built. The backups don't store a job's name. They store its number, and look the name up when you ask. Nobody had been into the backups. They hadn't needed to. You rename a thing once and every old copy agrees with you by itself.

I'd spent six weeks with a book where somebody reopened a plate and changed one line. This was tidier than that. Nothing had been removed. I'd asked, in writing, for an owner and a purpose for the inventory, and I'd been given an owner and a purpose, and the purpose was Pieter's sentence from the stand-up, almost word for word. Everybody agreed with me. My ticket had been answered.

The only place on earth where the old name still existed was a screenshot on my laptop from the first Thursday, which I'd taken because I screenshot things. I put the screenshot next to the list. They disagreed. And for the first time in my life I was in the one position I've never been allowed: the paper said one thing and I said the other, and I was right.

It felt like nothing. It didn't feel like winning. It felt like a cold coming on.

The last thing I checked was when. The scheduler keeps a line for the most recent change to anything, and you can't type a date into that one. *Modified: Wednesday, 16:52.*

The card was on the mat when I got home. A plain postcard, in an envelope, in a hand I knew by now.

*Dear Mr. Klippe. They telephoned on Tuesday, the same lady. She asked whether I had heard anything further from you since the sale. I said that I had not. I have kept a shop for forty years and I do not think I have ever lied worse. I tell you so that you are not surprised by anything. I think it best that we do not write again.*

I read it standing in the hall with my coat on and Mora trying to get her nose into the envelope in case it was food.

Saturday I'd told a man what his phone call does. Tuesday they rang him, and he lied to them, badly. Wednesday at eight minutes to five the thing I'd asked about got a new name, an owner and a ticket older than itself.

I can't prove the one caused the other. I've been paid for eleven years to notice when two things move a day apart, and I've never once been allowed to call that proof. But nothing else happened that week. I hadn't asked anything at work since June. The only thing I'd done was drive to Groningen and be plain.

So that was two things I'd learned, and I didn't like how they fit. Telling works. A man had stopped doing his small job because I'd told him what it was for. And they'd noticed him stop within three days, and the bill for that was on my hall floor, in his handwriting.

I put the card in the folder. Ana asked from the kitchen who was writing to me now, and I said the bookshop, about the book, and that was nearly true.

---

Friday I went to Pieter.

Not in stand-up. His office, door open, the way you'd ask about a day off. I'd decided on one question, the dullest one I had, and I'd decided not to mention the old name, because I wanted to hear which name he used.

"Quick one. The two-fourteen job, the nightly one. Who owns it now?"

There was no pause this time. That was the difference. In June he'd had to go and find his sentence. Now it was right there on top.

"Continuity," he said. And then, kindly, because he is kind, "Which is you, as of the first."

"Me."

"Your new lot. It's all theirs now, that end of things. So if you've got questions about it, from the first you can ask yourself." He smiled at his own joke, and it was a decent joke. "It's a good move, James. They asked for you. People don't get asked for."

"No," I said. "I suppose they don't."

"Have a good weekend."

I had a not very good weekend.

---

I told Ana I had a cold. I did have a cold, in the way that you can have one if you need one. I sat in the kitchen most of Saturday with Buddy under the table and the folder open and did the thing I'm paid to do, which is work out what a system is from what it does, without being allowed to see in.

Two options, I told the dog, because by then it was a liturgy.

One. It really was an old diagnostic job. Some conscientious soul in continuity saw my ticket, tidied the job up, gave it a proper name and an owner, filed the paperwork somebody should have filed years ago, and backdated it so the audit would look right. People do that. I've done something like that. And a bookseller in Groningen got a courtesy call about a sale, and the two things landed in the same week because weeks are short.

Two. Somebody reads the queue where tickets go to die. Somebody learned on Tuesday that a helper had been told something, and by Wednesday afternoon the thing I knew about had a boring name and a boring reason and a place on a list nobody reads. And the man who knew it by its old name was being moved, from the first, to the one team in the bank that would never need to ask about it, because they own it.

I'd been giving option one eighty-five percent since June, out of habit. That Saturday I wrote down sixty, and looked at it, and crossed it out, and didn't write anything in its place. An error doesn't fill in the mandatory fields. An error doesn't get the purpose right, in Pieter's words, six weeks after Pieter said them. I'd wanted it to be an error more than I'd admitted. That was what the week cost me, and I noticed it go.

Mora brought me the toy at some point, wet, and put it in my hand, and I threw it down the hall without thinking, and she brought it back.

Here is what I had on the table. A thin book in two dead men's handwriting, with forty-one entries and mine at eleven. A date on its last page, the second of December, four months off, with a reference I didn't know how to read and one word under it. Three rows with my number on them. A receipt, a card, and a screenshot of a name that had stopped existing on Wednesday at eight minutes to five. And a letter from HR, which had come on Friday while I was in Pieter's office, confirming my transfer to the Business Continuity Program with effect from the first of the month.

So as of the first, the job was mine. I'd be the owner. If anybody ever opened a ticket asking what it was, the ticket would come to me.

Ana came down in the evening, looked at the table, looked at me, and felt my forehead with the back of her hand like a mother.

"You don't have a cold."

"No."

"Is it the new job?"

"Partly."

"Does it come with more money?"

"I didn't ask."

"Mulder," she said. "That's the first thing you ask." She took a biscuit for Buddy from the tin and went back up, and I sat there with a pen in my hand, thinking that she was right, and that I still hadn't asked who *they* were.
