# Twenty minutes

Ana's mother lives in a flat where every surface has a cloth on it, and I put the objects on the cloth, one at a time, in the order I'd decided on in the car, and Ana sat opposite with her arms folded and her mother in the bedroom with the television up loud on purpose, and Mora under the table with her chin on my foot, because it had been three weeks and in Mora's world that's a resurrection. Buddy was in the car downstairs with the window open a hand. Two flights.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "You can stop me. You can throw me out. I'm going to put things on the table and tell you what they are, and I'm not going to tell you what I *think* until the end, and then you can decide."

"Go."

The two books. "This is a sentence that was changed in 1931, two years after the man who wrote it walked into some hills. They changed one line and left the typos so nobody'd check. I don't know who. I found it on our kitchen floor. That's the night you came down for water and I said I hadn't found anything weird."

"I remember."

A stack of photographs, where a book should have been. "These are pictures of a list of forty more. Kept by a dead man and his grandfather. I was handed it in a crate. The book itself was taken off our kitchen table the night Buddy was at the vet. Nothing else was. I don't know who took it. I know who'd walked the lane."

The three pages. "This is the style guide the edits were made from. His daughter found it in a dresser. It's got a section on what to do when someone like me notices, and the answer is: agree with him, and reclassify."

The captures. "This is a job that runs every night at the bank and sends forty kilobytes to a foundation. It's a list of staff numbers with two words beside each. I'm on it three times. *Wrote (dealer). Asked (job). Posted (forum).* After I asked what it was, it got a new name, an owner and a ticket. This screenshot is the only place the old name still exists. The bank pays for it per person. It's called a recall index. I don't know what they do with the number."

The referral. "This is a form Pieter gave me in a car park. Bereavement, sleep, preoccupation with historical documents. I signed it."

"You *signed* it?"

"On the hood of the car. It seemed polite."

The annex. "This is a list of who gets a desk and a bed if the bank burns down, and their families. It says no pets. Those are my initials at the bottom. I checked it for them."

The page from the schedule. She looked at it and didn't touch it. "You've seen that."

"Our address."

"Our address. The word in the box is the annex's word for Buddy. The visit was four nights early, round the back. Nobody came to the door. I don't know what a visit is supposed to be."

The watcher's printouts. "This is a thing I built the night of the barbecue. It copies the forum every night and shows me what changed. Three weeks of it. People who post a question get left alone. People who post what Henk said, more or less, in their own words, get a reply with bloodlines in it inside four days, and then they stop posting. Four of them so far. I don't know why. That's a label, not a theory."

"Who's Henk?"

My notebook, open. "A retired engineer in Zwolle. His address is on the schedule. He drove into a canal in October, three days after a nice man came about his pension. These are his words, from his widow. I wrote them down in her kitchen and she corrected me once."

Ana read them. She read them twice.

The flask. "And this is what the nice man left on her table. Julian carries one like it. So does half the country. I don't know that it's his."

The slip from Noor. "This is a number a woman in Leiden wrote from memory because I asked instead of argued. It's an item in the foundation's archive. She's gone quiet since. Not dead. Working. There's a difference, and it's my fault."

Page 212. "This is the tool Frank sent us a link about, with the skull. It's been told, out of the box, never to see the job. One boring sentence in the defaults. It's got a review date on it, the thirty-first of December."

And then the last thing, which wasn't an object, so I put my hand flat on the cloth where it would have gone.

"And this is me. On the floor. Four nights. I can't put it on the table. I can tell you I've been afraid of dying since I was sixteen, in a corridor, and that I'm not anymore, and that it happened on the kitchen floor with the dog watching and the oven clock on. I got a hand. That's all. Somebody's hand, holding mine, somewhere cold. No words. Then my own hand went cold for a minute and I sat on it. I'm not glowing. I'm not going to make you do anything."

I took my hand off the cloth. "That's it. Nineteen minutes. What I think is on the table. You can throw it out."

---

Ana didn't say anything for a long time. Her mother's television laughed at something.

Then she reached across and picked up the notebook, and read Henk's two lines again, and closed it, and put it down exactly where it had been, square to the cloth, the way her grandmother would have.

"You sit on your hand," she said.

"I just told you that."

"No. I'm telling *you.* You did it at the funeral. At the grave, after everyone had gone past. You let go of me and put your hand in your armpit like you'd burned it, and in the car park you were sitting on it. I thought it was your circulation. I nearly said something." She was looking at my right hand. "Where were you?"

So I told her. Cold mud, a low sky, a cracked hand reaching, for about as long as a camera flash, while a priest said amen. And then her face, and her question, and my yes.

"You were in *mud*," she said. "At Grandma's grave."

"For a second."

"And then you came back and told me she was okay."

"I said yes. It wasn't a very good yes."

"No," Ana said. "It wasn't. I took it anyway." She wiped her eye with the heel of her hand, angrily, like it had let her down.

Then she said, "I don't believe it."

"Okay."

"I want to say that, so you've heard it. I don't believe there's a *them*. I don't believe a bank in a wig has been paying for a machine that counts souls. I don't believe you've got a soul, or I do, but not like that, not like a thing that gets *counted*, that's insane, James, that's the most insane thing anyone's ever put on my mother's tablecloth." She wasn't crying. She was doing the other thing, the clear thing. "And I've been sitting here for nineteen minutes watching you put it there, and you did it the way you do the toaster. In order. Boring. With the bits you couldn't prove *labeled.* You said *I don't know* six times. I counted. You've never said I don't know six times in a *year.*"

"I've stopped pretending."

"I can see that. That's what I can't get past." She unfolded her arms. "You look different. You've looked different since the phone call. You stand like somebody who's put something heavy down, and I've been married to you for eleven years and I've never once seen you without it, and I didn't know it was there until it wasn't."

"So what do you believe?"

"*You.*" She said it like a slap and a kiss at once, the way she says most things that matter. "I believe you. I don't believe it and I believe you and I don't know what to do with that, so I'm going to sit here until I do."

---

What she did, in the end, was ask for the plan. Not the memoir. That's her. Twenty minutes of what, and then: *so what are you going to do about it, Mulder.*

"I don't have a plan. I've got a problem. Here's the problem." I moved the schedule next to the captures. "Everything I do to find out what it is gets written down. I asked about the job and I got a row. I wrote to a dealer and I got a row. Then I tried shutting up at work, and what I got for that was a friend who comes to barbecues. I can't ask and I can't stop."

She put her finger on the schedule. Not on our line. Lower.

"This one hasn't happened yet."

"No. Hengelo. A woman on her own. Nine days."

"So what are you doing about her?"

"Nothing."

"*Nothing.*"

"I have a rule. I tell nobody until I know what they were told and can put their own paper in front of them. I don't know what she's been told. I've got nothing of hers. If I knock on her door with this, I'm a stranger with a bank's list and a story, and that's all I am."

"You made that up. The rule."

"Yes."

"When?"

"After the last time I told somebody. At work. She wrote a kind note about my sleep."

She looked at me for a while. "And if something happens to her, in nine days, while you're keeping a rule you made up."

"Then it happened while I kept it. I know that."

She didn't tell me it was all right. I'd have liked her to. She took her finger off the page.

"What do they want? With you."

"To buy me, I think. They put me on the program the week I asked. The annex is seats. There's room on it."

"They'd offer you *me*," Ana said.

"Yes."

"And the dogs."

"No. It says so. I initialed it."

She stood up. She went to the kettle, which is what her family does instead of deciding, and she filled it, and she didn't turn it on, and she stood with her back to me, and Mora's chin stayed on my foot.

"I'm coming home," she said. "Tonight. Mora's eaten a cushion and Mum's stopped pretending she hasn't noticed."

"Ana, the address is still—"

"The address is still on a list, yes, I can read. And you've just told me the list runs to the end of the year, and I'm not spending it in my mother's spare room waiting for whoever they send to turn up at *our* house and offer you a desk while I'm here. If they want to offer you a desk they can offer it with me in the room." She turned round. "That's the plan. That's the whole plan. I don't believe it and I believe you, and the four of us are going to sit in our own kitchen, and if somebody comes to the door, I'll open it. Because I'm not in. Whatever that means. I'm not on any list of yours or theirs, and I'm the only one of us who can say that, and I'd like to see what happens to whoever they send when the wife answers the door and offers him tea."

I looked at my wife, standing by a cold kettle in her mother's kitchen with her arms folded, and understood that she'd just done the thing I do, the thing I'm supposedly good at, which is to look at a system from outside and work out what it can't handle, and that she'd done it in four minutes.

"Okay," I said.

"Good. Where's the hat?"

"In the car. With Buddy."

"Then it's coming home too."

It looked ridiculous on the back seat, next to an old dog, and Ana looked at it through the window, and for the first time since the pub she almost laughed.

Almost. We're not there yet. But I saw it go across her face, and I'd have signed for forty more years of almost.
