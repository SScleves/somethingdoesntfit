# Groningen

The letter had been on the fridge for five weeks, under a magnet shaped like a lemon, which is where things go in our house to be dealt with by someone else.

I'd dealt with half of it. I'd bought the man's book. What I hadn't dealt with was the postscript, the one about being plain about one's reasons, which Ana had translated on the night it came. "That's what careful sounds like in a shop."

Five weeks is how long it took me to hear it properly. Five weeks, three rows with my badge number on them, and a notebook in two dead men's handwriting with my sentence at number eleven. By then I'd done the arithmetic on who could have told anyone about a letter. One dealer who never answered. One who sent four words. And one who'd sold the professor his 1931 copy in 2011, and sold me mine in June, and told me in a different ink to be plain. He was the only person alive, as far as I knew, who touched both the book and my row.

I could have written to him again. But I'd seen what happens to things I write to him.

So on the Saturday I told Ana I was going to look at a bookshop, which was true, and she said take Buddy, he likes the car, which was also true, and I drove two hours north with an old dog asleep on the back seat and the folder on the passenger seat under my phone, like a ticket.

---

Groningen is a nice town and the shop was in the nice part of it, down a street with bicycles leaning on everything. It was exactly what you want a shop like that to be: narrow, deep, smelling of paper and radiator, with a bell on the door that had clearly been chosen for its sound. It was July, and I don't leave a dog in a car in July, so Buddy came in with me, slowly, paying for the step.

The dealer was in the back. He was older than I'd expected, and smaller, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and a cardigan that had been expensive once.

I'd had two hours to think about how to be plain. It's not my best register. I've got charm for three minutes and deflection for the rest of the evening, and neither was any use here.

"My name is James Klippe," I said. "I bought the Tresidder from you in June."

"The second impression." He took the glasses down. "I hope it arrived well."

"It did. I haven't come about the book. I've come to ask you who you told about my letter. And I'd like to tell you why I'm asking before you answer, because you advised me to."

His face did something I'd only seen once before, on Pieter, in a glass box. Then it did something Pieter's hadn't. It looked relieved, the way you're relieved when the dentist finally calls your name.

He looked past me at the door, and then at me.

"May I ask," he said, "who sent you?"

"Nobody. I came on my own. Well." I looked down. "With him."

Buddy had found the one patch of sun on the floorboards and was lowering himself into it in stages, with the grunt. The dealer watched the whole operation. I think the dog did more for me than the sentence did. Whoever he'd been expecting, they don't arrive with a twelve-year-old who needs a minute to lie down.

"I wondered when," he said, mostly to himself. He went and turned the sign on the door to *closed*, and came back. "You'd better sit down, then. Bring him through. He'll want the rug."

---

There was a customer's chair by the desk. I sat in it, and Buddy relocated under the desk, and between the dealer and me was a ledger, an actual ledger with a cloth spine. I went first, because I'd said I would.

I told him the dull version, which is the true one. That I collect differences between printings. That I'd found one sentence in his Tresidder that didn't match the first impression, and wanted a third copy to check whether it was my copy or the whole run. That I'd written to three shops on a Monday night from my kitchen. And that my employer, a bank, runs something every night that makes a list.

Then I took the sheet out of the folder and put it on his desk. Three lines, a number, two words each.

"That's my staff number. The top line has the date of the Monday I wrote to you. I didn't tell anyone at work. I only know it's there because I went looking, for a different reason."

He put the glasses back on. He read the top line for a long time, for two words.

"*Dealer*," he said.

"I think the brackets are you."

He put his hand flat on the ledger while he talked, the way you'd steady a boat.

He'd sold the professor the 1931 printing in 2011. A dry book nobody wanted, from a house clearance. He remembered it because the professor had been so pleased, and the professor was not a man who showed pleasure. A few weeks after, two people had come to the shop. Polite people, a man and a woman, well dressed, with a card from a foundation that looked after archives. They'd been interested in the professor's purchase. They'd asked, very reasonably, whether the shop might let them know if anyone else ever asked for that title. Not the book, I was to understand. They had the book. The *title*. They said certain editions were followed for conservation. A kindness to old books, the woman had called it. They'd left a telephone number. They had not threatened him, and he wanted me to be clear on that. He'd thought it slightly odd and entirely harmless, and written the number inside the back cover of the ledger, and in all the years since nobody had asked for that title.

"And then you wrote to me," he said. He turned the ledger around. There was a line in it, in a neat old hand, on a Monday in June. My name, and the title of the book. "I telephoned that morning. Before I'd answered you."

"What did they say?"

"Thank you." He looked at his hands. "The same woman, I think. Older. She said it was very helpful and that the foundation appreciated it. I took it that they might make you an offer for the professor's copy, if you had it. That is what I would have meant." He paused. "I wrote the postscript because I thought, if they do come, he should be careful what he says about why he wants the book. Dealers get strange about provenance. That is all I thought it was."

"Conservation."

"Yes."

"And when I walked in just now, you asked who sent me."

He didn't answer that. He didn't need to. He'd thought the polite people had come back to ask what else he knew, and he'd been ready to turn the sign and tell them.

---

I wasn't angry. I'd expected to be. I sat in a customer's chair looking at a small old man in a good cardigan who had made one phone call in all that time, and what I felt was what I feel at work, in the worst outages, when you find the line of config that did it and it was written by somebody decent who was doing what the ticket said.

So I did what I do at work. I told him what the thing he'd touched actually does.

I said the number in his ledger wasn't a conservation department, or wasn't only one. I said I didn't know what it was. I said that within a day of his call, a bank he'd never heard of had my name on a nightly list of people who ask questions, next to a hundred others, and that the list goes somewhere I can't find. I said that when I asked at work what the list was, I was told to leave it, and my question went on the list too. I said the professor had found the same sentence I'd found, and forty others, and had died in March with an appointment written in a notebook that he didn't live to keep.

"You didn't do anything wrong," I said. "I need you to hear that. You answered a question, because somebody asked it nicely and gave you a reason. As far as I can tell that's all anybody does for them."

He was quiet for a long time. The radiator ticked. Under the desk Buddy sighed, the long one.

"He asked me once," the dealer said. "The professor. Years after. Whether anyone had been in asking about him." He looked at the ledger. "I said no. Which was true, by then. They had only come the once." He put his glasses on, and took them off again. "I have thought about that answer more than I'd like, since March."

"I'm not here to ask you to do anything."

"No. I see that." He closed the ledger. "I don't believe I shall telephone again."

I'd like to say I just took that and thanked him. But I'd been plain for forty minutes and it seemed a bad moment to stop.

"If you stop, they may notice that too. You've rung that number once, ever, and the one time was me. I don't know what they do with a man who goes quiet."

"I am seventy-four and I sell books that nobody wants," he said. "Let them notice."

It was the first time I'd told anyone what their small job was for, and he'd simply stopped. He had also made himself visible on my account, an old man in a cardigan, and on the way up I hadn't given that one minute's thought. I wrote it down in the car, because it belonged in the same folder as the rest.

---

He gave me something before I left. I didn't ask for it. He went into the back and came out with a file and took one sheet from it: his copy of the receipt from 2011, for the 1931 printing. The professor's name. The date. The price, which was nothing, about a tenth of what I'd paid. And in the corner, in the same neat hand, two words.

*Second impression.*

"He always wanted it written down, which one," the dealer said. "On everything I sold him, even where there was only the one printing. I thought it was an affectation." He almost smiled. "I'm a bookseller. I thought it was a *nice* affectation."

I put the receipt in the folder with page 118 and the three rows. I thanked him and meant it. Buddy needed two attempts to get up, and the dealer held the door for him, with the bell.

---

Ana rang when I was on the ring road. I put her on the speaker.

"Did you buy anything?"

"No."

"You went to a bookshop two hours away and bought nothing."

"I didn't go to buy. The man who wrote to me, the careful one. I wanted to ask him something in person."

There was a pause on the line, and it wasn't a bad pause. It was the pause of someone who has just heard a straight answer where she'd expected the charming one, and is deciding what to do with it.

"And did you?"

"Yes."

"Is this the measuring thing?"

"It's the measuring thing."

"Okay, Mulder," she said. "Drive safe. Give Buddy a biscuit from me."

I gave Buddy a biscuit. He took it without fully waking up, which is a skill.

I drove south with the folder on the seat beside me, thicker by one sheet, and somewhere past Zwolle I passed a long straight stretch of canal road with the water flat beside it. I didn't know yet what had happened there, and I didn't slow down.
