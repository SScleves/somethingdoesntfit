# Washington, 1953

They met on a Wednesday in January, in a room in Washington arranged by the Central Intelligence Agency, and they were done by Saturday. Four days.

Five men. H. P. Robertson, who chaired it, a physicist from Caltech who had worked for the government through the war and after it. Luis Alvarez, who would win the Nobel Prize fifteen years later for something else entirely. Lloyd Berkner, Samuel Goudsmit, Thornton Page. Physicists, an astronomer, the kind of men who are asked to sit on panels because their names end arguments. Air Force officers came in to brief them, and an astronomer from Ohio State called Allen Hynek sat in, who was the Air Force's consultant on the subject and who would spend the rest of his life changing his mind about it.

The subject was flying saucers. The Agency called them unidentified flying objects, which was newer and sounded less like a joke, and the reason the Agency was interested at all was not that it believed in them. It was that the previous summer, in July, so many people had reported them over Washington that the reports had jammed the channels the Air Force used for real things. Radar operators at National Airport had tracked them. Jets had been scrambled. The newspapers had the story for a week. And somebody at the Agency had looked at the volume of traffic and thought, not *what are they*, but *what would happen if the Russians did this on purpose, the night before they did something else*.

So: five scientists, four days, and a question that was really about the telephone.

---

They watched films. That is documented, and it matters, because the films were the best evidence the Air Force had. One had been taken by a Navy warrant officer in Utah, over Tremonton, the summer before: a cluster of bright dots moving against a blue sky, which Navy photo analysts had spent about a thousand hours on and concluded were not birds, not balloons, not aircraft. The panel watched it, and watched it again, and decided they were birds. Seagulls, probably. In the sunlight.

They heard the cases. They heard about radar returns and weather inversions. They heard Hynek, who was not yet the man he would become, say what the Air Force had hired him to say. They were, by every account including their own, unimpressed, and they were unimpressed quickly, in the way of clever men who have been asked to look at something beneath them and intend to be fair about it for exactly as long as it takes.

By Saturday they had a report. It was short, and it was secret. A cut version was let out a few years later. The full text was not public until the 1970s, and when it was, people who had spent twenty years being laughed at read it and understood, for the first time, that the laughing had been a recommendation.

---

The panel found no evidence that the objects were a direct threat to national security. That was the headline, and it was true, and if the report had stopped there nobody would remember it.

It did not stop there. It found that the reports themselves were the threat. That the continued emphasis on them was *resulting in a threat to the orderly functioning of the protective organs of the body politic*. That the channels could be clogged. That the public could be made to panic, or could be trained to ignore real things.

And so it recommended a program. Of *training and debunking*.

That is the word. It is in the document. *Debunking.* The aim, the report said, was to reduce public interest in flying saucers, and the means were television, motion pictures and popular articles.

It suggested who might help. Psychologists familiar with mass psychology. Advertising experts. It suggested, by name, that the Walt Disney company might be approached, because of its experience with the public. It noted that the amateur groups which had formed around the subject, the civilian clubs with their newsletters, *should be watched because of their potentially great influence on mass thinking*.

Then the five men signed it and went home. Robertson to Caltech. Alvarez to Berkeley and, in time, to Stockholm. It was four days' work.

---

Here is what is not in the report, and should be said.

Whether anyone ever ran the program as written is disputed. What is not disputed is that the Air Force officers who spent the next decade explaining lights in the sky as Venus or a balloon, to farmers and pilots and policemen who knew what Venus looked like, could not have been working from the report. It was secret. Most of them never saw it. If they were given a reason, the reason was clogged channels and the public good, and it was believable, because it was partly true.

Hynek was in the room. He went on explaining for another thirteen years, and then in Michigan in 1966 he offered *swamp gas* to a room full of reporters as a possible explanation for some lights, and the country laughed at him, and something in him finished turning. He spent the rest of his life on the other side. The point is that he did the work for thirteen years, and that the work got done.

Five of the cleverest men in the country, four days, and a recommendation that whatever people were seeing, the thing to do about it was to make the subject ridiculous.

The subject did become ridiculous. For a generation, in the richest country on earth, a pilot who reported something he could not identify knew he would be laughed at, and mostly didn't. The clubs with the newsletters became a joke, and the joke became the word, and the word is still the word.

If you want to read it, it is online. Look for the Robertson Panel, January 1953. It has been public for decades, and it has never once been news.
