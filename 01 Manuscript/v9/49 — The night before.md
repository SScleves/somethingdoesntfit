# The night before

We left on Sunday after dark, with a Doberman in the back and twelve envelopes in a supermarket bag on my lap, and I'd like to say we had a plan. We had a list and a full tank. Ana had a thermos. I had a stomach that had stopped working on Friday and hadn't sent anyone to explain.

Ana drove. She's a better driver than me in every way you can measure, and in a few you can't, like how she doesn't take it personally when a Volkswagen cuts in. I only drive when she lets me, which is mostly when she wants to sleep. She drove the way she does anything she's decided on, both hands, radio off, and Mora's head between the seats breathing on the gearstick like a second engine.

Buddy was at Frank's. That had been its own negotiation. Ana's mother lives up two flights with no lift, and Buddy stopped doing stairs the year before, so that left Frank, who lives alone in a flat with a bike in the hallway and a fridge full of oat milk and had never once been responsible for anything that breathes.

"What does he eat?"

"His food. It's in the bag. Not your food."

"What if he wants my food?"

"He'll want your food. He's a dog. Say no."

"I can't say no to that face, mate. Look at it." Buddy was looking at him from the bottom of the stairs to the flat, Frank's on the first floor, with the expression of an old man who has been told about the stairs and is filing a complaint. "Do I carry him?"

"You carry him."

He carried him. Twenty-eight kilos of gray dog up a narrow Rotterdam staircase, Frank going red and saying *fuck* on every third step, Buddy calm in his arms like a bishop being moved to a new diocese. At the top Frank put him down and said, "I still don't believe any of it, you know," and I said I knew, and he said, "Go on. Go. I've got the dog," and did the skull at me with his actual face, which I'd never seen him do without a phone.

---

The list was twelve names. Twelve people who would sit in a room at nine on Monday morning and, at item four, confirm the trustees of a foundation that had been invoicing their bank per flagged person since 1994. Eleven of them had never heard my name. The twelfth was Anneke, who'd had her sheet since Wednesday and read it twice, which I'm told is a thing she does not do.

Each envelope had a sheet. For the board the last box said something different from the others. Their names weren't missing from the list; they were the list, practically. So it said: *On Monday you will be asked to confirm these trustees. This is what the foundation charges, and for what. These are the people it keeps. These are your staff who are not kept. This is your desk, and the three other people who have been sold it. Please check before you vote.*

*Please check.* I'd rewritten that line eleven times. Every version that sounded clever sounded crazy.

"Read me the first one," Ana said.

"Leiderdorp. A woman. Treasury, twenty years. Two kids, one horse."

"How do you know about the horse?"

"She's got a horse in her profile picture."

"That's not knowing about a horse, James. That's a picture."

She was right. I wrote *horse?* next to it, like a man doing something.

---

Leiderdorp was a letterbox. A neat brick house with a light on downstairs and the blue flicker of a television, and I'd decided, we'd decided, at the kitchen table, with the kettle, not to knock. A man at your door at nine on a Sunday night with a document is a man you call the police about. A letter is a letter.

So I walked up the path with the envelope like it was a parcel of something alive, and I put it through the slot, and the slot had one of those brush things inside, and the envelope stuck. Half in, half out. I pushed. It folded. I pulled it back to straighten it and the brush took the corner off.

Inside, a dog started barking. A small one, the kind that barks with its whole body.

I stood there with half a sheet of evidence in my hand and the other half somewhere in a stranger's hallway, and the television went quiet.

I ran. I'm not proud of it. I ran back down the path and got in the car and said "Go, go, go," and Ana didn't go, because Ana doesn't go for anybody who says it three times. She looked at me, and at the torn corner in my hand, and said, "Did you just post half of it?"

"Most of it. Eighty percent. The important part."

"Which part's in your hand?"

I looked. The bottom right corner. The word *check.*

"Fuck," I said.

She laughed so hard she had to put her forehead on the wheel, and Mora barked once at nothing, in solidarity, and the hall light came on in the house and we drove away with our lights off for the first twenty meters like idiots in a film, and then turned them on because it's illegal.

"Good start," Ana said. "Very professional."

---

After that I had a system. I'm good at systems. I folded each envelope once more, and I held the flap of the letterbox up with two fingers, and I fed the envelope in slowly, like you'd feed a pill to a dog, and I want it understood that this was the most competent I was all night.

The Hague, two, ten minutes apart. Both letterboxes, both fine, both in streets so quiet I could hear the canal. At the second one a cat watched me from a windowsill with the face of a cat who had seen men post things before and thought less of all of them.

Delft. Leidschendam. Wassenaar. At Wassenaar the house had a gate, and the gate had an intercom, and the letterbox was on the far side of the gate. We sat in the car for four minutes looking at it.

"Throw it over," Ana said.

"It's a bank document."

"It's an envelope. Throw it."

I threw it over. It went beautifully, a little spin on it, and landed face down on a wet gravel drive about a meter from the door, and I stood at the gate in the drizzle and thought about everything I'd ever read about the history of the world, the edits, the forty-one books, the hill in Turkey, the professor in his blue chair, and all of it came down to this: a man in a damp coat watching an envelope get rained on in Wassenaar.

A light went on in the porch. The door opened. A man in a sweater came out, looked at the envelope, looked at the gate, looked at me.

"Klippe?" he said.

I didn't say anything. My mouth wouldn't.

He bent and picked the envelope up and shook the water off it. "Anneke rang. Four of us, this afternoon. She said a man might come with an envelope and that I should read it and then ring her." He looked at the car behind me, at the Doberman's head in the back window, ears up, like a hood ornament. "She didn't say you'd throw it."

"There's a gate."

"There's a bell on the gate."

"I didn't want to ring the bell."

He nodded like that was a reasonable thing for a grown man to say. "I'll read it tonight." Then, because he was Dutch and it was raining: "Do you want a coffee?"

I said we had more to do. He said, "Then go," and went in, and I walked back to the car, and Ana said, "Well?" and I said, "Anneke's been ringing people," and Ana started the engine and said, "Good," in a voice that told me how scared she'd been and wasn't going to say.

So that was two already turned. That's the thing nobody tells you about doing something like this: halfway through, it stops being yours. Anneke had rung four. One of the four, in Voorschoten, met us at the door at half past eleven in a dressing gown with her own copy already printed, because Anneke had scanned hers and sent it, and she held out my envelope back to me and said, "I don't need two. Give it to someone who hasn't got one." It was the first time anyone had given me anything back in the whole year. I sat in the car afterwards and couldn't talk for about a minute, and Ana let me, and Mora put her nose in my ear, which is wet and enormous and didn't help at all, and helped.

---

The wrong house was in Zoetermeer.

Number fourteen. I walked up to number fourteen, I fed the envelope in with my two-finger pill technique, and I was halfway back down the path when the door opened behind me and a very large man in boxer shorts said, "Hey."

I turned around. He had my envelope in his hand.

"This isn't me," he said.

"It's fourteen?"

"Fourteen B." He pointed. Next door. A second door I hadn't seen, round the side, with a second bell. He looked at the name on the envelope. "That's the guy upstairs. He's never in. He's got a boat."

"Okay."

"What is it?"

I have spent eleven years at a bank explaining things to people who don't want them explained, and in that moment, in Zoetermeer, at a quarter to midnight, with a stranger in boxer shorts holding the end of the world, the best I had was: "It's about his pension."

"At midnight?"

"It's urgent. For a pension."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he said, "Okay, man," and posted it through the B door himself, carefully, with two fingers, and went back inside. I'd like to think he went to bed and never thought about it again. I'd like to think a lot of things.

"Wrong house?" Ana said.

"Wrong house."

"Was it one of them?"

"It was a guy with a boat. Next door. His neighbor's got a boat."

"So it was nobody."

"It was a very large nobody in his underwear."

She nodded. "This is how they're going to make the film," she said. "The underwear guy. They'll cut everything else."

---

The van picked us up after Zoetermeer, on the A12.

White, no windows in the back, the kind every plumber in the country drives. It came onto the motorway at a junction behind us and stayed there. Three cars back. Then two. Then three again. Ana saw it first. She said, "There's a van," in exactly the voice she'd use for *there's a sign,* and I turned round, and Mora, who'd been asleep across the back seat with her legs in the air like a dead insect, turned over and sat up and looked out the back window and went completely still. She never goes still. She's a Doberman. She was built still and then someone forgot to turn it on.

"What do we do?"

"Nothing." My voice came out about a third higher than normal. "We drive. If they're following us they're following us to a letterbox. That's a fingerprint. They don't do fingerprints."

"You don't know that."

"I've read their paperwork."

"You've read their paperwork," Ana said, and changed lanes, and the van didn't, and then it did.

Forty kilometers. I counted. I counted the way I count everything when I'm scared, which is out loud in my head: exits, kilometer markers, the number of times Mora's ears moved. I held the supermarket bag of envelopes on my lap with both arms like it was a child that might jump out. I thought about Henk Boersma in his canal. I thought about Noor's message, *there's a van on my street,* and then nothing. I thought, very clearly, *if this is it, I'd like it to be after the next envelope, at least, so I've done eight.*

At the junction for Gouda, the van indicated, politely, in plenty of time, and came off, and was gone.

Mora watched the empty road behind us for another minute. Then she lay back down with a groan and put her chin on my seat.

"That was a van," Ana said.

"That was a van."

"I've decided it was a van."

"Good decision."

We never talked about it again. I've thought about it plenty. I think it was a van, and I think those were the longest forty kilometers of my life, and I think both of those things are true, and that's what they do to you, in the end. You can never again just see a van.

---

At midnight I was at the depot.

"I'm not coming in," Ana said, in the car park, with the engine running. "I'm sitting here with the dog. If you're not out by one, I'm coming in with the dog, and I'm not asking anyone's badge."

"That's—"

"That's not a negotiation."

The building looked the way it always looked, low and long and gray behind its car park, the foundation's name on the sign and a smaller name under it, and no lights on the desk floor where I'd eaten a sandwich at desk forty during the drill. One light upstairs, behind the glass door.

Aylin had propped the outer door open with a fire extinguisher. That's the detail I can't get over. In a year of watching her, the pencil, the cards squared to the edge of the desk, the visitor book in its own gray box, I'd never seen her let a single thing lean on anything. A fire extinguisher, against a door, at midnight. It was the loudest thing she ever said to me.

She walked me up without a word. Her badge said *A. Pekcan, Keeper of Collections*, and she'd clipped it on to come in at midnight for this, which I also can't get over. The reading room was lit by the one lamp on the long table. The visitor book was on it, open at 1931, and beside it a flat scanner, the kind libraries use, already warm.

"Forty minutes," she said. "He reads on Sunday nights. He comes at one."

"Okay."

"The slip."

I had it in my coat, with the notebook. My own capitals, the shelfmark, the date, and across the bottom in her neat ink, *in conservation*, and her initials. She took it to her desk and sat down and uncapped a pen, and drew one straight line through the two words, the way you'd strike a wrong figure in a ledger, so that it could still be read. Under it she wrote *on shelf*, and the date, and initialed that too.

"I've never corrected a status in nine years," she said. "There. Now I have." Then she got up and went through the door at the back, the one to the stacks, and I heard a drawer, and she came back with a gray folder and put it on the table at the far end from the scanner, and went to her desk and sat down with her back to me and picked up a card, and I understood the deal. She wouldn't watch. She wouldn't help. She wouldn't stop me. And she'd brought the item, because a request had been made in her reading room, and in her reading room requests are answered.

I did the book first, because the book was for Anneke. My hands were shaking so badly I put the first page in upside down. Then the second. Somewhere around page ten I got a rhythm, lift, flatten, lid, button, the little light crawling across, and I stopped reading the words, on purpose, because I had forty minutes and because I was afraid of whose names I'd see. You can't not see some. *Seated.* *Quiet.* *Deceased.* *Quiet.* The same few words for ninety-five years in a dozen different hands, and I turned the pages like a man stacking plates.

And under my feet, the whole time, the floor hummed.

I'd heard it at the drill and filed it under air conditioning. It wasn't. It was lower, steadier, a big thing kept running in a room under a room: a generator, for a hundred desks sold four times, running on a Sunday night for nobody. Mora would have hated it. She hates the dishwasher.

I finished the book at twenty to one. I put it back exactly how it had been, open at the front, and slid the memory card into the last envelope, the twelfth, Anneke's, and went down the table to the folder.

---

I'd like to tell you what it was in order, because the order is how I understood it.

It was a letter. Three sheets of thin paper, folded once, the folds gone soft, in a hand I knew from the margins of a flood survey, because I'd spent a year with his sentence and a man's handwriting gets into you. At the top, *Urfa*, and a date in the summer of 1929. At the bottom, *Tresidder*. It was to his publisher in London. The folder's label was the shelfmark from the professor's last page, in a hand from before Aylin's, and a note in pencil: *correspondence file, transferred Leiden*.

Most of it was business. He was sorry about the delay with the corrections. He had been ill. He was staying in the house of a family outside the town, and the grandfather of the house had died in the week, and he had been in the room.

Then this. I copied it word for word, and I'll give it to you the way I copied it, because I'm not going to improve on him.

*The old man had my hand. I do not think he knew whose it was. He said a thing to me in his own language, and the son, who has some English, gave it to me afterward at the door, and I set it down here while I have it exactly: "Don't be afraid. Hold on to whoever is holding your hand." I have heard this before. My aunt said it to me, in Devon, when I was nine and she was dying, and I have never told anyone, because a boy of nine does not know what he has heard and a man of fifty does not like to say. I have now heard it twice, from two people who could not have met, in two languages, both of them going. I do not know what it is. I propose to put the plain fact of it into the next impression, in a note to the chapter on the river peoples, with no more claim than that it was said. You will tell me the trade will not like it. I find I no longer mind what the trade likes.*

Then he said he was going east for a fortnight, on foot, to look at some stones a shepherd had told him about, and would write from there.

He didn't write from there.

I sat at the long table with the professor's shelfmark in a dead man's folder and my notebook open beside it, and I read the sentence three times. Then I turned back a page in the notebook to Henk, in Marijke's kitchen, in my handwriting: *said not to be frightened, only to hold on.* And the eleven handles under it, with their endings. *Hold on to whoever's got your hand. Hold on to the one holding you. Hold on to whoever is holding your hand*, twice.

Two copies is evidence. I'd had the eleven and Henk, and that was two copies by anyone's count, and I'd still half believed I'd been reading too much into a forum. Here was paper. Ninety-six years old, in an archive, requested by a professor who never came, marked *in conservation* in front of me by a woman who knew it was on the shelf. The same words. Not the idea of them. The words.

And the next impression had come out in 1931, two years after he walked into the hills, and the note wasn't in it, and the chapter on the river peoples had lost a hand instead.

I copied the letter into the notebook. All three sheets, the business and the illness and the stones, not just the sentence, because a copy you've trimmed is a copy somebody can argue with. It took me twelve minutes and my hand hurt. Then I put the sheets back in the order they'd been, folded the way they'd been, closed the folder, and left it on the table where she'd put it.

I didn't take it. I want to say that plainly, because I could have, and the man who'd taken the thin book from my kitchen had taught me exactly how much good that does. It was hers. It was the archive's. A letter in a reading room is a thing anyone can be sent to see. A letter in my coat is a thing I say I've got.

"The last column," Aylin said, without turning around. "Next to you. What does it say now?"

I looked. December. My name, in her hand. One word.

"Pending."

"Good." She turned a card over. "When it changes, I'd like to be the one who writes it. I think I've earned that much." Then, still not turning: "You left it on the table."

"It's yours."

"It's the archive's." She capped her pen. "He'd have taken it. The professor. He argued for two pages. You asked for it on a slip and you've copied it and left it where it goes." She put the card in the drawer. "I'll put it back on the shelf myself. Nobody has asked for it in ninety years, and now two men have in one, and it's going to be where the card says it is when the next one does."

I went down through the dark floor and out past the fire extinguisher and Ana had the car moving before my door was closed.

"Well?"

"I've got the book. All of it."

"And?"

I had the notebook in my hand. I hadn't put it away. "And it's written down," I said. "In 1929. The sentence. Somebody wrote it down and sent it to London, and then he walked into the hills."

She didn't ask what sentence. She knew. She'd heard the half of it I'd posted, read over my shoulder on the kitchen floor on Thursday. She drove for a bit.

"Henk's," she said.

"Henk's. And the eleven."

"Okay," said Ana, and that was all, and I think it was the second time in fifteen years I'd heard her say the word that way.

---

Three more after that. Utrecht, a letterbox, no lights. Bunnik, a man who opened the door himself at two in the morning because he couldn't sleep, read the first paragraph standing in his socks under the hall light, said "Fuck" very quietly, then "Thank you," and closed the door. And one we couldn't do, a farmhouse past Woerden at the end of a dike road, no car, no lights, nobody. I put the envelope through the door anyway.

"He's away," Ana said. "That's one who votes without reading it."

"I know."

"Ring Anneke."

"It's three in the morning."

"Ring Anneke."

I rang Anneke. She answered on the second ring in a voice that had not been asleep, listened, said, "I'll try him. Thank you. Go to the next one," and hung up.

"I like her," Ana said.

"She approved the invoice thirty times."

"And then she read it."

---

There was no next one. There was only Anneke's.

She lives in a terraced house in Rotterdam-Kralingen with a bike chained to the railing and a pot of something dead on the step. We got there at ten to six, in the first gray, and I sat in the car with the twelfth envelope for a while because I'd suddenly forgotten how to stand up. A year. Twelve envelopes. A torn corner in my coat pocket that said *check.*

I walked up the three steps. I lifted my hand to knock.

The door opened before I did.

She was dressed. Not in a dressing gown; properly dressed, for work, at ten to six on a Monday morning, shoes on, glasses on her head. She looked like she'd been standing in her hall for some time. Her right hand was tucked under her left arm, flat against her side, the way you'd hold a hand you'd shut in a door.

I knew it. I'd done it on my own kitchen floor. I'd seen Noor do it at her table and Marijke describe it in a doorway. I'd never thought I'd see it on the head of risk.

I held out the envelope. "The visitor book. All of it. Nineteen thirty-one to now."

She took it with her left hand. She didn't open it.

"I've read the sheet," she said. "All of it. Twice." She looked past me at the car, at Ana with her head back against the headrest, at the dog's ears in the back window. Then she looked at me, and her voice did something I hadn't heard it do.

"The page from the job. The reason column. You blacked the numbers out."

"They're employee numbers. They're not mine to give you."

"One of them is mine."

I didn't say anything.

"The fourth row. *Asked (job).* The week after yours. I asked my own director what the line reconciled, on the telephone, in October, because an engineer had put it on a table in front of me in a room I'd been told was about an archive. One question. And I'm a row." She took her hand out from under her arm and looked at it, and put it back. "It's been cold since I read that. That was Wednesday. It comes and goes."

"Yes."

"You know what this is." It wasn't a question. "Is it true? What the sentence says. What they count."

I'd been asked that once, by a widow in Zwolle, and I hadn't had an answer, and I didn't now, and I'd learned at least not to pretend.

"A hand, twice," I said. "On a kitchen floor, and at a grave. That's all I've had. I don't know."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way you'd sign off a figure you'd checked yourself.

"Go home, Mr Klippe."

"The farmhouse. Woerden."

"I got him at four. He's driving back. He's not happy." She almost smiled. "Go home."

She closed the door. I stood on her step and looked at the dead plant for a while.

When I got back in the car, Ana didn't ask. She looked at me and I said, "Twelve," and she put her forehead down on the steering wheel, just for a second, and then lifted it and started the engine.

"Frank's," she said. "Buddy. Then bed. And then you're going to go and hit a ball with a stick and not be anywhere near any of this."

"It's Monday."

"Monday you hit a ball with a stick and keep your phone face down." She pulled out. "You've done your bit, Mulder. Now you just have to not be in the room."

I was asleep before Kralingen. The last thing I remember is Mora's chin landing on my shoulder, heavy and warm and smelling, I'm sorry to say, very strongly of whatever she'd found in Anneke's front garden.
