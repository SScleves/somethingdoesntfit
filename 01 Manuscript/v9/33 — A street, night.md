# A street, night

He walked the street twice, once in each direction, because a man who walks a street once is going somewhere and a man who walks it twice has lost something, and people forgive the second.

The Doberman didn't start up. He'd expected it to and had planned for the wait, and the silence told him what he'd been told the week before, which was that the wife had gone and taken the loud one. He stood for a moment at the corner with his hands in his coat and let the information settle, the way you let a drink settle, before you decide what it's for.

The gray car was in the drive. It had been in the drive on Monday at ten and at three, and on Tuesday at eleven, and a man who is at home on a weekday with his car outside is a man who has decided to be at home. There was something new over the front door, white, the size of an egg, with a small red light in it. He admired that. It was pointed at the path.

What he had been asked for was simple. The pages had been photographed in March, all of them, flat and square. What was wanted now was the original, because a notebook in two hands is a different thing from a photograph of one, and because it should never have gone to a market in a crate. He had been asked to recover it. He had been given a date for the visit, four days off, and a method, which was the usual one: ring the bell, wipe your feet, be kind. Nobody had told him what to do about a man who would not leave his own kitchen.

The lights were on. Kitchen, and one upstairs. Through the kitchen window, the end of a table, a coat over something on it, a shelf by the far wall with a sleeve of pale green on it, needles still in.

The old dog was at the window.

Not barking. Standing, with its front feet on the low sill where the radiator was, looking out, at him, steadily, the way it had looked at him from a doorway all one afternoon in October. He looked back. He'd always liked dogs, and the liking was not a tool, whatever the people above him assumed, and the old dog knew it wasn't, and had decided that it didn't matter, and he respected that more than he respected most of what he'd been told in rooms.

"Hello, foreman," he said, quietly, to the glass.

The dog didn't move.

He walked to the end of the street and along the back lane, where the gates were, counting, and stopped at the fourth, and looked at the latch, which was the kind that lifts, and at the gap under the gate, which was the width of a hand, and at the bowl just inside it. He had stepped over that bowl with a bag of ice. The old dog was let out last thing at night and drank from it, and from no other. The man had told him so himself, at this gate, in October.

He stood there for a time that he did not measure.

Then he crouched, and did what he had come to the lane to do, which took less time than tying a shoe, and walked back along the lane, and round, to the car. He'd parked it at the far end, under the one streetlight that was out, which he'd arranged in September with a catapult and a ball bearing, because it was a long job and a man thinks ahead. He sat. He didn't start the engine. He took the flask out of the glovebox and found it empty and put it back.

He didn't look at his phone. He didn't look at the house. He looked at the windshield, at the drops on it, and he thought about a sentence, the way he sometimes did, turning it to see if it had a side he hadn't seen. *A frightened wife is a seat we don't have to pay for.* It had a side. The side was that the wife had gone, and had taken the loud dog, and the man had stayed with the one that couldn't do stairs. A man like that does not leave the house for a date on a schedule. He leaves it for one thing.

At eleven the kitchen light went off. At twenty past, the one upstairs.

At ten past two the kitchen light came on. At a quarter past, the front door, and the man in his socks with something large in a blanket, and the gray car going out of the drive faster than it had ever gone in. The egg over the door watched it leave.

He gave it five minutes. Then he went in by the lane and the gate and the kitchen door, which was not locked, because a man carrying a dog does not go back to lock a door. He did not turn on a light. He lifted the coat. There was a page with a bank's name on it, and under it two green books that he left, and a folder that he left, and a thin one with no title on the spine. He took the thin one. He put the coat back as it had been. He was in the house for under two minutes, and he did not go near the front.

He drove to a flat with a name on the bell that wasn't his, and fed a cat.

In the morning he wrote three lines. *Item recovered. Wife absent. Subject not approached.* He read them twice before he sent them. He did not write a line about the dog. Nobody had asked for that part, and he had learned that the lines nobody asked for were the ones that got read.
