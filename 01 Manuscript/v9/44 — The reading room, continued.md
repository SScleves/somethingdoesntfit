# The reading room, continued

The generator stopped.

I don't know how long it ran. Long enough for the tray to stop shivering against the table; I'd been watching the ledger shake on its wedges. The old man had waited for it to finish the way you wait for a truck to pass before you go on with a sentence. The woman by the window hadn't moved at all.

"Tests," he said. "They test it on Mondays. I've asked them not to and they tell me it has to be Mondays. One doesn't argue with engineers." He smiled at me. "Does one."

I'd practiced a first sentence at two sets of traffic lights. It went: *I'd like to ask for an item.* It wasn't a sentence I needed. There'd been a pad of request slips on Aylin's desk downstairs, next to the visitor book, the kind every reading room has, and I'd taken one while she was turning the big book toward me, because I was a reader and that's what readers do.

I filled it in on my knee under the table, in pencil, in capitals, so there'd be no argument about my handwriting. A letter, four digits, a slash, two more. My name. The date. I didn't look at my phone to check it. I'd checked it against Noor's slip in her hall, one character at a time, and I'd had it by heart for three weeks.

Then I put it on the table, square to the edge, on his side of the tray, and took my hand away.

He didn't pick it up. He read it where it lay, upside down, which is a thing I'm used to watching people do, and he looked, for a moment, almost pleased.

"He asked too," he said.

He didn't say who. He didn't have to.

"Two pages of argument and four lines of request. We answer requests. He was given this day, and he didn't come, and then you did, carrying his date around like a man who's inherited a dentist." He turned the slip round with one finger so that it faced me. "Nobody else has, you know. In nine years. People write to us about their rights. They write to us about Sumerians. You've written down a shelfmark and asked for it, at the desk, on the day. It's very nearly moving."

"Can I see it?"

He lifted his voice very slightly, not turning his head. "Aylin."

She'd been in the doorway. I don't know for how long. She came to the table with her glasses on their cord and he slid the slip toward her with the same finger.

"Mark it, would you. In conservation."

She looked at the slip. Then she looked at him, and it was the second time I'd seen her face move. She knew the shelfmark. You could see her know it: a woman who knows where every item in her building is, and that this one was on its shelf, sound, twenty meters from where she stood.

"In conservation," she said.

"If you would."

She wrote it, in ink, in a neat hand, across the bottom of my slip where the status goes, and initialed it, and the ink was still wet when she put it back on the table in front of me, and she went back to the doorway without looking at anybody.

So: I'd asked. I'd done the one thing a dead man wrote at the bottom of a page and a woman in Leiden told me to do before anything else. And what I'd got for it was a piece of paper that said the item I'd asked for was unavailable, in somebody else's handwriting, which, if you think about it, is the only thing they ever give anyone.

"Thank you," I said, and put it in my pocket. It had her initials on it.

---

"You've got a bag," the old man said.

"I've got a bag."

"Everyone who gets this far has a bag." He folded his hands. "Go on, then."

I'd thought about the order for three weeks, and in the end I did it the way I'd do it at work, which is: start with the thing on their screen.

I took out the two green books. Tresidder, 1926. The professor's, first impression, and mine, second. I opened them both at 118 and put one on either side of the tray, so that the ledger lay between them, open at its page with the one line struck through and the other written beside it.

Then I got a pencil out and did what I do.

I'm going to describe it, because the thing itself is dull and the dullness is the point. The ledger entry was a printer's job line, in Dutch, in a hand with no interest in being read: a customer, a title, a quantity, a page number. One hundred and eighteen. Beside the struck line somebody had written in a smaller hand, in English, eight words. I read them against the professor's copy, left to right, with the pencil. They matched the first impression, word for word: *appear to have been transmitted to the early settled*. The struck line. Then I read the line written beside it against my copy. *Appear to have arisen among the first settled*. The second impression. Letter for letter.

I ticked each word in both books, lightly, in pencil, the way a proofreader does. Nobody stopped me. I'm a reader and they were my books.

"You've put the two impressions either side of your own order book," I said. "The struck line is the first printing. The line beside it is the second. The second says it's the first. I found the difference on my kitchen floor in April, and I've found what ordered it on your tray, and I've just checked them against each other with a pencil. Those are three copies that agree. In my work that's not an argument. That's a finding."

"It's very tidy," the old man said. He was looking at the pencil ticks, not at me. "I've always liked tidy."

I put the rest down. Not fast. Each one where I could reach it.

The captures: a list of staff numbers and two words each, printed, with three rows circled in my own pen. *Wrote (dealer). Asked (job). Posted (forum).* "This is what runs at the bank at fourteen minutes past two. I caught it myself. It's called bc-recon-nightly now, with an owner and a ticket. This is the only place the old name still exists."

The invoice sheet. Thirty-one years, one line per quarter, pulled from the bank's own ledgers one evening. "Reconciliation, per flagged person. Six steps. The last one was the week I asked."

For the first time, the woman by the window turned her head. A few degrees. Enough that I saw her face properly: a face from a photograph on an intranet page. Anneke de Wit, head of risk, whose department had approved that invoice, or whose signature had, thirty-odd times, sitting in a reading room on a Monday in December watching an engineer from her own bank put it on a table.

She didn't speak. But she looked at the sheet, and she kept looking at it, and the old man noticed that.

The accounts. Thirty-one of them, downloaded from the register for nothing, with an envelope on top in Frank's handwriting: two columns of years. "Your own grants. Films, a publisher, a retreat center, in the years the count was low. A friend of mine found that on his own, at my kitchen table, with a pen. Twenty-oh-seven's cold and there's nothing. We don't know why."

The old man looked at the envelope a little longer than at the others. "We fund a great many things," he said. "Some of them are embarrassing. That's philanthropy."

The watcher. Five weeks of printouts, a page a night. "I copy the forum at 02:14 and show what changed. Questions get left alone. Anyone who describes a last minute, a place and a hand, gets dressed up inside four days and then stops. Four so far. My own post, which I deleted for a woman in Leiden, has been put back six times, a sentence longer each time."

"You're very thorough," he said.

The schedule page, with my acknowledgment on it, and in the margin, in my hand, what had happened at eight of the eleven addresses. *Canal. Sold. Care. Quiet. Quiet. Quiet. Quiet.* And the eighth, added on Sunday morning: *Care. House listed.* Beside our own line, one word, and then: *Nobody came to the door. Round the back, four nights early. Dog lived.*

And last, because it was the only thing on the table I hadn't made or pulled, Henk Boersma's words in my notebook, and next to them a steel flask.

He read it all. He took longer over my handwriting than the typed parts. He read Henk's two lines twice. Then he put everything square on the tray, beside the ledger, and he sat back and did something I hadn't expected, which was laugh.

Not at me. A small, delighted sound, the laugh of an old man who's been shown a card trick and seen how it's done and likes it more for that.

"Oh, this is very good," he said. "Anneke, isn't this good?"

She didn't answer.

"They all come in here with the old things. The pyramids. The flood. The thing in Turkey. They sit where you're sitting and tell me about Sumerians, and I listen, because it's polite, and it's never once been interesting, because the old things are a *result*. You've brought me the thing being done. With a pencil." He said *pencil* like a vintage. "And I'll tell you what I liked best, since you've earned it. Annex C."

"The annex isn't on the table."

"No. It's in a file at your bank with your initials on it, and it's the cleanest reconciliation that program has had in years. Every name checked against a record. Every vendor allocation signed for. The pets clause read, I'm told, and initialed anyway." He inclined his head. "Thank you for that. Genuinely. It saved somebody a week."

I'd known that was coming, in some form, since Pieter's office. It still landed.

"You haven't said one of them's wrong," I said.

"Mr. Klippe." He folded his hands. "I'm going to do you the courtesy of not insulting you, and I'd ask the same. You've put nine things on this table and asked me, in effect, to confirm each one, and I'm not going to, because I don't need to, and because each of them could be explained in a court by a bored man in an hour. A printer's correction. A vendor's job with an owner and a ticket. A charity's grants. A forum. An invoice for a service you don't understand. Some sad things that happened in the east of the country in an autumn." He tilted his head. "You know what they are. I know what they are. What they are *in a room* is nothing."

"Then why am I in one?"

He didn't answer that. He reached, instead, into the inside pocket of his jacket, and took out a single folded sheet, and unfolded it, and I knew the form before he'd got it flat, because I'd signed it on the hood of a car.

"Anneke," he said, "you should hear this. It's from Mr. Klippe's own employer. A wellbeing referral, from September." He read it in a kind voice, like a man reading a reference. "*Recent bereavement (family). Disturbed sleep. Preoccupation with historical documents.* Signed by his manager." He paused. "And countersigned by Mr. Klippe. On the same day. Which I thought was very honest of him."

He folded it and put it back in his pocket.

That's what the room was for. I understood it the way you understand a dashboard that's been built for somebody else. It wasn't for me. Nothing I'd put on the tray had been read by the man across from me; it had been *watched being read* by the woman at the window, who approved an invoice thirty times a year and had come to see what kind of person complained about it. And now she'd heard: a man who'd lost somebody, who didn't sleep, who was preoccupied with old documents, which, to be fair, was exactly what she was looking at.

The woman by the window had turned back to the window.

---

"Now," he said, and he unfolded his hands. "You've put your papers on my table. Let me put mine."

He didn't put anything down.

"There are seats in the building below us. You've sat in one. Forty of them belong to your employer, under a contract you've read, with an annex you've reconciled. The people on that annex are not being rewarded. They're being *kept*. Because somebody will need to do the payroll on the Wednesday, Mr. Klippe. That's all continuity ever is."

"And the offer."

"Five seats." He said it flatly, like a price. "You. Your wife. Her mother, if you like; I'm told they're close. Two more, your choice. A friend, a colleague, the man who sells curtains, I don't care. Five names on Annex C, dependents per allocation, in a building you've already got a desk in."

I waited.

"No dogs," he said. "I'm sorry. You've read the clause. You initialed it. I'm told it's a question of air."

---

I'd like to tell you I thought about it. I didn't, and that's not courage. I'd already thought about it, for three weeks, on a kitchen floor, with the dog in question asleep across my feet, and I'd found there was a sentence underneath all the other sentences, and the sentence was Ana's, at four in the morning, and it was *run where, I've got a Doberman.*

"No," I said.

He nodded, as if I'd confirmed a delivery time.

"May I ask why? Not to persuade you. I'm curious. Most people, at this table, take a day."

I looked at the tray. The ledger. My two books with their pencil ticks. Nine things I'd made. A thin book was not among them, and I'd looked for it, on every shelf behind him, since I sat down.

"There's a clause on the annex I read and initialed anyway," I said. "I'd like that back. That's the whole reason."

I stood up. I picked up my two books and the notebook and the flask. I left the rest. They were copies. I'd kept the originals the way I keep everything, which is twice.

"Mr. Klippe," he said, as I got to the door. "You'll be back. Not for the seat. You've got the face of a man who's asked for an item."

"It's in conservation," I said. "I've got the slip."

---

Aylin didn't look up as I passed her desk. The visitor book was open. I didn't sign out. Nobody had asked me to.

The stairs down go past a door on the landing, gray, with a reader beside it, the same reader as every door in the lower floor, and my lanyard was in my coat pocket because I'd come straight from a drill brief and I'm untidy. I know what my badge opens. I'd sat at desk forty behind that door with a sandwich.

I stood on the landing for about as long as it takes to decide not to, and then I went on down the stairs and out through the front, like a reader, into a car park where it had started, very lightly, to snow.

Buddy was in the car. He'd got up. He was standing on the back seat with his nose against the window, watching the door I'd come out of, and when he saw me his tail went, once, and he lay down again, with the grunt.

I sat in the driver's seat for a while before I started the engine. I'd asked, and been given a slip. I'd shown them what I'd made, and been read my own form. I'd said no to five seats, out loud, to their faces, in front of the woman who signed their invoice.

In my work you never tell a system what you're watching for, because it'll give you exactly that and nothing else, forever. I'd just told one. I'd told it the dog.

I drove home in the snow, with the slip in my pocket and her initials on it.
