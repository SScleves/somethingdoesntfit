# Chapter Five

I spent the next two weeks trying to prove myself wrong.

I want that understood, because of where this ends up. I did not run toward the strange explanation. I ran at it with everything I had, trying to kill it, the way you're supposed to. A good finding is one that survives your own best attempt to make it go away. That's not conspiracy thinking. That's the opposite. That's the discipline the conspiracy people skip, which is why they end up where they end up.

So I built the case against myself first.

**Theory one: I'm wrong about the plates.** Maybe the two copies aren't the same impression. Maybe I misread the printing statement, or the statement lies, or 1958 print shops did something I don't know about. I spent a full weekend on it — bibliography forums, a scanned printer's manual, an afternoon composing a very casual email to an antiquarian dealer three countries away, the kind of email where you pretend to be less interested than you are. He wrote back kind and certain. Shared typos, shared ink flaws, identical gatherings: same impression, no question, congratulations on the find, what's the variant? I did not tell him what the variant was. I told him I was still checking. I have thought about that reflex since — that I lied to a stranger, immediately, on instinct, to protect a sentence about flood myths. You don't hide something boring.

**Theory two: I'm wrong about the impossibility.** Maybe pages do get quietly swapped and I just never learned how. So I learned. Cancel leaves, tipped-in corrections, stop-press changes — I read until I could have taught it. And every method leaves a mark. A stub in the gutter. A leaf a hair's width proud of its neighbors. A shadow where the glue went. I took the professor's copy back to the window in daylight, with a loupe, and went down the gutter of that signature millimeter by millimeter looking for the scar that would let me sleep.

The page was clean. Born with the book. I keep saying that because it keeps being the problem.

**Theory three — the good one, the one I wanted — is that I'm wrong about me.** Misremembering. Confabulation. I read the honest version everywhere for years, and my brain, primed by grief and too many tabs, hallucinated a sinister twin. Memory is a liar with a good face; I said that myself, one chapter and one lifetime ago. This was the theory I *wanted*, because it cost the least. If the broken thing is me, the world stays intact.

There was only one problem with theory three.

The book was still on the table.

I could put my thumb on the word. *Transmitted.* It didn't care what I remembered. It didn't flicker when I doubted it. That's the whole reason I trust objects over minds — an object doesn't have a version of events. I'd built my entire quiet life on that principle, and now the principle was sitting on my kitchen table being extremely unhelpful, holding perfectly still, telling the truth in two directions at once.

---

Here's a thing I haven't told you, because I've spent thirty years not telling anyone, and it picked that fortnight to come back.

When I was a kid — nine, ten — I read something in a book. I don't even remember the book, which is its own small cruelty; I remember the *fact*, the shape of it, the certainty of having read it. And later, when it mattered, when I repeated it, the world informed me I was wrong. The book didn't say that. The book had never said that. Check again. And I checked, and the sentence I remembered wasn't there, and every adult in the vicinity arranged their face into the expression you use for a child who has confidently invented something.

You misremembered.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this into more than it was. It wasn't trauma. Nobody hit me. It was smaller and it lasted longer than trauma: it was the first time I understood that my own certainty was not admissible evidence. That I could be sure — completely, cleanly sure — and be the only one, and being the only one is the same as being wrong, socially, functionally, in every way that pays. So I learned to distrust myself first, before anyone else could do it for me. I built a whole personality on getting to *you misremembered* before the room did. The Record is that personality with a filing system. I don't trust my memory. I photograph everything. I keep the receipts because once, when I was ten, I couldn't produce one.

You see why the two books did what they did to me.

For thirty years the lesson had been: when you and the record disagree, *you* are the error. And here, finally, was a record that disagreed with *itself*, in daylight, under a loupe, with no scar — and for the first time in my life the math came out the other way. Maybe I wasn't the error. Maybe I never had been. Maybe, once, a long time ago, I read exactly what I thought I read, and the book got changed, and a ten-year-old took the blame for a thing a ten-year-old could not have imagined.

I'm not saying that's what happened when I was a kid. I have no copy of that book; that ship sailed decades ago, and I know better than to build on a memory I can't photograph. I'm saying that the possibility, which had been sealed for thirty years, came unsealed on my kitchen floor — and once a possibility like that is open, you cannot get it shut again. I'd know. I've tried.

---

Ana started noticing around week two. Of course she did.

It wasn't the reading; the reading was old news, *your X-Files*, tinfoil sizes at Christmas. It was that I'd gone quiet in the specific way I go quiet when something has its hooks in me — present at dinner, gone underneath. She's lived with the tell for years.

"You're doing the thing," she said. We were doing the dishes. I washed, she dried; that's the treaty.

"What thing."

"The submarine thing. You're right here and you're eight hundred meters down."

"I'm here."

"Your hands are here." She took a plate from me. "Is it the professor's books?"

And this was the moment. I felt it be the moment. The true answer was long and sounded insane and started with *I think one of them was altered by someone who understood it better than the man who wrote it,* and I could see, in advance, the exact micro-expression it would put on the face of the woman I love — not disbelief, she'd never; something worse, a flicker of *here we go*, a recalibration of how much weight to put on me. I'd spent my whole life managing for that flicker. I wasn't going to volunteer for it in my own kitchen.

"There's a printing thing that doesn't add up," I said. "It's nerdy. It's boring. It's a me problem."

All true. Every word. And a lie built entirely out of true words, which is the only kind I know how to tell her.

She looked at me for a second longer than the sentence needed. Ana's not fooled by true words; she invented half of them.

"Okay," she said. And then, drying the last plate, not looking at me: "Just — don't disappear into it. Abuela's gone six weeks. I'd like my husband on land for a while."

That landed somewhere the books hadn't reached. Because she was right, and because she'd handed me the honest frame for the whole thing and I hadn't taken it: I'd been treating this as a puzzle. She was treating it as a widow watching her husband pick a fight with a bookshelf while she grieved.

"I'm on land," I said.

Third lie. I meant to make it true.

I opened the file that night and put one line in the notes, where nobody will ever read it:

*Told A. I am on land. Present tense. Review.*

---

That night I went and sat with the shrink question, which is a thing I can do because I have, at last count, an entire subcommittee of former shrinks living in my head, and I know their moves.

I've been seeing them my whole life. That's a longer story, and it goes somewhere specific, and I'll take you there when we're both ready. For now the only part that matters is the outcome, which is embarrassing: they keep graduating me. Every few years one of them closes the notebook and delivers a version of the same speech — nothing here to treat, you're not unwell, you just think too much and you'd like company for it, and I can't in good conscience keep charging you for that. I have been, no exaggeration, *fired for being fine* more times than most people get hired.

So I ran the finding past the committee, the way I do. I asked the question they'd ask.

*Is there any version of this where you are simply a grieving man who found a misprint and needs it to be something bigger, because something bigger is easier to hunt than a dead grandmother-in-law you weren't even allowed to properly mourn?*

I sat with that one honestly. I'm not too proud for it. It's a good question; it's *my* question, aimed back at me.

And the answer, annoyingly, was: partly. Yes. Some of the heat on this was grief looking for a job. I'll cop to that in full.

But partly isn't wholly, and the committee has never once let me file a real anomaly under a convenient feeling. Grief could explain why I *cared* about the word.

It could not put the word on the page.

The word was on the page.

I closed the committee, and the notebook, and went to bed next to my wife, and lay there in the dark being on land, and did not sleep, because somewhere under the floor of me a thirty-year-old sentence had come unsealed, and it turns out that once you're allowed to wonder whether you were ever really the error — you start wondering about everything. Every correction you ever swallowed. Every time the room was sure and you went along.

You start wanting to check.

Not the book.

Everything.

That's where it stopped being about a book.
