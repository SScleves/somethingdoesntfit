# Chapter Twenty-Seven

The coordinates had been sitting at the bottom of that list for three years and I had been treating them like the last question on an exam.

Six decimal places. Which is precision to about a hundred millimetres, and which is either a man being exact or a man copying down whatever a screen gave him, and it took me a stupidly long time to accept that it was the second one, because the second one is what everybody does.

They're forty-one kilometres east of Şanlıurfa. Which the older maps call Urfa. Which is where a reader in comparative mythology walked out of a town on foot in 1929, heading east, and was never seen again.

I have looked at the satellite view of that spot more times than I have looked at photographs of my own wedding.

There is a building on it.

---

It is not a ruin, it is not a cave mouth, it is not a hole in a hillside with a symbol over it. It is a low, wide, single-storey building of about four thousand square metres with a pale roof and a service yard, at the end of a two-kilometre asphalt spur off a provincial road, with a line of young trees along the eastern side that somebody has been watering for a decade, and a car park with marked bays for about thirty vehicles.

There is a sign at the turning. You can read it on the street imagery. It is in Turkish and English and it gives the name of the facility and the name of a foundation, and the English says **Regional Finds Depot and Conservation Archive.**

It has a website with four pages, in the way that institutions have websites, and one of the pages is a PDF of a collections policy last revised in 2019, and one of them is a phone number and an address and the opening hours of an office.

I sat and looked at that for about a week and I want to describe the specific feeling, because it was not triumph.

It was **embarrassment**.

Three years. A list, a chart, a spreadsheet of clustered years, a style guide in its fourth revision, a man in a station café, a man on a golf course. And at the end of it, the place is *on the internet*, with opening hours, and a photograph of the ribbon being cut in 2013 by three people in coats, one of whom is a provincial governor and two of whom I have never identified.

---

Now the part that took another fortnight and that made the whole thing sit down properly.

Look at the site on the older imagery.

The building goes up in 2011 and 2012 and it is finished in 2013. Before that, the same footprint is empty, and it is empty in every image back to 2003, which is as far as it goes.

But the *access road* is older than the building.

You can see it. It's not a construction track — a construction track is a scar that gets abandoned or absorbed. This is a made road, asphalt, with proper drainage on the downhill side, running two kilometres off a provincial highway to a piece of hillside with nothing on it, and it is there in 2004, and it is there in the earliest image I could get anybody to sell me, which is 1998.

A road to nothing, maintained for fifteen years, and then a depot built at the end of it.

And then the survey documents, which are public in Turkey the same as anywhere, because a building of that size needs geotechnical work and the geotechnical work is filed.

The geotechnical report describes the substrate as *soft limestone with existing subsurface voids*, and — this is the sentence, this is the whole chapter, and it is in an annex about foundation loading — it notes that the design accommodates *existing subsurface access*, and it gives a depth.

Twenty-two metres.

They did not build a vault. Nobody digs a vault in that country and nobody would need to, because that whole region is made of the stuff, and human beings have been cutting rooms and stairs and stables and churches and entire multi-level towns out of that soft rock for a very long time, and everybody knows this, and it's on the postcards.

They built **a lid**.

A perfectly legitimate, publicly funded, professionally staffed conservation depot with a good climate-control specification and a car park, positioned over a hole that was already there.

And I understood, sitting at my kitchen table late at night, why this had taken a hundred years and forty entries and three dead men to find.

It isn't hidden. It's the most visible building for ten kilometres. It has a *sign*.

The concealment is not the location. The concealment is that there is a perfectly good reason for it to be there — and there is no version of looking at it that produces a question, because a conservation depot in a region absolutely stuffed with archaeology is the least remarkable object in the landscape.

The best hiding place has always been a legitimate institution with a reason to exist.

---

Before I go on I have to do a piece of accounting, because I'd been putting it off since roughly the previous winter, and it's four items long.

For three years I had asked myself, on and off, in the small hours, why nothing had happened to me. Tresidder walked into the hills. A neurophysiologist had a paper withdrawn at his own request when he was not able to request anything. Fourteen people were *retired* in a single year by a man who used the word without noticing.

And I sat here, in a house, with a job and a dog.

The four reasons, as I had them written down:

**One: I was cheap to mock.** A man with a hobby and a folder is not a problem, he's a category, and the category has a costume and everyone knows the costume. The mask was my armour.
*Expired in April, when they added three paragraphs to twelve years of my posts. You do not spend that kind of effort on a man who is already harmless. They took the mask off the shelf and put it on me deliberately, which means the free version had stopped working.*

**Two: I was Quiet.** The list is a map of who noticed and what it cost them, and the people it cost nothing were the ones who noticed and *stopped.* Readers aren't threats. Publishers are.
*Expired in December, on a tram platform, when I decided to stop collecting evidence and start generating responses. That's the exact moment. I've dated it.*

**Three: I was an accident.** The single most protective fact of my life is that a man with a beard couldn't be bothered to open a crate. There is no version of their model that predicted me, because I am not in any of the places a person like me is supposed to come from.
*Expired the week I wrote to Els Maasen. From then on I was in their coverage, because I'd walked into it myself, on paper, with my name on it.*

**Four: I was bookish.** Vanishing is for the physical layer. Tresidder walked toward a *place*. The neurophysiologist had a *mechanism*. I had photocopies.
*Expired the day I opened a transfer schedule in an annex in six-point type and wrote down a set of coordinates.*

All four. In order. Over about eighteen months, each one for a reason I can name, and every one of them expired because of something I chose to do.

I wrote that list out on a Tuesday night and looked at it and thought, quite calmly: *right. So that's finished, then.*

---

Which brings me to the other thing I did that autumn, and it started as housekeeping and it ended up being the worst forty minutes of the entire business.

I wanted to know who Dave was. Not for the plot. For Ana — because she'd been sat on a step with him telling him about a blanket, and because there was a version of the next six months in which I was in another country and she was not, and I wanted a name to give somebody if it came to that.

I had a name. Noor had given me one: **Julian Kalder**, consultant, in her department for nine days in September 2017, overseeing the transfer.

It's an easy name to run. That's not detective work; conservation is a small accredited world and it keeps public registers, because the entire point of accreditation is that you can check it.

Here is what I found in about ninety minutes, and every single item is public, and you could do it tonight.

**Julian Kalder, professional accreditation number,** issued 1994, category: conservation of library and archive materials. Status: **active.** Renewed annually. Fees current.

**Three published papers.** 1996, 1999, 2003. Two on iron-gall ink corrosion, one on adhesive selection in leaf attachment. Dry, careful, competent, and — Noor told me later, when I broke my promise and wrote to her once, and I'm not sorry — genuinely good. She'd read the 1999 one as a student.

**A membership record** in a professional body going back to 1989.

**A photograph**, on an archived conference page from 2002, of a panel of four people in a room with bad carpet.

I looked at that photograph for a long time.

He is second from the left. He is in his forties, balding, in a jumper, holding a paper cup, and he has the specific unphotogenic ordinariness of somebody who has been told to stand still at a conference.

It is not the man from my garden. It is not the man from the seventh tee. It is not the same person and it is not close, and no amount of fourteen years does that to a face.

---

And then, because I check everything twice, and because that is now the only thing I have left that is entirely mine, I ran the name once more with the word *obituary* on the end of it.

There's a newsletter. Professional bodies have newsletters; they come out quarterly, they contain committee minutes and conference notices, and at the back there is a page called something like *Members Remembered.*

**Spring 2011.**

Four sentences. Julian Kalder, born 1961, died in January, suddenly, at home. A short, warm paragraph about his work on ink corrosion. A line saying that he is survived by a sister. A note that donations may be made to a hospice.

He has been dead for fourteen years.

His accreditation has been renewed every year since, on time, with the fee paid.

---

I sat in my kitchen with a dog asleep on a duvet and understood that I had gone backwards.

Because for nine weeks I had been carrying around a small hard fact that I thought was mine: *the man is called Julian Kalder.* I'd written it down. I'd told Ana. It was the one thing I had.

And it turns out that Julian Kalder was a real man who spent his life being careful with paper, who wrote a paper in 1999 that a woman in Leiden still remembers, who died at home in the winter of 2011 at the age of forty-nine, and who has a sister.

And somebody has been walking around inside his professional life ever since — going to a Dutch university in 2017 and being kind to a crying woman in a corridor, and signing a transfer schedule for forty crates, with a dead conservator's accreditation number at the bottom of it, active, in good standing, fees current.

I don't know who the man in my garden is.

That's the actual result of ninety minutes of good work. I know less than I did on Tuesday. On Tuesday I had a name and a photograph of a stranger and a story about a corridor in 2017. Now I have a dead man's paperwork and a face I have played a dozen rounds of golf with.

I went and checked the club, of course. Because that's what I do.

There is no Dave. There is no membership, because he was never a member — he came as a visitor, fourteen times over three months, and every time he paid the green fee in cash at the counter, and the woman who has given my dog a sausage for nine years remembers him perfectly and liked him very much and wrote his first name in a book because that is all anybody writes.

That is the whole of it. Every single thing I know about that man is something he handed me: a first name, a job description that was three words long and boring on purpose, a nickname he used for me, and an opinion about my marriage that turned out to be correct.

The rest — the accreditation, the papers on ink, the sister, the hospice — belongs to a careful man who died in January 2011 and who never knew what his name was going to be used for.

---

I told Ana that night. All of it, in about four minutes, because we do that now.

She listened, and then she was quiet for a while, and then she said:

"Are you going to tell his sister?"

And that had not occurred to me. It had genuinely not occurred to me, in ninety minutes of very good work, that there was a woman somewhere who has a dead brother whose name is out walking around.

"I don't know what I'd say."

"No," Ana said. "Neither do I."

We left it. I think about it about once a month. I have never done anything about it and I don't expect to now, and it is one of the two or three things from this whole business that I would take back if I could take anything back.
