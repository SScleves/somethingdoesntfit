# Chapter Thirteen

Six days. I know it was six because I counted them, which is not something I'd normally admit.

Sunday came up grey and still and I got there before he'd finished unloading, which I had never done in a decade, and he clocked that immediately.

"You're early."

"I'm never early."

"No," Beard Man agreed. "You're not."

He carried on unloading. I stood there in the cold like a man waiting outside a headmaster's office and let him do four boxes, because I had worked out on the walk over that if I came in hot he would go sideways, and if I waited he might not.

Then he said, "Ask it, then," and I found that my mouth was dry.

---

"The professor," I said. "The one whose books you sold me."

"Mm."

"What was his name?"

He straightened up and looked at me over the tables for a moment.

"You bought a dozen books off me," he said. "Twelve. Fourteen months ago. And you've come back every Sunday since and never once asked me his name."

"I know."

"Why now?"

And here is where I had to make a decision, standing in a wet market at nine in the morning, and I didn't make it well. I made it fast, which is different.

"Because there's a notebook in with them," I said, "in his handwriting, and I've spent a year on it, and I need to know who he was."

Beard Man put down the box he was holding. He did it slowly and he put it down flat, and he looked at me with an expression I'd never seen on him — I'd seen amusement, I'd seen the thousand-yard sentence, I'd seen him actively bored by his own anecdote. I had never seen him careful.

"A notebook," he said.

"A list."

"Right." He looked past me, down the row of stalls, in a way I did not like at all. "Right."

---

"His name was Hendrik Maasen," he said, eventually. "Doctor. Not medical. He taught at the university until whenever they stop letting you, and then he didn't."

"What did he teach?"

"Ancient history, near enough. I asked him once and he gave me an answer that took ten minutes and I couldn't have repeated a word of it." A small dry noise that in another man would have been a laugh. "I know what that's like from the other side, before you say anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to."

He got the crate out from under the table and turned it over and sat on it, which by then I understood was the signal that we were doing this properly.

"He came here for twenty-two years," he said. "Same as you. Bit less regular at the end. He bought what you'd expect and a lot you wouldn't. And he asked questions like yours, which is why I've always got on with you, if you want to know."

"How did he die?"

"In his chair," Beard Man said. "Which is what everybody wants and nobody says out loud. Sixty-nine. His heart. It was quick and it was at home and there was nothing in it."

"Who told you that?"

"His daughter told me that. She's the one who cleared the house, and she's the one who sold me the books, and she's the one who cried in this exact spot for about four minutes while I stood here like a fence post."

---

"The family kept the furniture," I said.

"They did."

"You said that at the time. It's stuck with me for a year."

"It sticks with everyone."

"Why did they keep the furniture?"

And Beard Man said, in a completely level voice, still sitting on the crate:

"Because the furniture didn't take anything from them."

I didn't say anything.

"His father was the same," he went on. "That's what she told me. Whatever this is, it took the grandfather and then it took her dad, in her words, not mine — took him in the sense that he was in the house and he wasn't *there*, for years, and then he'd come back for a while and be lovely, and then he'd go again. And she said something I've thought about since, which is that she wasn't angry at him. She was angry at the books, and she knew that was stupid, and she did it anyway. So she kept the table and the chairs and the dresser her mother liked, and she sold me two hundred kilos of the other thing for one hundred and forty euros, and I think she'd have paid me to take it."

"Three crates," I said. "You had three crates."

"I had five."

I looked up.

"You had five?"

"I had five, and you came on the second Sunday and picked over two of them like a heron, and you bought your twelve."

---

Here it is. This is the bit I came for and didn't know I was coming for.

"What happened to the other three?"

"A man bought them," Beard Man said. "The Sunday after you. All three, the lot, didn't look inside, didn't haggle. Paid cash and had a van."

"A dealer."

"No." He said it flatly. "I've sold to dealers for forty years. A dealer haggles because it's their money and they're going to sell it again. This one didn't open a crate."

"What did he look like?"

"Ordinary." He shrugged, and I could see him being annoyed at himself. "That's not helpful, is it. Fifties. Coat. Polite. Dutch, or good enough that I couldn't tell. He was pleasant to me, and I've had forty years of people not being pleasant to me, so I'd have remembered rude."

"And?"

"And before he paid he asked me a question." Beard Man looked at me. "He asked if there had been any notebooks."

The market went on around us. Somebody two stalls down was arguing about the price of a lamp.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him there'd been no notebooks," he said. "Because there hadn't been. Not that I'd seen. I'd never opened your twelve, had I. You bought them off the bottom of a crate and I charged you almost nothing and threw one in for free because twelve sounds better." He rubbed his beard. "First time in my life being sloppy has done anybody any good."

---

I'm going to stop the scene here for a second because I need to say what that did to me, and it did not do what you'd think.

It did not make me feel hunted. That came later.

What it did — standing in the cold with my hands in my pockets, in a market I'd been coming to for a decade — was make me feel *lucky*, and there is no worse feeling available to a man like me.

Because I run on the assumption that if you look properly, you find. That's the whole religion. Method beats luck; that's why I check the boring corner; that's why I photograph before I read.

And here was the truth: I had the only copy of that list on the planet because a man with a beard couldn't be bothered to open a crate, and because I got there on the second Sunday instead of the third.

Seven days either way and I'd have nothing. Seven days either way and there is no book, no shelf, no two years, and I'm a man who plays golf badly and has a nice wife and a folder called Reading.

Everything that follows happened because of a gap of one week in another man's laziness.

I've never got comfortable with that. I don't think I'm supposed to.

---

"There's a daughter," I said. "Would she talk to me?"

"Els," he said. "Els Maasen. She's in Deventer, or she was; there's a shop, she does something with fabric. That's all I've got and I'm not going to go looking, and if you tell her where you got her name I'll deny it, and then you and I are going to be strangers who sell each other books, which would be a shame, because you're the only one who lets me finish."

"I won't."

"You will. You'll do it kindly and you'll do it because you think you have to." He stood up off the crate with the sound men his age make. "She told me one more thing and then I'm done with this and we're not doing it again."

"Go on."

"She said the last six months he'd stopped sleeping." He picked up the box. "Not couldn't. Stopped. She'd ring at two in the morning if she couldn't sleep herself and he'd pick up on the first ring and be perfectly bright. And she asked him about it once and he said —"

He stopped, and got the words right, which is not something he usually bothers with.

"He said: *I'd rather be awake for it.*"

---

I bought two books I didn't want and walked home the long way.

And what I noticed, about halfway, was that I'd stopped thinking about Hendrik Maasen and started thinking about my own house.

Specifically: the shelf by the window. The list on it. Two hundred books on the floor. A laptop on the kitchen table with a ten-year-old password on a folder called Reading.

And the thought arrived complete, in my own voice, the way the worst ones do.

*If I died in my chair tonight, at sixty-nine or forty-four, Ana would grieve for a year and then she would need the room back. And she would be right to. And a man in a coat would come to a market with a van and cash and no interest in haggling, and he would ask a polite question about notebooks, and this time somebody would say yes.*

I let myself into the house. Mora did the resurrection thing. Buddy did the two thumps from the blanket by the radiator.

And I stood in my own hallway with my coat on and thought, for the first time in this entire story:

*I need to make copies.*
