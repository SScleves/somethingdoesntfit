# Chapter Thirty-One

I got home on the Friday and Ana had made the thing with the aubergines and Mora ate a tea towel, and I have never in my life been so pleased about a tea towel.

Buddy met me at the door.

Not at the bottom step. At the door, out of the kitchen, down the hall, slowly, paying for every step, with the tail going the whole way — and I put the bag down and sat on the floor of my own hallway in my coat and he put his forehead against my chest and stood there, and Mora came and lay across my legs because if there is affection happening she is contractually entitled to be in it, and Ana stood in the kitchen doorway with a wooden spoon and let it go on for a long time before she said anything.

What she said was: "So that's it, then."

And I said, "That's it."

Neither of us knew what that meant.

---

Here is what happened over the following year, and it is the least cinematic account of a resolution you will ever read.

**Nothing.**

Nobody came to the house. Nobody rang. There was no black car, no envelope, no meeting in a car park. I was not sacked; I was, in fact, promoted eight months later, into a job I quite like and am mildly bad at.

Somewhere between two and four hundred people did a division sum.

I know that because a dozen of them wrote to me, and I have every one of them, and they are the strangest correspondence any man has ever received. Six of them told me I was wrong. Two of those six were *right* that I was wrong — one had my air-handling design margin out by a factor that matters and the other pointed out, correctly and rather coldly, that a provisioning schedule can be phased and that I had treated twelve years as a single block when it may be a first tranche. I published both corrections under my own name, in the dullest possible language, in the same place I published the erratum from Ghent. It got about eleven views. It always does.

Three of the dozen wrote to tell me about their own jobs. I'm not going to say anything else about that.

And two of them — and this is the only part of this whole business that has ever made me cry — wrote to say thank you, and then asked me not to write back.

I don't know what any of them did. I will never know. That's the design; I built it that way on purpose so that I would never be able to know, because a man who can list the people he has compromised is a man who can be made to.

---

Now the cost, and this is the actual chapter.

I can never say any of it out loud. Not once. Not for the rest of my life.

I need to explain that properly, because when I've tried to describe it to Ana it comes out sounding like fear and it isn't fear.

There is a thing I built. I have mentioned it once: four in the morning, a dressing gown, forty minutes, the most ridiculous act of my adult life. I said at the time that I was not going to describe it, and I have kept that for four years.

I'll tell you the shape of it now, because you need the shape and not the mechanism, and because the mechanism isn't interesting — it is, genuinely, the most ordinary thing in my profession, turned around and pointed at me.

**It does nothing at all as long as I keep checking in.**

That's it. That's the entire architecture. There is a small, boring, unglamorous thing I do, and it takes about eleven seconds, and I have done it every single day for four years — in a bathroom with the tap running, in a hotel in Şanlıurfa, on the morning of my father-in-law's funeral, on the first tee at seven in the morning with a dog in the cart, which is where the four retirees have decided I say a little prayer, and I have never corrected them.

And if I ever stop — if I stop for long enough that it can't be a bad signal, a lost phone, a hospital, a holiday — then it goes.

That's what makes it worth anything. Not the payload. **The arming.** Everything I have is worth exactly nothing on the day I publish it and everything on the day I don't, and the only way to keep it worth something is to be a man who has never said any of this to anybody.

So: I hold the door.

I don't get to walk through it. That's the whole cost and it is not dramatic and nobody will ever know it happened. I am a man of forty-five with a mortgage and a wife and two dogs and a job I am mildly bad at, who has three years of the strangest work anybody has ever done in a folder, and who is going to go to barbecues for the next thirty years and be charming for three minutes at a time and never once mention it.

Which — and I have thought about this a great deal — is precisely what I have been complaining about for this entire book. I have spent three years furious about a machinery that takes the hand out of the sentence.

And here I am, taking my own hand out, on purpose, because that is what makes the sentence work.

I don't have an answer to that. I've stopped looking for one.

---

There is one more thing and then I'll leave the plot alone.

The payload is not a claim. I understood that on a tram platform four years ago and it's the only strategic idea I've ever had: **a claim is what the mask defeats.** Add two per cent to a claim and it's finished, and they can add two per cent to anything, and they're extremely good at it, and one of them sat in my garden with tongs.

So there's no claim in it.

There are **diffs**. Forty of them, minus the one I struck out in Ghent, which is thirty-nine, plus the two that my correspondents have since made me qualify, so call it thirty-seven that I would put in front of anybody. Photographs of both pages. The plate blemish at the top of 118. The transfer schedule from annex C. A dozen public documents and four divisions.

And the arithmetic. Three hundred and forty, and about nine hundred, and the instruction that goes with it, which is six words long and which I have never improved:

**Check whether you're on it.**

That's not a claim either. It's a task. You cannot add two per cent to a task; either the reader does the division or they don't, and if they do it they own the answer, and nobody can take a thing off a person that they worked out themselves at their own kitchen table.

That's the entire lesson of three years, and I got it from a dead man who copied out forty lines in his best handwriting and never once wrote down what he thought it meant.

---

And this.

This, that you are holding. All of it — the funeral, the eighty-five per cent, the tabs, the folder called Reading, the ruler on the table at midnight, Beard Man on his crate in the rain, a kitchen in Deventer, a fabric shop in Leiden, a barbecue, a terrace, a hospital corridor in 1997, an old dog on a duvet on a kitchen floor.

This is in there too.

It always was. I started writing it in the second year, badly, as notes, and then properly, and I have kept it up ever since, and it goes in with the diffs and the arithmetic, and it sits there doing absolutely nothing.

I have known for a long time what that means about how you came to be reading it.

I'm not going to make anything of that. I'd rather not; I'd rather you didn't either. It could be a great many things. It could be a bus, or a heart, like Hendrik's, at sixty-nine, in a blue chair with the bin not put out. It could be that I got old and forgot, which frankly is the one I'd pick.

Or it could be that at some point a man decided that eleven seconds a day for the rest of his life was not, in the end, the thing to do with a life, and stopped on purpose, and went and had an afternoon.

I genuinely don't know which. I'm writing this on a Tuesday in October and there's a dog asleep under the table and my wife is upstairs and I have no plans.

---

So that's the last thing I'll ask of you, and it's small, and it's the whole book.

**Don't believe me.**

Not one word of it. Not the flashes, not the kitchen floor, not the man who finished my sentence in a garden, none of it — you weren't there and neither of us can check it and I have spent three years explaining why that matters.

Go and do the division.

It is four sums and a dozen documents and it will cost you an evening, and if you're right and I'm wrong you should say so loudly and I'd deserve it.

And if you find you haven't got an evening — if it turns out that this week the boiler's gone and the money's short and the thing with your mother is happening and there is simply, genuinely, no hour in it anywhere —

Then I'd ask you to sit with that for a minute, because that's the finding.

That was always the finding.
