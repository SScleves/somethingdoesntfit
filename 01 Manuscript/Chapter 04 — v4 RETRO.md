# Chapter Four

The professor's books earned their shelf space in the first week.

I read the mythology in the evenings — the serious stuff, the footnotes-in-three-languages stuff — and it was better company than I expected. The man had annotated like he was arguing with the authors: pencil lines in the margins, the occasional *no* — just *no* — next to a paragraph that had displeased him. I caught myself looking forward to the marks more than the text. Reading a used book is eavesdropping on the previous reader. Reading his felt more like being led.

The thin one with no title I didn't open. I can't fully explain that. I took it down twice, turned it over, put it back. Some books you save for dessert. Some you circle. I told myself I was circling.

And on the ninth day — a Tuesday, because the big things in my life apparently subscribe to Tuesdays — I found the sentence.

---

I wasn't cataloguing. I want that clear, because it matters. I was reading — the professor's flood survey, a 1926 thing called *Waters Before Memory*, dry as toast, the kind of book that says "hitherto" without embarrassment — and about forty pages in, something started to itch.

Nothing I could point at. Just a low wrongness, the sense you get walking into your own kitchen and knowing, before you can say how, that someone's been in it. I knew this book. I owned it — bought years ago in another market in another city, read once, logged, shelved. I know my own shelves. So the wrongness wasn't *what is this book;* it was smaller and far more annoying than that. It was *this isn't how it goes.*

Which is exactly the kind of thing I don't get to say. My memory doesn't get a vote. I learned that at ten and built a life on it: when I and the record disagree, I'm the error. So I did what I always do with my own certainty — I moved to kill it. Misremembering. Grief brain. Too many tabs. I kept reading, faster now, half looking for the thing that would prove me the fool and let me sleep.

I'd been skimming by then — ticking sentences off by their shape, *yes, yes, there it is, knew it,* the way you reassure yourself down a checklist when you're tired and you'd like to be wrong. I read the paragraph about where the flood stories came from the way I'd read the forty pages before it: first few words, last few words, length about right, done. Three lines later something in the back of me curdled and said: *go back. You read that too fast. You read it the way you wanted it to say.*

So I went back and did the thing I hate, which is reading like a child. One word at a time. Out loud, in an empty kitchen, at midnight, like a man being punished.

> *In approaching these traditions, the student must hold one principle above all others, resisting always the romance that such material invites: that the correspondences among them, however striking, appear to have been transmitted to the early settled peoples of the great river valleys, and must for that reason be studied with the discipline the subject demands.*

Fine. Dry. Correct-sounding. The kind of sentence you nod through on your way to the actual content.

Except I heard myself say, to nobody: "That's not what it says."

Because it wasn't. Not in my copy. I was suddenly, physically certain of it — and physical certainty is the one thing I'm least allowed to have. There was only one way to settle it, and for once the way was twelve feet away: second shelf, left side.

I got up, fully expecting to lose the way I always lose, and went to get the other book.

---

Here's the boring part. Sit through it. The boring part is the whole case.

On the way back to the kitchen I opened my copy to the page and read the passage, fast, standing up — and nothing. Same sentence. Of course it was. I very nearly put the book back on the shelf right there, and I want you to know how close it was, because the whole rest of my life turned on not doing it.

Instead I sat down with two copies of a book nobody has cared about since 1926, side by side on a kitchen table, a man in his socks about to prove himself the fool.

Same publisher. Same year on the title page. Same green cloth, same faded spine. The copyright pages differed by one line, and it was the line I'd have bet on: his said *first edition, first impression*; mine said *first edition, second impression*. So: two printings. Fine. That's not a mystery, that's my hobby. A publisher reopens the plates between runs, fixes what needs fixing, and — if he's the quiet type — says nothing. Retractions nobody admitted to. This should have been Christmas.

I checked the rest of that page for the usual signs of a tidy-up, the little corrections that tell you someone was in there with a red pencil. Same two typos. Both copies. Untouched.

I ran the books in parallel, page against page. Same line breaks, same hyphenations. Same ink flaw in the top margin of 118 — a blemish in the plate itself, which means the plate was never cleaned. Identical for a hundred pages, two hundred pages, the sameness of a print run nobody had opened up.

---

Ana came down for water around midnight and found me with both books open, a ruler on the table, and my nose about ten centimeters off the paper.

"Did you buy the same book twice?"

"Hm. I hadn't thought of it that way."

"Why do you have two?"

"The professor had good taste."

"That's not an answer."

"He and I agree on exactly one book. I find that touching."

She looked at the table. The ruler. Me. I watched her do the math.

"You've got the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely have a face. The one where you found something weird and you're deciding whether to tell me."

"I haven't found anything weird."

And here's the thing — that was *true*. Two hundred identical pages is the opposite of weird; it's the most boring result my hobby can produce. I hadn't found anything. I was lying about *nothing*, to my wife, at midnight, with a ruler on the table, and some part of me had already decided the nothing was mine.

She filled her glass, kissed the top of my head, and went up.

"Don't measure all night, Scully."

I said something charming. I don't remember what. The persona handles the small stuff.

---

So after the rest of the book, there I was again, page 118 — the blemish page — and this time I did it properly. Both books flat. One finger on each line. The way I should have read it the first time.

First words — identical. *In approaching these traditions, the student must hold one principle above all others, resisting always the romance that such material invites: that the correspondences among them, however striking, appear to have* — twenty-eight words, letter for letter, both copies. The comfortable blur that had carried me past it in the first place.

Last words — identical. *settled peoples of the great river valleys, and must for that reason be studied with the discipline the subject demands.* Same tail. Same full stop.

Same length. Same line count. The justification barely disturbed.

The middle was different.

His — first impression: *…appear to have been transmitted to the early settled peoples…*

Mine — second impression: *…appear to have arisen among the first settled peoples…*

That's it. That's the whole thing. A handful of words, sitting dead center in a sentence I'd swear now was designed to be skimmed. Wrapped on both sides in twenty identical words each, exactly where a tired man checking the beginning and the end would never, ever look. I'd passed that sentence three times the lazy way and it had passed me back three times, politely, like a doorman who knows you don't belong but isn't paid enough to say so.

And I'll be honest about what I felt, because it wasn't what you'd expect. It was disappointment. When I catch a change, normally it's a *concept* — a retraction, a claim walked back, something with weight. This was a grammar quibble. *Transmitted to, arisen among.* I'd chased a feeling through two hundred pages and it had led me to the folklore equivalent of a comma. I'd found something, and it was nothing.

So I did what I do when a thing is nothing and I'm too wound up to sleep: I explained it to the dog.

"Here's what's supposed to happen, Buddy." He opened one eye. "Publisher reopens the plates. Second impression. That's press time, that's money, so you fix everything while you're in there. The typos — everyone fixes the typos, that's the whole *reason* you're in there. The blemish on 118, obviously, you're already paying. And then you add the line. *Second impression, corrected.* Because the trade expects it, and because you're proud of the work. That's the job. That's what a second impression *is*."

Both eyes now.

"They fixed the typos, right?" I checked. "No. The blemish?" I checked, though I already knew. "No. The line?" No.

I sat there with my finger on the top margin of 118, on the blemish — still sitting there, on the very page they'd changed. Which meant they hadn't even replaced the plate. They'd reached into it. One line, in a plate they otherwise left alone.

"Why," I said to Buddy, "would you go to all that trouble and not fix *anything*?"

He didn't have a theory. Neither did I. And that was the strange part, if you know me: normally when I find something wrong there's an explanation standing right next to it, wearing the lab coat, ready to go. Here there wasn't one. There was nobody to wear it. So I tried it on myself — *the author changed his mind; the printer was lazy; it happens* — and it didn't fit me either. It made no sense in my mouth. Which meant there was still something to hunt.

Somebody had opened the plates of a finished book, changed a handful of words in one sentence on one page, and closed them again — leaving every known error in place so that the second impression would look, to anyone who checked the way people check, exactly like the first. And then printed it under the same words on the title page. *First edition.* Every copy sold after that day. Every library. Every scan. Every quotation in every paper written since. All of it wearing the first's clothes.

The original said one thing. The world has been reading the other.

Somewhere in my chest, a very old feeling lifted its head. It didn't have a name yet. It knew me, though. It had been waiting since I was a kid.

---

Then I did the obvious thing, which I should have done an hour earlier: I looked up the author.

H. M. Tresidder. Not a name that survives. A page and a half of the internet, most of it library catalogues. One book — this one. A reader in comparative mythology at a university that no longer has the department, the kind of Englishman the 1920s produced by the crate: a little archaeology, a little philology, a great deal of walking around other people's countries with a notebook. A few students, presumably. No photograph.

And a date. Disappeared 1929. Last seen leaving Urfa, in southeastern Turkey, on foot, heading east. Never found. Presumed dead in the hills.

I read that twice, then turned back to my copy's copyright page. *Second impression, 1931.*

Two years after he walked into the hills and didn't walk out, somebody reopened his plates and corrected his book.

---

Now — the part that kept me on the kitchen floor until three.

Read the two versions again as a stranger would. Go on. They're *nothing*. "Transmitted to the early," "arisen among the first" — two scholars quibbling about folklore, a war fought in journals no one alive has opened. Show these pages to a friend, a dealer, a professor, and you'd get a shrug. Variant readings. The author had second thoughts. Who cares.

I told myself that. Then I gave up, which is not like me, and went and lay down on the floor next to the dogs. Not to think. To stop.

Buddy was on his blanket. Mora was on the couch above me, upside down. I lay on my back on the kitchen floor with the two books on my chest like a man laid out for viewing, and the words went round in my mouth on their own, the way a song does when you're too tired to stop it.

Arise. Transmit. First. Early. Arise. Transmit.

I wasn't paying attention. I want to be accurate about that, because of what happened next: I did a little hand thing on *arise* — palm up, fingers lifting, nothing, a tic — and Buddy got up.

Slowly. Front legs, then the back, with the little grunt. And stood there, looking down at me on the floor, like: *you called?*

I laughed. It surprised me; I hadn't in a while. "Down," I said, and he lay down, offended.

"*Arise,*" I said again — and this time I gave it the voice, the one from the movies, the God voice, and the hand — and up he came, twelve years old and creaking, because a request from the floor is a request he honors.

That was too much for Mora. She came off the couch like she'd been fired from it, vanished, and came back with the toy.

The toy. There is one toy. It was a rope once; it is now a damp grey ecosystem that smells like the Paris Métro in July, and she loves it more than she loves either of us. Outside she's too tired to obey a single word. Inside, at four in the morning, she will play fetch until one of us dies.

She dropped it on my chest, on the books.

"No."

Stare.

"Fine." I threw it down the hall. She brought it back. Dropped it on my hand. I threw it. She brought it. Dropped it in my hand, wet, warm, which is the worst version. I threw it. Arise. Transmit. She brought it. Buddy, still standing, watched us like a foreman.

And somewhere around the tenth throw — I don't know how else to say this — something clicked. A small gear. I don't want to oversell it; it wasn't a light going on, it was a drawer closing somewhere in the back of the house.

She couldn't get the toy without me. That's the whole game. It has to *leave my hand.* Wet, warm, disgusting, it travels from me to her every single time — it's transmitted, that is literally the word, it's the same word they use for diseases, and for the same reason: nothing gets transmitted without somebody to catch it from. Whereas Buddy — Buddy just *arose.* Nobody handed him anything. He stood up on his own, from the floor, because I said a word.

One version of that sentence has a hand in it. Somebody's hand, holding something out. The other version, the story just stands up by itself.

"Huh," I said, to the ceiling.

I tried the other two, lying there. *First. Early.* First, first, first. Mora dropped the toy on my face. First — how does anyone know first? You dig, you hit the oldest thing, you say *these were the first people.* First is whatever's at the bottom of the hole. Early... early just means early. Early — I felt like it had room in it. Room for a before. But I couldn't make it do anything. I turned it over a few more times and put it down. Not everything opens at four in the morning.

I lay there with a wet rope on my chest and thought: maybe there's something here. And then, immediately, honestly: or maybe there's nothing anywhere, and I'm a man on a floor at four a.m. throwing the Paris Métro at a Doberman.

Both of those were true. And for some reason the second one is what got me up — the *maybe nothing*, because *maybe nothing* is a thing you can check. So I sat up, and the three things I'd half-figured came out in the order they'd arrived, none of them finished.

One. Whoever changed the sentence took the hand out. That's all I'll say for it. I don't know whose hand or what was in it. I know there was a hand, and now there isn't.

Two — and this one I didn't say out loud, because it frightened me more and I didn't want to hear it in the kitchen. It's not a fact they changed. Look at how the sentence opens: *the student must hold one principle above all others.* That's an instruction. A rule for looking. And maybe that's why this book, of all books — not because the idea was new; every myth on earth says the gods handed things down, Plato's got an old priest telling Solon the world's been wrecked by flood and fire and only the records remember. Nobody edits Plato. Plato's filed under *story.* Tresidder wrote it as *method* — a rule for students, in a discipline. The myths were allowed to say it. The footnotes, maybe, weren't. I'm not sure. It was four in the morning. But you don't have to fake a trial if you get to write the standard of proof.

Three. The silence. Fine — no claim, because a claim gets noticed. Fine — no note, because the author was two years dead. I could live with the silence. What I couldn't live with was the question it left standing in the middle of the kitchen: *why this idea?* Was this the only one? Or one of many? Somebody, somewhere, sometime after 1929, looked at a dry sentence in a book nobody read and *decided* it needed changing. Decided it was worth a plate. Decided the risk was fine. It's not weather. Somebody said *this one, this word, yes.*

I want to be precise about which of those scared me. Not the hand. Not the rule. Not even the man in the hills. Not yet.

The yes.

---

I didn't wake Ana. I noticed myself not even considering it, and I filed that too.

Mora had finally taken the toy back to the couch and gone upside down again, in the posture of a helicopter that has crashed and made its peace with it. Buddy had lain back down on his blanket, off duty. I stayed on the floor beside him with both copies, because a man on the floor is dog business, and he's a professional, even asleep.

"Two options," I told him, quiet. "One: some editor tidied up a dead man's book without the paperwork. Boring, sad, no story. Two: someone, at some point, decided what this page was allowed to say — waited until nobody could object, changed it without notice, and every copy printed since has agreed with him. And the only reason I know is that a dead man kept the one that didn't."

One thump of the tail, without opening his eyes. I chose not to interpret it.

I opened the Record and gave it the whole liturgy — title, year, both printings, photographs of both pages, the measurements, and a new line I'd never had reason to include before: *author status.* Then I reached the field where I classify the difference. Ten years of tidy vocabulary waiting in that little dropdown: *revision. correction. censorship (between editions). binding variant.* A taxonomy for every way a book is allowed to drift.

Nothing fit. This wasn't a correction; it had corrected nothing. It wasn't censorship; nothing was cut. It was a book pretending it had never changed.

I typed a new one and left it there:

**doesn't fit.**

And then, in the notes field underneath, where the Record lets you write things nobody will ever read, I typed the only fully honest sentence of that night:

*I have never once run this on a book where I already knew the answer.*

Ten years of the hobby. Hundreds of comparisons. Not one of them against a book that came with its own changes declared on the copyright page, where I could have marked my own work and found out whether I'm any good at this. I had a method I had never tested and a result I badly wanted. Any other week, I'd have laughed at me.

Then I sat on the kitchen floor next to an old dog, with cold coffee, and let myself think the whole thought — not the safe half, the whole thing.

Somebody changed a dead man's book and dressed it as the original.

To take a hand out of a sentence nobody was reading.

And the follow-up arrived on schedule, quiet, the way follow-ups do in my department:

*If it was worth doing here — where else?*
