# Chapter Eight

I spent nine days being wrong about the word *redefinition*, and I'm going to give you two of them, because I've decided you should see how this actually goes.

Day one I decided it meant the word *flood*. There's a whole literature about what the flood tablets meant by the flood and whether it was local, and I read a great deal of it and it was interesting and it had nothing to do with anything.

Day four I decided it was about carbon dating calibration curves, which is a real thing, which does move, which I now know a genuinely unnecessary amount about.

Day six my wife asked me what I was doing and I said "reading about tree rings" and heard it.

Day nine I stopped trying to be clever and did what I should have done first, which was look up what happened in 1994 in the hills outside Şanlıurfa, in southeastern Turkey.

Urfa. Where a man walked east in 1929 and never came back.

---

In 1994 a German archaeologist went to look at a low hill that had been surveyed decades earlier and written off as a medieval cemetery. The survey team back then had found some limestone slabs and broken pieces and concluded, reasonably, that it was a graveyard, and moved on. That's not incompetence. That's what you do with a field full of broken limestone in a country full of ruins: you write a line and you move on.

He went back and started digging and what came out of the ground was a set of enclosures: rings of standing stones, T-shaped, some of them five metres tall and weighing sixteen tonnes, carved — properly carved, in relief — with foxes and boars and vultures and scorpions and a recurring pair of arms and hands wrapped around the pillars as if the stones were people wearing belts.

And the dates came back at around nine thousand five hundred years before Christ.

I'm going to let that sit the way it sat with me, which was badly, for about four days.

Eleven and a half thousand years ago. Six thousand years before Stonehenge. Seven thousand years before the pyramids. Before pottery. Before the wheel. Before writing. Before — and this is the one that does the damage — before farming, or right at the edge of it, in the same handful of hills where the wild wheat that became our wheat still grows.

I did the arithmetic several times because I assumed I'd dropped a digit. I hadn't. The gap between that hill and the first pharaoh is longer than the gap between the first pharaoh and me.

---

Now. Here's where I have to be careful, and where I need you to trust me a little, because this is exactly the point at which the men I have spent years laughing at go off the road.

None of that is hidden.

It's not suppressed, it's not forbidden, it's not a secret. It is in the literature, it is on the site's own signage, it has been on television, it is on a Turkish banknote. There are academic careers built on it and arguments about it and revisions of it, and the revisions are getting better, not worse. Nobody is being silenced. Anybody who tells you the hill is being covered up is lying to you or hasn't looked.

What interested me was smaller than that, and it was about a word.

Before that hill, if you had asked what hunter-gatherers were capable of, you'd have got a fairly firm answer: small mobile bands, low population, no surplus, no monumental building, no organised labour, no religion of that scale. Not because anyone was being dogmatic — because that's what all the evidence said, and it said it consistently, and the story ran in one direction: you settle down, you farm, you get a surplus, you get a hierarchy, and *then* you build temples.

The hill is a temple built by people who had not settled down.

So the sentence in the textbooks had to change. And it did change, and here is the thing I sat with for a long time: **it changed by widening the category rather than by admitting the story was wrong.**

Hunter-gatherers, it turns out, were capable of organised monumental construction. Hunter-gatherers, it turns out, could concentrate labour seasonally. Hunter-gatherers, it turns out, may have built the temple *first* and farmed afterwards, because feeding the work crews is a hell of an incentive to domesticate a cereal.

All of which may well be true. Some of it is beautiful and I think about it while I'm losing golf balls.

But notice the shape. Nothing was ever wrong. The definition simply grew to include the thing that had contradicted it, and everybody moved on, and no page anywhere says *we were mistaken about what people were.*

I know that shape. I have been on the other end of it my entire life.

When I and the record disagree, I'm the error. And when the ground and the category disagree — the category gets a new definition, and everyone agrees there was never a problem.

That's not a conspiracy. That's how a field survives being surprised, and if it didn't do that, it couldn't function. I want to be scrupulous here: **the goalpost move is ordinary science.** Perfectly honest, perfectly defensible, done in public by people acting in good faith.

Which is precisely why it is the best possible place to hide.

If you wanted to move a date and have nobody notice, you would not need to suppress anything. You would only need to make sure the *category* moved with it. Because the human beings involved will do the rest themselves, sincerely, in print, out of professional necessity, and they will feel like they are being rigorous while they do it.

I wrote that in the file and then went for a walk because I didn't like it.

---

There were two things at the hill I couldn't put down, and only one of them is famous.

The famous one is the date.

The other one is that somebody buried it.

The enclosures didn't fall down and get covered by time. They were deliberately backfilled — packed, on purpose, by hand, with rubble and bone and debris, filled in to the tops of the pillars and left. That's why they survived and that's why they were invisible until 1994. It took an enormous amount of work by a large number of people to do that. Nobody knows why. There are theories: ritual closure, decommissioning, a ceremony of ending. They're decent theories. They are also, every single one of them, a guess about the interior life of people who left no writing.

What is *not* a guess is the physical fact.

Somebody took the most extraordinary thing built for the next six thousand years, and hid it, carefully, thoroughly, and then went away.

And here is the only sentence I would actually fight for. It's not a claim about who or why. It's smaller than that:

**The first monumental structure we know of was also the first deliberate concealment we know of, and it was the same people, and it was on purpose.**

Whatever else that is, it is a very old habit.

---

I did the did-you-know on the third tee that week, because I couldn't hold it.

The four retirees have been on that hole since 1998 and they are the only audience I have ever had that likes the real ones. I gave them the hill. The date, the pillars, the six thousand years before Stonehenge, and then, because I couldn't help it, the burial.

The one with the visor — I don't know his name, we've never done names, it's a golf course — said: "So who buried it?"

"Nobody knows."

"Somebody knows," he said, cheerfully, taking a practice swing. "Somebody always knows. They're just dead."

His friends laughed. I laughed. I had to stand very still for a second afterwards.

They asked me twice more that month to tell it again. It's the only material I've ever had that people ask for a second time, and I noticed, because I'd been performing did-you-knows for twenty years to make people go away, and this was the first one that made anyone come closer.

---

So: entry seventeen.

*Highlands of Anatolia*, 1963, page 212. First impression: *eleven thousand years.* The 1971 reprint: *a much earlier period.*

A phrase with a number in it, replaced by a phrase with no number in it. Which is a smaller change than the two I'd verified and a much worse one, when you sit with it. *Eleven thousand years* is checkable. *A much earlier period* is a sentence that cannot be wrong, and therefore cannot be tested, and therefore cannot ever be the thing that makes somebody go and look.

And in the margin, in blue ballpoint: *1994 — the hill.*

Thirty-one years before an archaeologist walked up that slope with a wheelbarrow, somebody wrote *eleven thousand years* about that region in a survey nobody read, and eight years later the number was gone.

I want to say clearly what I could and could not do with that.

I could not verify it. That is the whole problem, and it is the next two years of my life. There are three known copies of the 1963 first impression. One is in a national library. I have never been able to hold both printings of entry seventeen, and by my own rule — held, both, run — it does not count.

I ordered the 1971 reprint that night for nine euros and it came in a week and page 212 says exactly what the professor said it says. Which proves precisely nothing, since half of the sentence is the half I can't see.

What I had was a man's handwriting and a coincidence of dates.

I'd like to tell you I stayed disciplined about that. I did, in the file. In the file I wrote *unverified — one printing only — do not count.*

In my chest something else had already happened, and it had happened at the exact moment I read that a group of human beings had knelt down eleven thousand years ago and filled in the most remarkable thing they had ever made, and gone away, and said nothing.

---

I've thought a lot about which evening the change happened, and I think it was that one, in the front room, with the dog against my foot and the reprint open on my knee and the list on the arm of the chair.

Up to then I had a hobby. A hobby is a thing you do to things. You find them, you compare them, you file them, you go to bed. The finding is the pleasure. Nothing follows from it.

That night, for the first time, I turned the page and *expected* something.

That's the whole difference. A hobbyist collects. An investigator expects — and once you expect, you can be disappointed, and once you can be disappointed you're in it, because now the world owes you an answer and you're going to go and get it.

I got up, and I went to the shelf by the window, and I took the books off one whole shelf — the third one, at eye level, the good shelf — and stacked them on the floor in the corner where they stayed for the next two years and drove Ana slowly mad.

Then I put the list on the empty shelf, and the two copies of *Waters Before Memory* beside it, and the two paperbacks with the ziggurat on the cover beside those.

Room for thirty-eight more.

Buddy watched the entire performance from the doorway with the expression of a dog who has seen a man do a great many stupid things and has decided to stay for this one too.

"Forty-one," I told him. "Two down."

He thumped his tail once.

I have replayed that evening enough times to be sure of what I knew. I knew, in the ordinary way you know things about yourself that you have no intention of acting on, that a man who clears a shelf for forty-one entries is not going to be the same man when the shelf is full.

I cleared it anyway. That's the honest version.

Nobody made me. There was no vow, there was no calling, there was no moment of destiny in my front room in a quiet country on a Tuesday night.

There was a shelf, and a list, and a man who has never in his life been able to leave a thing half-checked.
