# Chapter Ten

The sleeve was still on the shelf in November.

Half a sleeve, pale green, needles still in it, paused mid-row since March. Nine months. We had both walked past it every day for nine months and neither of us had ever said one word about it out loud, which I think is the single most Dutch thing our marriage has ever done, and neither of us is Dutch.

I dusted around it once. That is the closest I came, for a long time, to being any good at this.

---

She waited until a Thursday, which was clever, because on a Thursday I have no excuse. No golf. No deadline. Nothing to be five minutes away from.

She'd made the thing with the aubergines. Mora was under the table with her chin on my foot in the hope of a policy change. Buddy was on his blanket by the radiator, which was new — the radiator thing had started that autumn, and I'd noticed and not said anything about that either.

"Can I ask you something," Ana said, "and can you not do the voice."

"What voice."

"The explaining voice. Where you make it interesting so I stop asking."

I put my fork down. "Okay."

"When was the last time you told me something fun?"

"What?"

"A did-you-know. A real one. The Romans, the thing about the bees, the man with the spear." She was doing her food, not looking at me, which is how she does the surgical ones. "You used to do about four a week. I used to complain about it."

"You did complain about it."

"I did. I want it noted that I complained." She looked up. "You haven't done one since summer."

I did the arithmetic, quickly, in my head, the way I do, and she was right, and she was more right than she knew, because it wasn't that I'd stopped having them. I had more of them than I'd ever had in my life. I had a folder of them. They'd just all stopped being tellable.

"I've been busy."

"That's not it."

"It's a bit it."

"James." She hardly ever uses it. It landed the way it always lands. "You're not busy. You're somewhere."

---

Here's where I should have told her.

I've been over this evening a lot. It comes up at four in the morning, at that hour when your head shows you the reruns. And what I keep landing on is that I had a clean run at it — a whole evening, an aubergine thing, two dogs, nobody drunk, no crisis, my wife asking me a direct question in a kind voice.

There was a version where I said: *a book on my shelf was altered by somebody after the author died, and I've found a second one, and there's a list of forty-one and I don't know who wrote it.*

She would have believed me. That's the part I underestimated for two more years. She'd have believed me because she has watched me be right about small dull things for fifteen years, and because she is the only person alive who has never once required me to be normal.

What I did instead was give her sixty per cent. I'm not proud of it. But I want to show you the mechanics, because it's not lying, it's worse — it's editing, and I'd been reading about editing for months and still didn't recognise my own hands doing it.

"There's a thing in the professor's books," I said. "One of them was changed between printings. Not a correction — somebody went in and changed a line and made it look like they hadn't."

"Okay." She waited. "And?"

"And it's the kind of thing that if I explain it properly takes an hour and if I explain it badly I sound like a man with a wall of string."

"Do you have a wall of string?"

"I have a shelf."

"I've seen the shelf. There are two hundred books on my floor because of the shelf."

"There are a hundred and sixty."

"There are two hundred, and I counted, and I want you to think about what that means about my Tuesday." She almost smiled. Then didn't. "Is that all of it?"

And there it was. The door, held open by somebody who loves you.

"That's the shape of it," I said.

Which is true. It is the shape of it. It's just not the size of it, and she asked me about size, and I answered about shape, and she is not a stupid woman.

She looked at me for a second longer than the sentence needed.

"Okay," she said, and started clearing the plates.

---

The rest of it happened by the sink, and it's the part I actually needed.

"You know what's strange?" she said, with her back to me. "You've got more interesting since Grandma died. And I see you less."

"That's not —"

"I'm not fighting. I'm telling you what it's like in here." She turned the tap on and then off again. "When we met you were the only person at that entire party who asked me a question. Do you remember what you asked me?"

"Something about yourself, not work, not family."

"Not work, not family," she agreed. "Which I found out later is a trick."

"It's not a trick."

"It's a trick, James. It's a very good trick and I fell for it and I'd fall for it again." She dried her hands. "But here's the thing about that question. You never ask me it any more. You ask it to strangers on aeroplanes."

I didn't have anything. The persona has a line for everything and it had nothing for that.

"I don't need to know what the book says," she said. "I've never needed to know what any of it says. I like that you're like this, I've always liked that you're like this, my friends think I'm mad and I don't care." She put the towel down. "I need to know where you are. That's all I ever ask you. And for about six months the answer has been *not here*, and you keep saying it in the present tense so it doesn't count as a promise."

I looked at her.

"You noticed that."

"I've been married to you for fifteen years. I notice everything. I just don't do a folder about it."

---

She went up at half ten. She kissed the top of my head on the way past, the same as always, which somehow made it worse.

I sat with the dogs.

This is the thing I've never been able to explain to anyone who hasn't lived with animals, and I'm not going to try very hard, because the people who know, know. I got down on the kitchen floor with my back against the cupboard, and Buddy came off his blanket — three steps, the grunt, the settling — and put himself against my leg, and Mora came and dropped the toy on my chest and then, uncharacteristically, lay down instead of demanding the throw.

And I told them all of it.

Out loud. In order. The two printings and the untouched typos and the plate blemish on page 118. The fifty per cent and the fifth change that never existed. The dropped negative on page 140. The nine euros and the ziggurat on the cover. The eleven thousand years and the men who knelt down and filled it all in on purpose and went away. Entry seventeen and the year 1994. The word *Ask* in shaking ink.

It took about forty minutes. Neither of them left.

At the end of it I said the thing I hadn't said upstairs, to the woman it was actually about, which is:

"I don't know how to tell her without becoming a man she has to manage."

Buddy sighed through his nose the way old dogs do, which I have decided not to interpret, and put his chin on my ankle.

It would be easy to make that scene warm and leave it there. It was warm. It was also the exact moment the shape of the next two years got set, and I set it, on a kitchen floor, late on a Thursday, by choosing an audience that could not ask me a follow-up question.

That's what they were for. That's what they'd always been for, and I'd been calling it love.

It was love. It was both. It's always both.

---

I went up eventually. She was asleep, or doing the thing where she's asleep enough not to have to talk.

I lay there and thought about the sleeve on the shelf, pale green, paused mid-row, nine months in.

Her grandmother used to undo whatever she'd knitted and start it again from the beginning. Ana always found that funny. She told me that story about four times a year for fifteen years and every single time she laughed at the same place.

I lay in the dark and thought: *the old woman wasn't being funny. She was checking her own work. She got to the end of a row and she wanted to know if it held.*

And then I thought: *that's not what she was doing either. You've been doing this for six months and now everything is about the thing.*

I caught it. That was the first time I saw it happen live — the pattern reaching out and eating something that had nothing to do with it, a dead woman's knitting, my wife's favourite story.

I caught it that night.

I didn't catch it many more times after that.
