# Chapter Twenty-Five

The corridor came back four days later and it came back in the afternoon, in daylight, while I was doing something else, which I understand is normal and which nobody tells you.

I was putting a bowl in the dishwasher.

---

I was sixteen. It was a Thursday and I was at school and then I was not at school, and then there was a corridor.

I have had this memory continuously for twenty-eight years. That's the thing. It never went anywhere. I could have told you about it at any point — I have told two of the shrinks about it, in a flat voice, as background, the way you give a doctor your history: *my father died when I was sixteen, it was sudden, I was there at the end.* Both of them wrote it down. One of them came back to it twice and I gave him nothing and he moved on, professionally, without pushing, and I remember being relieved and thinking well of him for it.

So I want to be exact about what happened at the dishwasher, because *repressed memory* is not what happened and I don't believe in it and neither should you.

Nothing came back. It was all already there.

What happened is that for twenty-eight years it had been a *file*, and on a Tuesday afternoon in August it was, for about ninety seconds, an afternoon.

The floor was that speckled grey. There was a radiator under the window at the end that was too hot to lean on and I leaned on it anyway. There was a machine somewhere doing three beeps and a pause, three beeps and a pause. My mother was in the room and I was in the corridor because a nurse had said *maybe give them a minute* and I had said okay, and I stood out there and looked at a noticeboard about hand hygiene and read the whole thing twice.

He died while I was reading a poster about washing your hands.

Nobody did anything wrong. I want to say that. The nurse was kind and my mother was not to blame for anything and I have never for one second thought otherwise. I was sixteen and I was asked to step out and I stepped out, which is what you do, because at sixteen the entire architecture of your personality is *doing what is expected in a corridor*.

And then it was afterwards, and it stayed afterwards for twenty-eight years.

---

Here's what I understood at the dishwasher, and it took the ninety seconds, and I did not go looking for it.

**I never did the grief.** Not because I'm cold and not because I'm clever. Because nobody gave me a container to put it in.

At the funeral they said the words. *He's at peace. He's in a better place. You'll see him again.* And I was sixteen and I had been a person who checks things since I was ten, and I stood there and thought *based on what*, and the thinking was not brave or precocious, it was **structural** — the words came, and my head did what my head does, and the words did not stick.

They stuck to everybody else. I watched them stick. I watched my mother take that sentence and hold it like bread and be able to breathe afterwards, and I could not, and what I concluded — at sixteen, silently, permanently — was that there was something wrong with me.

So I put it away. Not the memory. The *grief.* There was no shelf for it, so it went in the one place I have ever put anything I can't file, which is the category marked *tired*, and it has been sitting in there for twenty-eight years next to two hundred and eleven flashes.

---

I'd like to say something about the shrinks, and it is the only score-settling I am going to do, and it isn't with them.

Four of them, over twenty years. All competent. All decent. Every one of them eventually did the thing: closed the notebook and told me there was nothing here to treat, that I was not unwell, that I thought a great deal and would like company for it and they couldn't in good conscience keep charging me. Fired for being fine, four times.

And they were **right**. That's what I want to be clear about. Every one of them was right on the evidence available. I presented as a well-defended, high-functioning, articulate man with a good marriage and no symptoms, who mentioned in passing that his father had died and that he sometimes got a mental image of a hand.

There is no diagnostic category for *this man's central experience has no shelf in your building.* There isn't one. It isn't a failure of the profession, and I'd defend them against anybody, because the alternative to the categories is a world where anybody can say anything about the inside of your head.

But here's what it means, and it's the sentence I got up and wrote down:

**Four experts, twenty years, and the two most important facts about me were never found, because neither of them is a symptom of anything.**

A man who cannot be comforted by the standard words. And a man who has been getting something his whole life that his own system reclassifies as noise before he can look at it.

Both invisible. Both sitting in plain sight, in the notes, in handwriting, in four separate files.

Not hidden.

**Just not looked for.**

---

I have to be careful with the next part, because it's the one that ties the whole book together and it would be very easy to overplay it, and if I overplay it you'll stop trusting me and you'd be right to.

Here it is as plainly as I can put it.

At the graveside, on the first morning of all this, I did some arithmetic that I've never been able to unthink. If the wheel turns and you come back — you don't come back as *you*. You come back a stranger with a stranger's face in another decade, and the person you loved comes back somewhere else on their own schedule, and the two of you could pass each other in an airport and feel nothing.

I called it a machine for making sure no one ever finds anyone again.

I meant it as a complaint about a *belief*. I thought I was reviewing somebody's theology, unkindly, on a beautiful morning, while holding my wife's hand.

And in August, on a kitchen floor, what I got — whatever it was, and I have told you I don't know what it was — was hundreds of instances of exactly that. Not death. **Interruption.** Everything cut off mid-sentence and nothing carrying over. A process with an enormous amount of unfinished work in it and nobody keeping the ends together.

I had written the description three years before I had the experience, at a graveside, as a joke about the afterlife, and I had never once considered that a description could be **of something**.

I'm not going to tell you what does it or who does it or whether anybody does. I don't know. That is the actual answer and it is not going to improve.

What I'll say is what a monitoring man says when he's found the shape of a fault and none of the cause:

Somewhere in this, there is a mechanism that separates.

---

Then I went and got the list, and I read it for the fourth time in three years, and it was a different document.

Because there are entries on it I have always quietly considered the odd ones out.

**#26.** A journal article, 1993. Correlated activity in isolated subjects — two people in separate rooms, shielded, and something showing up in both. Withdrawn "at the author's request" in 1995. The author could not have requested it. In the margin, in Maasen's hand: *same method as 11. sixty-four years apart.*

**#38.** A popular book on physics, 1981, page 160. First printing: *the question of what constitutes an observer remains open.* Reprint: *the question is now generally regarded as settled.* In the margin: *see 11.*

For three years I had read those as noise. I had a beautiful clean pattern about the deep past — givers deleted, dates pulled down, *early* becomes *first* — and here were two entries about brains and one about physics, and I had put them in a mental sub-folder marked *the professor also had other interests*, and moved on.

*See 11.* He wrote it twice. Entry eleven is Tresidder. Page 118. *Transmitted to the early* → *arisen among the first.*

He was telling me they were the same edit. He'd been telling me for three years in two words and I hadn't been able to read it, because I had decided in advance what kind of list it was.

Look at what all four actually do.

*Transmitted to* → *arisen among.* Somebody handed it over becomes it came up by itself.
*A tradition of instruction* → *a tradition.* Somebody taught somebody becomes it was always like that.
*What constitutes an observer remains open* → *now generally regarded as settled.* The question of whether the thing looking is part of the thing becomes a closed matter you needn't raise.
And a paper about two shielded rooms, gone.

Every one of them removes a **connection**.

Between people and what they were given. Between one mind and another. Between the looker and the looked-at.

It is not a history project. It never was. I have spent three years believing I was investigating a hundred-year campaign about archaeology, and it isn't about archaeology; archaeology is just where the paperwork is easiest to see.

Whatever this is, it works on one principle, and the principle is: **make sure nothing is ever connected to anything.**

Which is, word for word, the arithmetic I did at a graveside.

---

I sat at the table with the list and my hands were shaking, which is not a thing I do, and the dog woke up and put his chin on my foot.

*Something doesn't fit.*

I have said that for twenty-five years. It's the thing I say when a system's story about itself doesn't match its behaviour, and it's what I typed into the taxonomy on the worst night of my life because none of the ten tidy words fit, and it is, I am aware, an absolutely ordinary sentence that anybody might say about anything.

It's one sentence.

I had thought I was saying it about a book. And then about forty books. And then about a hundred years of somebody's house style.

And on the fifth of August I said it about the fact that a man can love a woman for fifteen years and lose her grandmother and stand in gold light and feel a hand he almost knows, and have no way — *none*, not one, in the entire enormous apparatus of human comfort, science, medicine and religion — to put those things in the same room as each other.

Same sentence. Same complaint. Same missing thing.

It fits together, is what I'm saying. The thing I've been chasing on paper and the thing that's been happening in my head since I was six are not two subjects.

They're one seam, and I'd been standing on it my whole life.

---

I rang Ana at half past ten at night, which we don't do.

She picked up on the second ring, which told me something.

"What's wrong."

"Nothing's wrong. Buddy's fine, he's here, he's asleep." I was standing in the kitchen with the lights off. "I need to say something and I'm going to say it badly."

"Okay."

"I never grieved my dad."

There was a silence on the other end, and then my wife — who had been living at her mother's for five weeks, who had every entitlement in the world to say something else — said, very quietly:

"I know."

"You know?"

"James, I've known for fifteen years." I heard her sit down. "I didn't know how to say it without it sounding like an accusation. And every time I got close you'd do a joke."

I stood in the dark for a while.

"I don't know what to do with it," I said. "There's nowhere to put it. That's the actual problem, it was always the actual problem, I was sixteen and everybody handed me a sentence and the sentence wouldn't —"

"I know," she said again.

And then: "Do you want me to come home?"

And here is the thing I did, and everything after it proceeds from it.

I said, "Not yet."

Because I had, by then, a depot in Turkey with a very good climate-control specification, and a transfer schedule in an annex in six-point type, and a man who had been in my garden with tongs, and I was not going to bring my wife back into that house until I understood the shape of what I was going to do.

I told myself that was protecting her. I have thought about it a great deal since and I think, on balance, this time, it actually was.

I have used that sentence to mean four different things. Only one of them was true, and I could not tell them apart at the time, and neither could she.
