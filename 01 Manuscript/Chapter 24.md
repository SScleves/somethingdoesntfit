# Chapter Twenty-Four

August. The house was empty except for the two of us, and I want to describe what that month was actually like, because it wasn't dramatic and I think the undramatic version is rarer.

I got up. I fed the dog. I worked, and I worked competently — that's the part people don't believe, but I did; I did a good job on a bad system in Lisbon that August and somebody wrote a nice thing about it. I did the shopping. I took Buddy out on the flat by the water twice a day, slowly, on the new mats and the new tablets, and he got better, in the way twelve-year-old dogs get better, which is that they get about forty per cent of it back and you take it.

And I had run out of things to check.

I want to be precise, because *I've run out of leads* is a thing men say in films and it usually means they're sad. This was an inventory. I sat down on the first Saturday in August and made the list of everything that was still open, and there were four items on it, and I went through them one at a time over three weeks and closed all four.

The transfer schedules were public and I had them and the ninth destination was a depot in Turkey with a very good climate-control specification and no publication record. That was a *thread*, and I'll come back to it, and I did not touch it for another five months. I'll explain why later and it isn't cowardice, it's arithmetic.

Ana was at her mother's and we spoke on Sundays and it was fine, and fine is a terrible word and I mean every letter of it.

Noor was gone. Els Maasen had asked me not to write. Beard Man was Beard Man and I had used him up. Frank was Frank. There was nobody.

So the month came down to: an old dog, a table, forty ruled lines I had been reading for three years, and the specific silence of a house where somebody else's shoes aren't in the hall.

That's the condition. That's all it was. I didn't go looking for anything. I was not *seeking*; I have read enough of that literature since to know what seeking sounds like and I have no patience for it and I didn't do it.

I sat down because there was nothing else to do and because I'd been doing it badly for eight months already.

---

Twenty minutes, kitchen chair, timer, six in the morning, an old dog watching with visible concern.

I'd been doing that since the winter. I've told you what it produced: aching legs, the shopping, an argument I lost in 2011, and — occasionally, in among it — a face. A hand. Gone in less than a second.

I'd logged four hundred of them by August. Date, duration, description, and a column for familiarity, one to five, because I am who I am.

And in August, with nobody in the house, I did it for longer. Not because of any theory. Because the twenty minutes ended and there was nothing on the other side of it, so I stayed in the chair.

Three nights of nothing. Forty minutes, an hour, an hour and ten. My legs. The shopping. Frank's shirt. A tune. Nothing, nothing, nothing, and a rising irritation which I recognised, with some disgust, as the exact feeling I get when a test I've designed refuses to produce a result.

Which is the whole problem, and on the fourth night I understood it.

**I was running an experiment on myself and I was the instrument.** Everything I know, all of it, every single thing that has ever worked for me, depends on the observer and the observed being different objects. Photograph before you read. Tape on the boards, A and B. Never compare a book with itself.

And here there was one object.

So on the fourth night I stopped trying to get anywhere. That's it. That's the whole technique and it can't be taught and every tradition on earth has spent three thousand years saying it in a way that makes it sound like more than it is. I gave up. I sat in a chair at eleven at night with no intention of any kind, because the intention was the thing in the way, and because after three nights of failure I genuinely no longer cared what happened.

---

I'm going to describe what happened next as flatly as I can, and I want to say in advance that I have no idea what it was.

I'm not going to pretend to certainty I don't have; that would make everything else worthless. What follows is a report of an experience. An experience is not evidence of anything except itself.

The wall gave.

That's the only way I've ever been able to say it. There's a wall. There has been a wall my entire life and I never knew it was there, in the way you don't know about your own eyelids. It isn't a wall between me and the world. It's between me and — the rest of the file.

And it didn't come down. It gave, the way a jammed drawer gives, all at once, four centimetres, and then stops.

---

What came through was not light and it was not a tunnel and nobody was waiting for me.

It was **lives**.

Not a life. Not a story with a beginning. What I got was the texture of an enormous number of ordinary days, and I got them the way you get a smell — instantly, whole, unarguable, and impossible to render into a sentence afterwards without lying.

Here's what they were like, and this is the part I was not prepared for and could not have invented, because it's the opposite of every version of this I have ever read:

They were **boring**.

They were short and they were laborious and they were mostly weather. Cold. Being cold in a way that isn't an event because it's every day. Hands doing something repetitive that had to be done again tomorrow. Waiting for somebody. Children — a great deal of children, and this is where it stopped being bearable — children who were there and then were not there, and the *not there* handled with a kind of practical, exhausted competence that I could feel in my own chest and that no one alive in my country has any equipment for.

And under all of it, constantly, the thing I keep trying to describe to people and failing:

**Everything cut off mid-sentence.**

Not endings. Not deaths, particularly — deaths were in there, plenty of them, and they were the least of it. What was unbearable was the interruption. Somebody is in the middle of something with somebody. A conversation, a piece of work, a quarrel, a hand on a hand — and it *stops*, not because it finished but because that's where the record ends, and the next thing is somewhere else with somebody else and none of it carries.

Over and over. Hundreds of times. Thousands.

And it wasn't tragic. That's the thing that broke me. It was **administrative.** It had the exact quality of a system doing what it does: no malice, no author, no one at the desk — just a process, running, with an enormous amount of unfinished work in it, and nobody anywhere keeping the ends together.

I have spent my whole life looking at systems that lose things. I know what a system that loses things feels like from the inside of the log file. That is what it felt like.

---

At some point I was on the kitchen floor. I don't know how. I have no memory of getting out of the chair and I would very much like to have that memory.

Buddy was there. Obviously Buddy was there.

And there were hands.

I've been trying to write this paragraph for two days. Hands — specific ones, not symbols, not *a hand* in the way a poem has a hand. Hands with weather in them and work in them. A hand with the nail gone black. A very small hand, badly. A hand doing something with rope that my own hands, lying on lino in a kitchen in a flat country in the twenty-first century, knew how to do.

And one hand cracked across the knuckles.

Cold mud on my cheek and a low grey sky and a hand reaching, cracked at the knuckles, a hand I *almost* knew, the way you almost know a word in a language you've forgotten.

I have written that sentence once before. It was the first thing I put down. It happened in gold light at a graveside on a beautiful day, for less than the length of a camera flash, and I filed it under lack of sleep before it had finished, and I told you at the time that the difference between those two sentences would turn out to matter more than anything else in my life.

---

I came back at about four. It had been five hours. I'd have said forty minutes.

And I did the only thing I know how to do, which is that I got the notebook and wrote it down before the excitement could edit it, because excitement edits memory in about four seconds and I have watched it do it.

Then I sat there at the table and did the audit, and the audit is the actual chapter, and it took until it got light.

**Objection one, and it is enormous: I am a forty-four-year-old man who has been alone in a house for six weeks, whose wife has left, whose dog nearly died, who has been reading about death for three years, and who sat in the dark for five hours with his eyes closed.** Every single component of that produces exactly this. If a stranger described this to me I would have the explanation out of my mouth before he'd finished, and I would be right to, and it would be the likeliest thing.

**Objection two: I have been primed for a decade.** Everything in the last three years pushed toward it. If my head were going to manufacture something, it would manufacture *this*, in this vocabulary, with hands in it, because hands are what my entire book is about — a hand taken out of a sentence.

I wrote both of those down and I have never argued my way out of either.

Here is the one thing I have, and it is small, and it is the only reason I am putting any of this down.

**The log.**

Eight months of it by then. Four hundred entries. And in the days after, going back through it, and then back through the older file, and then back through my life —

They aren't new.

The face in the shower. The hand on the doorframe, aged nine, in a house we moved out of in 1993. Cold mud and a grey sky at a graveside. The one on the plane in 2011 that made me open my eyes. The four seconds before sleep, which everybody gets, which is why nobody has ever thought about it for one second in the history of the world.

I have been getting these since I was six years old. There is a column in a notebook in which I have been dutifully recording them since January, and there are two hundred and eleven of them from before I ever sat in a chair, and every last one of them was filed, automatically, instantly, without any decision on my part, under **tired**.

I keep a folder on printings. Four hundred entries, ten years, photographs of both pages, a taxonomy with a dropdown menu.

I have never in my entire life kept a record of the anomalies in my own head.

I have spent three years being professionally outraged that somebody edited a book without saying so, and the whole time there has been a second archive running, mine, unlogged, in which every single unexplained entry gets silently reclassified as noise by a default I did not write and never once read.

*monotonic drift, below threshold. reclassification, seasonal.*

The wall of comfort isn't out there. Or it isn't only out there. There's one shipped with the equipment.

---

I want to be honest about where that leaves me, because this is the point at which most books of this kind hand you something and I'm not going to.

I do not know what happened in that kitchen. I have no mechanism. I cannot tell you how, and I am not going to pretend, and if you have come this far hoping the end explains it, I would stop here, with my apologies and my genuine respect.

What I have is this, and it's all I've got, and it's enough to have changed everything I did afterwards:

For thirty-eight years I have had a category called *tired* into which anything I could not account for went automatically, without review.

And on the fifth of August I opened it, for the first time, and it was not empty.
