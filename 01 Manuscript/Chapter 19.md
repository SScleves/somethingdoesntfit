# Chapter Nineteen

Ana found the boards in February.

Not the list — the list was on a shelf in plain sight and she'd stopped seeing it a year ago, the way you stop seeing a coat rack. What she found was four printed pages on the kitchen table, because I'd printed them at work like an idiot and brought them home and fallen asleep on the sofa.

Seven names. Two columns. Lines drawn between them in pen.

"What's this?"

I was still doing the thing where you wake up and try to look like a man who has been awake.

"Company registers. It's public."

"I can see it's public, it's got a website address at the bottom." She sat down opposite me with it. Not angry. Reading. She reads faster than me. "Who are these?"

"They fund a digitisation programme at a library."

"And this side?"

"Also them."

She looked at the second column for a while. It says, at the top, in the language those things use, *continuity planning and critical-infrastructure resilience.* You don't need me to translate it and neither did she.

"James."

"It's probably nothing."

And my wife — who has never once in fifteen years asked me whether one of my things was *true*, who has bought me tinfoil hats in three sizes, who told me my weird was how I love things and gave me a medal for it at a kitchen table — put the paper down and said:

"This isn't a printing thing."

"No."

"You told me in October it was finished."

"I know."

"You said it at breakfast. You said *I think I've taken it as far as it goes.*" She wasn't raising her voice. That was the problem. "I remember it because I was pleased. I told my mother."

There was nothing to say to that. I've gone over it and there was nothing.

"Okay," she said, and stood up, and took the pages with her, which I hadn't expected, and put them on the shelf next to the sleeve. Then she went upstairs and slept in the spare room, and in the morning she was normal, and made coffee, and asked what time I'd be back, and that was worse than a fight by a distance I can't measure.

Mora slept in with her. Buddy stayed with me. He's not loyal, he's just old and the stairs are the stairs.


---

Noor had been in my life for about three months by then, and we had settled into the thing we do, which is that I send her a pattern and she tells me it isn't one.

What she cannot do — and this is the part that made us work — is see a pattern to save her life.

> **HER:** so you have nine of these
>
> **ME:** ten now
>
> **HER:** and each one is an interesting anomaly in a specific book
>
> **ME:** each one is an interesting anomaly in a specific book AND they all move the same direction
>
> **HER:** that is the part I don't do. I look at one object. When I look at ten objects and say something about all of them at once I get frightened of myself.

And I said the thing I'd been not saying for two years, into a chat window at midnight, to a woman I had never met.

> **ME:** that's what I'm for.

---

The chart took a weekend and I'll show you the axes because otherwise it's just a man being certain.

Down the side: about thirty civilisations, the ones we have enough of a record for — Uruk, Akkad, the Old Kingdom, the Indus cities, Ugarit, Mycenae, Hattusa, the Western Roman provinces, Angkor, the Classic Maya, Cahokia, Great Zimbabwe, on and on. Across the bottom: time, normalised, so that each one's rise and end sit on the same scale.

And then one column I had to define, and defining it is the whole argument, so here's how I defined it: **the highest point of the thing they were best at.** Not wealth, not army size, not extent — the peak of their own particular competence. Their best surveying, their best astronomy, their best plumbing, their best writing, their best road.

Then the distance from that peak to the end.

Now. I built that chart hoping to see nothing, which is what I always hope, and which I have learned is the only honest way to start.

They don't fall from the bottom.

They fall from near the top. Not at the top — that's too neat and it isn't what the data says. Within a generation or two *after* the best thing they ever did. The road is finished, the calendar is corrected, the aqueduct works, somebody writes something nobody will beat for four hundred years, and then, inside sixty or eighty years, it's over.

I sat back from that and felt cold, and then I did what I have trained myself to do, which is go and find the reason it's rubbish.

I found four. I'll give you them.

One: I chose the peaks. A man who defines the top of a curve and then measures from it will find whatever he likes, and I could feel my own thumb on the scale while I was doing it. Two: survivorship — I only have records for the ones that got good enough to leave records, so of *course* the record ends near the good part; that's what a record is. Three: complexity theory says this anyway, and has for decades, in print, by serious people who are not me: the more elaborate a system, the tighter its couplings, the less slack it has when something goes wrong. Four: thirty is not a lot of anything.

I sent it to Noor.

> **HER:** it's very pretty
>
> **ME:** and
>
> **HER:** and it proves nothing, and you know it proves nothing, or you would not have sent me the four reasons it proves nothing in the same message.
>
> **ME:** so it's worthless
>
> **HER:** no. It's a question. You're allowed a question. What you are not allowed is to go around describing it as a finding because it makes a nicer shape than a question does.

---

The thing that isn't a question came out of the same weekend and it took about ten minutes.

I've told you about the spreadsheet: forty entries, two columns of years, the year of impression A and the year of the edit. I'd made it in March, looked at it for four seconds, thought *huh*, and gone to bed.

I opened it again because I wanted to lay the edits against the chart and see whether the edits landed near anybody's peak. They don't, particularly, and that idea went in the bin where it belonged.

But when you sort forty numbers by the second column and just *look at them* — not as data, as a list, the way you'd look at a bus timetable — you can see it with your eyes, and I want to be clear that no statistics were harmed in the making of this observation, because that's exactly why I trust it.

They come in bunches.

1931. Then nothing for eighteen years. Then 1949, 1950, 1951, 1952. Then nothing much for nearly twenty. Then 1970, 1971, 1972. Quiet. Then 1988, 1989. Then 1994, 1995, 1996 — four of them in there.

And then, at the end, in the professor's late hand, the one that shakes: 2019. 2020. 2021. 2021. 2022. 2023. 2023.

Six years. Seven entries.

The gaps between the bunches: eighteen years, nineteen years, sixteen years, six years, and then the last gap isn't a gap, because the last bunch never stopped.

I did the only respectable thing available, which was to check whether I'd made the gaps by looking for them. I gave the years to Noor with no explanation and asked her what she saw, and she came back in half an hour with the same five bunches and one extra she thought might be a bunch and I don't. Then I went and found a man on a statistics forum, told him it was cinema release dates, and asked him whether a list like that was consistent with things happening at random.

He said: *possible but not likely, and your last six years are doing something the rest isn't.*

Then he asked what the films were and I said thank you and logged off, and I am aware of how that behaviour looks written down.

---

So here is the finding, stated as flatly as I can, because I have learned that flat is the only voice worth having:

**Whoever has been doing this does not do it steadily. They do it in bursts. The bursts used to be a generation apart and now they are not, and the current one has been running for six years and hasn't stopped.**

And then, underneath, the part I could not make go away, and could not prove either.

Look at when the bunches are. 1949 to 1952. 1970 to 1972. 1994 to 1996. If you know the century at all, you know what those years feel like. They aren't the bad years. They aren't wars or crashes. They're the good bits — the long exhale after, the years people put in the adverts, the decades everyone is nostalgic for.

The edits don't come when things fall apart.

They come when things get comfortable.

I told that to Buddy on the kitchen floor at half one in the morning, because there was nobody else in the house to tell, and I heard myself say it, and it's the only sentence from that whole winter I'd defend in front of anybody:

"They're not tidying up after something. They're getting ahead of something."

He put his chin on my ankle. It's not agreement. It's just what he does.

---

Two more things happened that month and I'll do them quickly.

The first is that I have to write down, here, at the point where I understood it, a question I could not answer then and could not answer for another year, and if I skip it I'm cheating you.

I had a hundred years of a lineage that appears to know how this goes. I had them editing *before* the bad part rather than after. I had them sitting on the boards of people who lease deep sites with good geology.

And there are exactly two readings of that, and my data cannot tell them apart.

**One:** they forecast. They're very good at it, they've been doing it for a long time, and everything else follows — you scrub the record because a frightened population is bad for asset values, and you sign the lease early because you can read a curve.

**Two:** they don't only forecast.

I want it understood that I did not know. For most of that year I leaned hard on the first one, because the first one is smaller, and because a man alone in a kitchen should always take the smaller version. Anyone who tells you they can tell a forecast from a cause with forty data points and a spreadsheet is selling you something, and by then I'd had enough people sell me things.

The second thing was a man called Sander who arrived at work in a very good suit.

He was an external reviewer, brought in after the pilot, and he had a laptop and a list of questions and a manner like a dentist's waiting room. He wanted to know who had been making non-standard changes to detection thresholds on the pilot instance.

I said I'd changed one, in a sandbox, to see what it did.

He wrote that down. He asked why. I told him the truth, which is that I wanted to know what the tool had been told to ignore. He wrote that down too, and asked whether I'd documented it, and I said no, and he said that was the actual finding, and I realised he'd been sent to look for undocumented changes and not for me, and that he was bored, and that he'd be gone in a fortnight.

He was. He was a perfectly nice man doing a dull job, and for about nine days I was certain he was going to end my life, and I have kept that, because of what happens later, and because of who I was not looking at while I was looking at him.

Frank walked past my desk after the interview and said, "Sander, yeah? He did us in Rotterdam last year. Absolute machine for a spreadsheet, no personality whatsoever, harmless." And then, "You all right, man? You look rough."

"Late nights."

"You should play more golf."

---

Last thing, and it's small, and it's the one I actually think about.

I did a did-you-know on the third tee that week. The retirees, the visor, the same four men since 1998. I gave them the calendar — that Ethiopia is running seven or eight years behind us, that it's not a mistake, that they simply never adopted a correction the rest of us adopted, and that there are people alive right now with two entirely legitimate answers to what year it is.

And nothing happened.

The one with the visor said, "Huh," and took his shot.

I have been performing that trick for twenty years. It has never once not worked. And I stood on that tee and understood, in about a second and a half, that the material hadn't changed — I had. It's a story if you tell it lightly. I hadn't told it lightly. Somewhere in the last two years I'd stopped doing did-you-knows and started doing *briefings*, and the difference is audible to everybody except the man doing it.

I got in the cart with the dog and we played the front nine and I didn't say much.
