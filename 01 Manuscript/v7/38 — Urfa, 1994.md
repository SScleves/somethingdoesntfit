# Urfa, 1994

The hill had been looked at before. That is the first thing to say, because it is the thing that matters.

In 1963 a joint survey from Istanbul and Chicago had walked the plateau north-east of Urfa, in the far south-east of Turkey, near the Syrian border, cataloguing anything that looked old. They noted a low mound with broken limestone slabs on top, and flint on the surface, and a stand of mulberry trees that the local farmers treated as holy, and they wrote it down as a likely medieval cemetery, and they moved on. The slabs were taken for gravestones. The hill was given a line in a report. The report was filed.

The farmers called it Göbekli Tepe. Potbelly Hill. They had been ploughing around the stones for generations and moving the ones that got in the way, and nobody had asked them, and they had not thought to say.

Thirty-one years later a German archaeologist called Klaus Schmidt, who was forty and had been working at a site forty kilometres away and was looking for somewhere of his own, read the 1963 report. He read the line about the hill. He read *medieval cemetery* and *limestone slabs* and he thought, later, by his own account, that it did not fit: there was no reason for a cemetery on a bare hilltop with no water, and the flint, by the survey's own description, was wrong for the Middle Ages by about ten thousand years.

So in October 1994 he drove out and climbed it.

---

What he saw from the top he described many times afterwards, and the description did not change, which in an archaeologist is a kind of honesty. The slabs were not gravestones. They were the tops of pillars, T-shaped, limestone, set in rings, mostly still buried, and the whole hill, as far as he could tell from standing on it, was not a hill at all. It was a mound of what people had put there. Fifteen metres of it. Deliberately.

He said he knew within a minute. He said, in the version he gave most often, that he had a choice on that hilltop, which was to walk away and tell nobody, or to spend the rest of his life there, and that there was no third option, and that he chose the second, and that he was right about the rest of his life, because he died in 2014, at sixty, swimming, with the site barely a tenth dug.

The first excavation season was 1995. The dates came back from the laboratory and were sent again because nobody believed them. The pillars had been raised around nine and a half thousand years before Christ. Before pottery. Before farming. Before the wheel, before writing, before any of the things that the textbooks said had to come first, because you cannot build on this scale without a surplus and you cannot have a surplus without agriculture and you cannot have agriculture without settling down. That was the order. Everyone knew the order. Schmidt's pillars were seven thousand years older than Stonehenge and six thousand older than the first cities, and they had been carved, with reliefs of foxes and boars and vultures and scorpions and a headless man, by people who were supposed to be living in caves and following herds.

He put it in a sentence that got him into trouble and is now in the guidebooks. *First came the temple, then the city.* Not the other way round. People had gathered to build this, and the gathering had required feeding, and the feeding had, perhaps, taught them to farm. The thing that the whole story of civilisation said was the effect might have been the cause.

And then, at some point around eight thousand years before Christ, the people who had built it buried it. On purpose. Carefully. They filled the enclosures with rubble and bone and flint and earth, ring by ring, and they walked away, and the hill grew mulberry trees, and farmers ploughed around the stones, and a survey wrote *medieval cemetery*, and the file was closed for thirty-one years.

---

That is the record, and it is enough. But there is one more thing on the record and it belongs here.

Nobody hid Göbekli Tepe. There was no conspiracy. A survey team in 1963 looked at a hill and saw what they expected to see, because the thing they were looking at was not possible, and when you are standing in front of something that is not possible the human eye is very good at finding the possible thing nearby and settling on that. Gravestones. Medieval. Move on. The report was honest. The men who wrote it were competent. They were simply looking at a thing from the wrong side of a sentence that everyone knew was true, and the sentence did the rest.

It took one man, reading the middle of an old report instead of the summary, to notice that the flint was wrong.

---

Fifteen kilometres from that hill, in the autumn of 1929, an Englishman with one book to his name walked out of Urfa on foot, heading east, with a notebook, and did not come back. He was last seen on the road. He had spent a year in the region. He had, in his one book, said that the traditions of the first settled peoples appeared to have been transmitted to them, and two years after he disappeared somebody changed that to *arisen among*.

There is nothing in any archive that puts H. M. Tresidder on Potbelly Hill. The farmers who might have remembered an Englishman asking about the stones were dead by 1994. The 1963 survey did not record any earlier visitor. This book will not tell you he found it, because it does not know, and neither did the professor, and neither does James, and he has looked.

What is known is where he walked out of. And what is known is what was fifteen kilometres from the road, under the mulberry trees, in 1929, where it had been for eleven thousand years, waiting for somebody to read the flint.
