# The drill

My first week on the continuity programme was a bus.

I'd been moved on a Monday. Pieter had said it in his nice voice, three weeks after the stand-up, the way you'd tell someone they'd been upgraded: *they asked for the person who reads things.* A woman called Saskia had walked me through a lobby telling me to notice everything, and I'd noticed that she said it like a line she'd been given, and I'd noticed that my badge, when I touched it to the reader on the new floor, made a different sound. Higher. One note up. I don't know who tunes those things, but somebody does, and somebody had decided I was a semitone more important than I'd been on Friday.

Continuity is the department that plans for the building not being there. Floods, fire, the thing nobody says out loud. If the bank can't open its doors on a Tuesday, continuity knows where everyone sits on Wednesday. It's a department made entirely of lists, which is why they'd want a man who reads lists, and I'd have believed that, actually, if I hadn't been on one.

On the Thursday there was a test. There's one every year. Forty named staff, chosen in advance, get on a bus at seven in the morning and are driven to the recovery site, and they sit at desks that have their names on them, and they log in, and they pretend the building has burned down, and at four o'clock they get back on the bus. It's called a drill. The word made me think of dentists, and of the thing you do to a wall, and I was on the list.

Number forty. Last name on the sheet. Pieter was twelve.

---

The bus went east, out past the ring road and into the flat country where the business parks are, the kind of landscape that's nothing but sheds with company names on them, each one the size of a village and containing, as far as anyone can tell, three cars. The recovery site was one of these. Low, long, grey, behind a car park with a sign at the entrance that said the name of a foundation I'd never heard of and, underneath, smaller, the name of a company I had, because it was on the lanyard of a man I'd played eighteen holes with in October.

I looked at that for a while, through the bus window, while the driver found the gate.

It didn't mean anything. I want to be clear about what I thought at the time, which was: of course. He sells the bank its recovery seats. This is the recovery site. His company's name is on the sign. That's not a coincidence; that's a supply chain. I'd have been more surprised if it hadn't been there. I noted it the way you note the make of a car, and I got off the bus with the other thirty-nine and collected a lanyard of my own, a temporary one, white, with my name printed on it and a number.

Forty.

They gave us a sandwich. At eight in the morning. I've never understood that about these things, the sandwich at eight; I think it's less about hunger than about giving people something to hold.

---

Inside, it was an office. That's the thing nobody tells you about the end of the world: it has carpet tiles.

A big open floor, a hundred desks maybe, in rows, each with a screen and a phone and a laminated card with a name on it. Half the names were ours. The other half were other banks, other companies, other people's Tuesdays. Everyone shares the site; that's how the economics work. You don't buy a building for a fire that might never come. You buy a seat in a building somebody else owns, and you hope that when your fire comes, theirs hasn't.

My desk was at the back by the window. Desk forty. My name was on the card, spelt right, with the vowel, which almost nobody manages. I sat down and logged in and the screen came up exactly as it would at work, same wallpaper, same six things I'd left open on Friday, and for a disorienting second I was at work, and then I looked up and saw forty people I half knew sitting in rows under strip lights eating sandwiches, and I was somewhere else.

Pieter came past. He was twelve, which put him in the second row, and he'd walked the length of the floor to find me, which he didn't need to do.

"Settling in?"

"It's a desk."

"It is a desk." He looked at the window. "Good spot, actually. You can see the car park." He said it as if the car park were a view, and then he said, in exactly the same voice, "How's the programme treating you?"

"Lots of lists."

"You like lists."

"I do."

He nodded, and stood there a moment longer than the conversation needed, and I had the feeling, which I'd had with him twice before, of a man wanting to say something and finding the sentence isn't in the building. Then someone called him, and he went.

I ate the sandwich. I did the drill. You log in, you open the things you'd open, you ring a number and tell a voice that you're operational, and the voice says thank you and you hear it typing. By eleven I'd done everything the laminated card asked of me and I had five hours left, and a window, and I'm not good with five hours.

So I read the building.

---

I'd like to say I was looking for something. I wasn't. I was doing the thing I do in hotels and other people's offices and, Ana says, other people's bathrooms, which is read every piece of paper in reach, because paper is where people put the things they've stopped noticing.

The fire plan by the door. The evacuation routes. The floor map, laminated, with our floor in blue and a second floor above us in grey, labelled, in a smaller font, ARCHIVE (FOUNDATION) — NO ACCESS. The cleaning rota. A notice about the car park gate. A list of extensions, and at the bottom of the list, one that said *Reading Room*, with a number that was the same as ours except for the first digit.

The floor map was the thing. Not because of what it said; because of what it was next to. There was a staircase at the far end of our floor, behind the kitchenette, with a glass door at the top, and through the glass door, from where I sat, I could see a landing, and a wall, and on the wall a sign in the same font as everything else that said the same two words, and under them, in the way these places do, a shelfmark. Not a room number. A shelfmark, the kind libraries use, with the letters and the dash and the numbers.

I wrote it down. On the back of the laminated card, with the pen they'd given us. I didn't know why. I know now that I was writing down a format, not a number, the way you'd write down a licence-plate style from a country you've never been to, in case you see it again.

Then I got up and went to look.

---

The glass door didn't open. My badge made no sound at all against the reader, which is a sound in itself, and I stood on the landing like a man who's wandered into the wrong part of a hospital and looked through.

A corridor. Carpet, better than ours. A door at the end with a window in it, and through the window, a room with a long table and a lamp and shelves, and a desk at the near end with a woman at it, with her back to me, doing something with a card index. An actual card index, wooden, with drawers. I hadn't seen one since I was a child.

She didn't turn round. I stood there for maybe ten seconds. There was a smell, even through the glass, or I imagined one: paper and dust and the specific cold of rooms that are kept at a temperature for the sake of what's in them and not who.

A reading room. The foundation's archive. On top of a bank's recovery site, in a shed in a business park, behind a glass door that didn't answer to the badge of a man whose name was on a laminated card forty desks away.

I went back down. I sat at desk forty and looked at the car park, which Pieter had called a view.

And there, in the car park, in a good coat, talking to a man in a high-visibility jacket who I took to be the site manager, with one hand in his pocket and the other making the small open gesture of someone explaining something simple to someone who should already know it, was Julian.

He didn't look up. He had no reason to; he didn't know I was there, and from the car park the window would have been a sheet of grey. I watched him talk for a minute. The site manager nodded a lot. Then Julian clapped him on the arm, lightly, the way you do with someone whose name you've made a point of learning, and got into something German and quiet and drove away.

I thought: he sells the seats. This is where the seats are. Of course he's here.

I thought: he said he'd hear things before they happened.

I thought, and I want to be honest about this, that it would be nice to tell him I'd seen him. That I'd ring him, that evening, and say *I was at your shed today, I waved and you didn't see*, and he'd laugh, and we'd fix a round.

I didn't ring him. I'd like to tell you it was instinct. It was the shelfmark. I had a format written on the back of a card in my pocket, and I didn't know where I'd seen it before, and some part of me that is slower and better than the rest wanted to find out first.

---

The bus went back at four. Pieter sat near the front. I sat at the back with the laminated card in my pocket and watched the sheds go by, each one the size of a village, each one with a name, and I thought about a floor map with one floor in blue and one in grey, and about the fact that in all the years the bank had been buying seats in that building, nobody on our side had ever asked what was upstairs, because why would you. It's an archive. It says so on the map.

At home, Buddy was at the window.

Ana said he'd been there since three. "He gets up when a car slows down. Any car. Then he lies back down. He's done it four times." She watched me take my coat off. "How was the end of the world?"

"It had a sandwich."

"Of course it did."

Later, in the kitchen, with the dogs asleep and Ana upstairs, I took the laminated card out of my pocket and turned it over and looked at the shelfmark I'd copied off a wall. Letters, a dash, numbers. A format.

I'd seen it. I knew I'd seen it. Not in a library. On a page.

It took me until the Saturday to remember where, and when I did I sat down on the kitchen floor the way I do, and I took the thin book out of the folder, and I turned to the last page, where a dead man had written a library reference, and a date, and the word *Ask*, and I put the card next to it.

Same letters. Same dash. Same shape.

The appointment the professor hadn't kept was upstairs from desk forty.
