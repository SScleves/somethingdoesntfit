# A market, Sunday

I found him with a putter, which Ana says is the most James thing I've ever done, and she's right, and I'd like it on the record that it was Beard Man's idea.

Not the putter. The method. I'd been standing at his stall on a Sunday three weeks after the vote, with Buddy in the trolley Ana had bought him because he couldn't do the market on foot any more, and Beard Man had been mid-sentence, as always, about something, I think the Templars, and he'd said, apropos of nothing, "You know how I find a book, James? Not by the title. Anyone can change a title. By the *binding*. A man can change his name but he can't change what he loves," and he'd gone straight back to the Templars, and I'd stood there with my hand on the dog and thought: copper.

Julian Kalder played Miura irons. Copper-plated, hand-forged in Japan, the kind of thing you buy once, with a putter by a man called Olson who makes forty a year, and seven wedges, which nobody needs, which he'd told me about on a tee box in April with the embarrassment of a man confessing to a mistress. I'd looked at the bag that day the way I look at everything, completely, and I'd filed it under *rich man's toys*, and I'd been wrong, because it wasn't a toy. It was the only thing he'd ever said about himself that wasn't for me.

There are maybe forty bags like that in Europe. The people who make clubs like that know where every set goes, because they're proud, and because they re-grip them, and because men who own them can't stop talking about them in the three or four places on the internet where such men gather. A set of copper Miuras, an Olson, seven wedges. Three places in the country fit them; two in Belgium; one in the north of Germany. You don't need an organisation. You need a Sunday afternoon and a man who reads the middle of golf forums.

I didn't do the finding. I want to be accurate. I gave Anneke the shape of it, in a two-line email, the way I'd given the auction house a two-line email a year ago, and Anneke gave it to people who, as she put it, *had resources*, and the resources did what resources do, and that was that. The organisation found him. It just needed to be told what to look for, and nobody had thought of it, because nobody in the organisation had ever played eighteen holes with him and watched his face when he talked about his wedges.

---

It was a market in a town in the north of Germany, over the border, a Sunday, cold. Anneke's people had been there since nine. I wasn't; I was in my own kitchen with Ana and the dogs and a phone on the table, face up for once, and at ten past eleven it buzzed, once, with a message from a number I didn't know that said *now*, and I rang the number he'd put in my phone on a thirteenth tee while I was looking for a ball.

He answered on the fourth ring. I could hear the market behind him. Someone selling cheese.

"James."

"Julian."

A pause. I could hear him turn, I think; the cheese got quieter. I imagine him seeing the first one then, a man he knew by code, by a stall, not looking at him. And turning, and seeing the second.

"Ah," he said.

"Part of the deal was you gone. You'll have heard. I didn't get that part. I got five other things and I took them, and I thought that was the end of it, and then I went to the market with my dog in a trolley and a man told me you can't change what you love."

"The clubs."

"The clubs."

He laughed. He actually laughed, Julian Kalder, surrounded in a market in Germany, and it was the real one, the one from the back nine. "Oh, that's good. That's very good, James. Forty bags in the world. I used to say that to people. I said it to *you*."

"You did."

"And you remembered." The cheese had gone completely. I heard footsteps on cobbles that weren't his. "That's the thing I never got right about you, you know. I had you as a man who notices. Everyone had you as a man who notices; it's on your file, it's practically your name. Notices small things, asks the dull question, reads the middle. And I thought, fine, I'll be the one thing he doesn't notice, I'll be so completely what he wants that he won't look. And it worked. For months. You sat at my table. You let me into your house."

"I did."

"So what was it? Not the clubs. Before. When did you know?"

I thought about Deventer, by a fence, with Frank telling the gorilla-mask story. I thought about a pub and a word from a margin. I thought about a dog in a doorway for four hours.

"Buddy wouldn't go near you," I said.

Silence on the line.

"Everyone goes near Buddy. Everyone. He's the friendliest animal that's ever lived. He's pressed his head against the leg of every plumber in the province. And he stood in a doorway and watched you for a whole evening and didn't come out. I noticed that. I filed it under nothing. It sat in the file for months, next to Deventer and the word in the pub, and I didn't look at it, because I liked you, Julian. I liked you more than I've liked anyone in years. And then you came to the gate at night with a water bowl."

"James—"

"You shouldn't have touched my dog."

The footsteps on the cobbles had stopped. I imagine them around him now, four or five, at a polite distance, not looking at him, in good coats.

"No," Julian said. "No. I shouldn't." And then, in a different voice, the one from the end of the corporate day, in a car park, with his keys in his hand: "Was he all right? Afterwards? You never said."

"He walked onto a green last Monday. Four steps. In the frost."

"Good." He meant it. That's the thing I've never been able to make anyone understand about him, and I've stopped trying. "That's good, James."

"Is it true? What you told the long table? That you crossed. Nine years ago. In Leiden."

"Yes."

"And you took the seat."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He was quiet for a long time. The market noise had changed; it had the shape of people stepping back. When he spoke it was almost gentle.

"Because I could see it," he said. "All of it. The whole machine. I came out of that room in Leiden and for the first time in my life nothing frightened me, and I could see how it all worked, and it was *beautiful*, James. It's the most beautiful thing anyone's ever built. And they offered me a place in it. And I thought: a man who isn't afraid, and can see the machine, and has a place in it. What on earth else would he do?" A breath. "You'd have done the same."

"No," I said. "I wouldn't. That's the bit you got wrong about me. It's the only bit."

"Perhaps." I heard him turn again. "They're very close now. I should go. I'd like to say one thing first, and I'd like you to believe it, because it's the only true thing I ever told you without being paid to."

"Go on."

"I liked you. All of it was the job, the golf, the barbecue, your wife, the dog. All of it. And I liked you anyway. That wasn't in the file. That was the thing *I* didn't notice." He half-laughed. "Nobody counts that column either."

"No."

"Tell Ana I'm sorry about the dinner. She did very well. Her mother's pills. I worked it out on the drive home and I was impressed; I'd have put money on you and it was her. Tell her that." A pause. "And James."

"Yes."

"Leave it. Whatever you're thinking of doing next. Don't. You've won. You won't believe me, but you have. Go home."

He hung up.

---

I sat at my kitchen table with the phone in my hand and Ana across from me and the dogs on the floor, and I didn't know what had happened to him, and I don't know now, and I've decided, the way Ana decided about the van, not to.

I know the clubs turned up. That's the one thing I know. Six weeks later a bag arrived at the house by courier, no note, no sender: copper Miura irons, an Olson putter, seven wedges, re-gripped, cleaned, the way you'd return something borrowed. Ana wanted to burn them. I put them in the garage. They're still there. I've never hit one. I don't know whether they were sent by Anneke's people as a message, or by him, as a joke, or by somebody I'll never meet as a receipt.

I know that nobody has ever been found. I know that *found* is a word with a column, and that somewhere in a visitor book there's a line for him too, in Aylin's hand, with a word in it, and that I've never asked her what it says.

And I know that Beard Man was right, about the binding, and that it's the only thing anyone in this whole story got exactly right, and that he said it about Templars and went straight back to them, and has no idea.
