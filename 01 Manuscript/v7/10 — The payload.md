# The payload

There's a thing engineers do when someone tells them to leave something alone, and it isn't disobedience. I want to be precise about this, because Pieter would call it disobedience and he'd be wrong, and the difference is the only defence I've got.

If a job runs on your systems and you don't know what it does, you don't open it. Opening it is a decision. You don't stop it, you don't change it, you don't touch it. You stand next to it while it runs and you watch what comes out. That's not interfering. That's the job description. I've done it a thousand times to things I was allowed to look at and nobody ever called it anything but Tuesday.

So on the Thursday night I set an alarm for ten past two, and I went downstairs in my socks, and I opened my laptop on the kitchen table where the two copies of page 118 still lived in their folder, and I waited for fourteen minutes past.

Buddy came and lay across my feet. He does that when I'm up at the wrong time. He doesn't ask why. He just makes sure that if I get up to do something stupid, I'll have to move a dog first.

---

I'm going to describe what I did in a way that will satisfy nobody. The technical people will find it too vague and everyone else will find it too much. Fine. The honest version is: I watched the door the job sends things through, and when it sent something, I kept a copy. That's it. I didn't break anything. I didn't log in as anyone I'm not. The data went where it always goes. It also went into a file on my laptop called, I'm embarrassed to say, `nothing.txt`, because if Ana came down for water I wanted the screen to tell the truth.

02:14:00. Nine seconds. Forty-one kilobytes, which is nothing. A photo of the dogs is a hundred times that. Forty-one kilobytes is a shopping list. It's an email without an attachment. It's the amount of text a person could read in the time it takes to make coffee.

I opened it expecting noise. Diagnostics look like noise. Timestamps, hexadecimal, the digestive sounds of a system talking to itself. Pieter had said *diagnostic artefact* and some part of me, the part that has spent thirty years losing arguments with paperwork, expected to open the file and find exactly that and go back to bed feeling like the fool, which is a feeling I've made my peace with.

It wasn't noise.

It was a list.

One line per entry, one hundred and twelve lines. Each line was an employee ID, the eight-digit number we all have on the back of our badges, and then a tab, and then two words.

Not a sentence. Two words. Lowercase. Like a note someone writes to themselves.

`40217733	posted (forum)`
`40193058	wrote (dealer)`
`40221904	asked (hr)`
`40188120	searched (archive)`
`40209417	posted (forum)`

I sat there with Buddy on my feet and read all hundred and twelve, and then I read them again, and the second time I wasn't reading the IDs. I was reading the verbs.

Posted. Wrote. Asked. Searched. Requested. Enquired. Raised.

Every one of them was a thing a person does with words, to someone else, on purpose. Nobody on the list had *thought* anything. You can't put a thought in a file. Every line was a moment when somebody in my company had stopped, and typed, or picked up a pen, or opened their mouth in a meeting, and put a question somewhere it could be read.

And the thing in brackets was where.

---

I'd like to tell you I understood it then. I didn't. I want that on the record, because the version of this story where I'm clever is the version where you shouldn't trust me. What I did was the thing I do, which is stupider and slower and the only reason I'm alive: I checked whether I was on it.

My number is 40176502. I know it the way you know your own phone number, because I've typed it into a hundred forms. I searched the file.

Nothing.

I felt two things at once, and I've thought about which came first and I can't tell you. One was relief, the ordinary animal kind. The other was something that took me a day to name, and the name is disappointment. A hundred and twelve people in my building had done something that got them put on a list that went somewhere nobody could find at fourteen minutes past two in the morning, and I'd been reading old books on a kitchen floor for a month and asking questions in my own head, and I hadn't made the cut. Whatever this was, it wasn't about me.

That was Thursday.

---

I need to tell you what else I'd done that week, because the order matters and I almost missed it.

Tuesday was the stand-up. Tuesday I'd said, out loud, in a glass box, in front of Pieter and Frank and six other people, *there's a job on the list with no owner, I want to find out what it is.* And Pieter had said leave it, and I'd left it, out loud, and gone back to my desk.

Thursday night I captured the file. A hundred and twelve lines.

Friday night I captured it again. I wasn't going to. I'd told myself Thursday was enough, that I had the shape of it and the shape was somebody else's problem. But I woke at two anyway, because the body learns a time faster than it learns a lesson, and Buddy was already at the bottom of the stairs looking up at me like I was late.

02:14:00. Nine seconds. Forty-one kilobytes.

A hundred and thirteen lines.

I didn't search for my number. I didn't have to. New entries go at the bottom; it's a list, and whoever wrote the thing that writes it was tidy. I scrolled down past the hundred and twelve I'd read twice the night before, and the last line was:

`40176502	asked (job)`

---

I'm going to try to describe the next few minutes accurately, which means boringly.

I didn't panic. I didn't feel watched, not yet; that came later and in a different room. What I felt, sitting at my kitchen table at two in the morning with my own badge number on a list I wasn't supposed to have, was the specific, cold, almost pleasant feeling I get at work when a system finally tells me what it is.

Because it had. In two words. *Asked (job).*

The list wasn't a list of people who'd done something wrong. Nobody on it had broken a rule. It was a list of people who had *asked*. Posted a question on a forum. Written to a dealer. Enquired at HR. Searched an archive. Raised a thing in a meeting. The job didn't count what you did or what you knew or what you thought at four in the morning on a kitchen floor. It counted whether you'd turned it into words and handed the words to someone.

I'd been on this for a month and I hadn't been on the list, because for a month I'd kept it in my head and in my kitchen and in a folder in a bag. And then on Tuesday I'd said eleven words in a meeting, and on Friday I was on the list, with a reason beside my name that was exactly, precisely true.

You don't need to read minds. People write.

I looked at the bracket. *(job).* Not *(forum)*, not *(dealer)*. Someone, or something, had a category for people who asked about the thing itself. I was the only one in it.

---

Buddy had gone back to sleep across my feet. Mora was upstairs on the bed, in my place, which she takes the moment I leave it and defends on my return like a tenant with rights.

I sat there and I did what I do, which is think about the plumbing, because the plumbing is the only part of any mystery I've ever been qualified to look at.

Whatever made the list ran inside our systems. It had to; it was reading our forums and our HR tickets and our stand-up notes, or someone's notes of the stand-up, which was a thought I put down carefully and did not pick up again that night. It ran at the same time in every office. It had been in the backups for as long as there had been backups. And it sent its hundred-odd lines, every night, through a door that opened for nine seconds to somewhere that didn't exist, and closed.

Someone had put it there. Someone paid for it to stay. And someone, somewhere, at fourteen minutes past two, received a list of names every night and knew, by the next morning, who in a four-hundred-year-old bank had asked a question.

There's a story I'd read years ago and filed under things that happen to other people. A technician in San Francisco, an ordinary one, the kind of man who reads the wiring diagrams because somebody has to, found a locked room on the sixth floor of his own building where the internet was being copied through a splitter to a government agency. He found it because he read the documents nobody read. I'd always liked that story. I'd liked it the way you like stories about people who are a bit like you, from a safe distance. I didn't like it that night. That night I thought about what happened to him after, which was lawyers and a long time of being called a crank, and then being right, which as far as I can tell didn't help.

I closed the laptop. I didn't delete `nothing.txt`. I renamed it, which is the kind of brave I am.

---

Here is what I understood by the time I went back upstairs, and here is what I didn't, because I want both on the record.

I understood that asking had a cost, and that the cost was being counted, and that I'd paid it on Tuesday without knowing the price. I understood that the hundred and twelve other people on that list had each paid it too, for a forum post or a letter or a question to HR, and that most of them had no idea they were on anything. I understood that Pieter had looked at me across a glass box with a face the size of a man remembering an appointment and said *leave it*, and that whatever he knew, he knew at least that much: that there was something to leave.

What I didn't understand was what the list was *for*. A list of people who ask is a strange thing to want. It's not a list of thieves or leakers or people who'd fail a background check. It's a list of the curious. And a hundred and twelve curious people in one bank, counted nightly, sent nowhere, is either the most pointless piece of software ever written or it's a piece of something much bigger, the way one gear is a piece of a clock.

I got into bed. Mora moved over by one dog's width, which is her maximum concession. Ana was asleep on her side with her hand under her cheek, and I lay there in the dark next to her and didn't tell her, and I knew already that not telling her was going to be a bigger problem than the list.

The alarm was still set for ten past two. I didn't turn it off.

I'd stopped asking out loud. I want to be clear that I hadn't stopped asking.
