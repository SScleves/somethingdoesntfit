# Groningen

The letter had been on the fridge for three weeks under a magnet shaped like a lemon, which is where things go in our house to be dealt with by someone else.

It was from a dealer. Antiquarian books, a shop in Groningen I'd never been to, a name I'd seen on the inside cover of two of the professor's books in pencil, the way dealers sign their work. It was a nice letter. It said that the writer understood I had acquired the late professor's library, that he had supplied the professor for many years, that he would be glad to be of service should I wish to add to the collection or to part with any of it, and that in his experience it was always best, in these matters, to be plain about one's reasons.

Ana had read it over my shoulder when it came. "That's what careful sounds like in a shop," she said, and went back to her programme, and I'd stood there with the letter thinking that it was the first time I'd seen my own name written down by someone connected to the book, and that I didn't like it, and that I couldn't have told you why.

Three weeks is how long it took me to understand why. Three weeks, and a list with my badge number on it, and the job gone from every backup, and Frank honestly not remembering the stand-up. By then I knew exactly what *be plain about one's reasons* meant. It meant that somebody had already been plain about mine.

So on the Saturday I told Ana I was going to look at a bookshop, which was true, and she said take Buddy, he likes the car, which was also true, and I drove two hours north with an old dog asleep on the back seat and the letter on the passenger seat under my phone, like a ticket.

---

Groningen is a nice town and the shop was in the nice part of it, down a street with bicycles leaning on everything, and it was exactly what you want a shop like that to be: narrow, deep, smelling of paper and radiator, a bell on the door that had clearly been chosen for its sound. I left Buddy in the car with the window down. He gave me the look he gives me when I leave him anywhere, which is not reproach, exactly. It's more that he's noting it.

The dealer was in the back. He was older than I'd expected and smaller, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and a cardigan that had been expensive once, and he looked up when the bell went and saw me and his face did something I've only seen once before, on Pieter, in a glass box.

He knew who I was. That was the first thing. I'd never sent a photograph; I'd never replied to the letter. He knew.

The second thing was worse. He wasn't surprised to see me. He was *relieved*. The way you're relieved when the dentist finally calls your name.

"Mr Klippe," he said. He said it properly, with the right vowel, which almost nobody does. "I wondered when."

"You wrote to me."

"I did." He took the glasses down and folded them and put them in his cardigan pocket, and he looked past me at the door, and then at me. "May I ask," he said, "who sent you?"

---

I need to slow down here, because this is where I got it wrong, and the way I got it wrong is the only reason the rest of the afternoon happened.

I thought he was asking who'd referred me. A dealer's question. *Who gave you my name.* So I said, "Nobody. You wrote to me. I came."

And he nodded slowly, the way you nod when someone's answered a different question from the one you asked, and he said, "Of course," and he went and turned the sign on the door to closed and came back, and he said, "You'd better sit down, then. I'll tell you what I told them. It's the same."

*Them.*

I sat down. There was a chair by the desk, a customer's chair, and I sat in it, and he sat in his, and between us on the desk was a ledger, an actual ledger, the kind with a cloth spine, and he put his hand flat on it while he talked, the way you'd steady a boat.

He'd sold the professor the 1931 printing. Years ago. Eleven, twelve. A dry book nobody wanted, from a lot he'd bought at a house clearance; he remembered it because the professor had been so pleased, and the professor was not a man who showed pleasure. A few weeks after, two people had come to the shop. Polite people. A man and a woman, well dressed, with a card from a foundation that did something with archives. They'd been interested in the professor's purchase. They'd asked, very reasonably, whether the shop might let them know if anyone else ever asked for that title. Not the book, you understand; they had the book. The *title*. They'd left a number. They had not threatened him. He wanted me to understand that. They had said it was a matter of provenance, and that the foundation took an interest in certain editions for conservation reasons, and he had thought, at the time, that it was slightly odd and entirely harmless, and he had written the number inside the back cover of the ledger, and he had forgotten about it for eleven years.

"And then you wrote to me," he said.

"No," I said. "I didn't. I never replied."

"Not to me." He turned the ledger around. There was a line in it, in a neat old hand, three and a half weeks back. My name, the professor's name, and the title of the book. "You wrote to the auction house that handled his effects. You asked whether the professor's library had been catalogued. The auction house asked me, because I'd supplied him. And I," he said, and he took his hand off the ledger, "telephoned the number."

---

I'd done that. I'd forgotten I'd done that. A two-line email to an auction house in week two, before the list, before any of it, asking a question any buyer would ask. It had never occurred to me to count it.

Three and a half weeks ago. Before the ticket. Before the stand-up. Before my badge number appeared on a list at two in the morning with *asked (job)* beside it, somebody in a foundation had already received a phone call from a bookshop in Groningen saying that a man called Klippe was asking about the professor's books.

I'd thought the list was the beginning. The list was the second thing.

"What did they say," I said. "When you rang."

"Thank you." He looked at his hands. "A woman. She thanked me. She said it was very helpful, and that the foundation appreciated it, and that someone might be in touch with you about the collection. I took that to mean they'd make you an offer. That's what I'd have meant." He paused. "I wrote to you afterwards because I thought, if they do make an offer, he should know to be careful what he says about why he wants the books. Dealers get strange about provenance. That's all I thought it was. Strange about provenance."

"Be plain about one's reasons."

"Yes."

"And when I walked in just now," I said, "you asked who sent me."

He didn't answer that. He didn't need to. He'd thought I was from the foundation. He'd thought they'd come to ask him what else he knew, and he'd turned the sign on the door and sat down to tell them, because that's what you do when the polite people come back.

---

I want to tell you I was angry. I wasn't. I sat in a customer's chair in a bookshop in Groningen looking at a small old man in a good cardigan who had made one phone call in eleven years, and what I felt was something I've only felt at work, in the worst outages, when you find the line of config that did it and it was written by someone decent who was just doing what the ticket said.

So I did what I do at work. I told him what the thing he'd touched actually does.

I didn't tell him about the list. I'm not an idiot, and he was a stranger, and he had a number in the back of a ledger. I told him the shape of it. I said that there was a book, and the book had been changed between printings, and that the professor had noticed, and had died, in a chair, with an appointment in his diary that he didn't keep. I said that I had noticed too, and that I'd asked a question about it, once, at work, and that within a week the question had been erased and I'd been moved somewhere I could be watched. I said that the number in his ledger wasn't a conservation department. I said I didn't know what it was. I said that the woman who'd thanked him had, as far as I could tell, been thanking him for giving her my name before I'd even found anything, so that when I did find it, they'd be ready.

I said: "You didn't do anything wrong. I need you to hear that. You answered a question, because somebody asked it nicely and gave you a reason. That's all anybody on their side ever does. That's the whole trick."

He was quiet for a long time. The radiator ticked. Somewhere out on the street a bicycle bell went, twice.

Then he said, "He asked me once. The professor. Years after. Whether anyone had been in asking about him." He looked at the ledger. "I said no. Which was true, by then. They'd only come the once." He put his glasses back on, and took them off again. "I've thought about that answer more than I'd like, since March."

---

He gave me something before I left. I didn't ask for it. He went into the back and came out with a folder and took one sheet out of it and handed it to me: his copy of the receipt, from eleven years ago, for the 1931 printing. The professor's name. The date. The price, which was nothing. And in the corner, in the same neat hand, the two words he'd written on everything he sold that man, because the professor had asked him to, and he'd never asked why.

*First impression.*

"He always wanted to know which one," the dealer said. "Even when there was only one. I thought it was an affectation." He almost smiled. "I'm a bookseller. I thought it was a *nice* affectation."

I folded the receipt into the folder with page 118 and I thanked him, and I meant it, and I asked him not to ring the number again, and he said, "No. No, I don't suppose I will," in the voice of a man who has just understood what a sign on a door is for.

---

Buddy was asleep in the back with his chin on the armrest. I sat in the car for a while before I started it.

Ana rang when I was on the ring road. I put her on the speaker.

"Did you buy anything?"

"No."

"You went to a bookshop two hours away and bought nothing."

"He didn't have what I was looking for."

"What were you looking for?"

And I had it right there, the thing I do, the charming deflection, *a first impression of something boring, you'd hate it*, and the persona had it loaded, and I didn't say it. I don't know why. I think it was the receipt. I think it was a dead man asking a bookseller to write two words on everything, for years, because he wanted to know which one, and never once telling him why.

"Something I should have asked about sooner," I said. "I'll tell you tonight."

There was a pause on the line, and it wasn't a bad pause. It was the pause of a person who has heard a new sentence from someone she's known for fifteen years and is deciding what to do with it.

"Okay," she said. "Drive safe. Give Buddy a biscuit from me."

I gave Buddy a biscuit. He took it without waking up fully, which is a skill.

I drove home south in the dark with the folder on the seat beside me, thicker by one sheet of paper, and somewhere past Zwolle I passed a long straight stretch of canal road with the water black beside it, and I didn't know yet what had happened there, and I didn't slow down.
