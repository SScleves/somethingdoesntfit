# The night before

We left on Friday after dark with a Doberman in the back and eleven envelopes in the glovebox, and I want to say at the start that there was no chase. I've read the kind of book where there's a chase. This isn't one. This is a book about a man and his wife driving a thousand kilometres to put paper through letterboxes, and the most dramatic thing that happens is a van.

Ana drove. I'd like it on the record that she is a better driver than me in every measurable way and that I only ever drive because she lets me. She drove the way she does everything she's decided to do, which is completely, with both hands, and the radio off, and Mora's head between the seats breathing on the gearstick.

I had the list. Eleven names, eleven addresses, eleven envelopes, each with a sheet inside, each sheet the same shape as the three hundred before it except for the last section, which for the board said something different. Not *your name is not on this list.* Their names were on it; they were the board. For them it said: *On Monday you will be asked to confirm the trustees of a foundation. This is what the foundation charges your bank, per flagged person. This is who the foundation keeps. These are the names of your staff who are not kept. Please check.*

Maastricht first, because it was furthest. Three hours. We did it in silence, mostly, the good kind, with the motorway empty and the dog asleep by Eindhoven, and somewhere around Weert Ana said, without taking her eyes off the road, "If this works, what happens to him?"

"To who?"

"The old man."

I'd thought about that. "Nothing. He'll be retired. That's what boards do; they don't punish, they retire. He'll go on sitting in a reading room, I expect. Just not in charge of it."

"And Julian?"

I didn't answer for a while.

"Part of the deal," I said, "if there's a deal, is going to be him."

She nodded, as if I'd confirmed a thing she'd known, and drove.

---

Maastricht at half past ten. A tall house on a quiet street with a brass letterbox, and a light on upstairs. I got out with the envelope. I'd thought about knocking; we'd decided not to, because a man at the door at half past ten with a document is a thing people ring the police about, and a letter is a letter. But standing at the gate I saw the curtain move upstairs, and a face, a woman's, and she looked at me, and then at the car, with my wife at the wheel and a Doberman's head in the back window, and she made a decision I watched her make, and came down.

She opened the door on the chain. "Yes?"

"Mrs Hendriks. I work for the bank. I'm sorry about the hour. There's something you should read before Monday's meeting, and I didn't want it to go through the post." I held out the envelope. "You don't have to let me in. You don't have to say anything. I'd just like you to have read it."

She looked at the envelope. She looked at me.

"You're the one with the lists," she said.

I don't know what my face did. Something.

"Anneke rang me this afternoon." She unhooked the chain. "She rang four of us. The ones she trusts. She said a man might come with an envelope, and that I should read whatever was in it, and that she'd already read hers." She took the envelope. "Twice, she said. She'd read it twice. I've known Anneke de Wit for fifteen years and I've never once heard her say she read anything twice."

"Then you'll read it."

"I'll read it tonight." She looked past me at the car. "Is that your wife?"

"Yes."

"Tell her to come in. It's freezing. And the dog."

I said we had ten more to do. She nodded slowly, as if that confirmed something too, and said, "Then go," and shut the door, and I walked back to the car and got in and Ana said, "Well?" and I said, "Anneke's turned," and Ana put the car in gear and said, "Good," in a voice that meant she'd been more afraid than she'd let me see.

---

Two were already turned. That's what I mean when I say there was no chase: the thing was already happening without me, the way things do once you've put enough paper in enough hands. Anneke had rung four. One of the four, in The Hague, met us at the door at one in the morning in a dressing gown with the envelope already in her hand, because Anneke had scanned hers and sent it, and she gave it back to me and said, "I don't need two. Give it to someone who hasn't got one," which was the first time anyone in the whole of this had given me anything back, and I sat in the car afterwards and couldn't speak for a minute, and Ana let me.

One wasn't home. Zeeland, a farmhouse at the end of a dyke road, at three in the morning, dark, no car. We put the envelope through the door and Ana said, "If he's away till Monday that's one who votes blind," and I said I knew, and she said, "Ring Anneke," and I said it was three in the morning, and Ana said, "Ring Anneke," and I did, and Anneke answered on the second ring in a voice that had not been asleep, and said she'd handle Zeeland, and hung up, and Ana said, "I like her," and I said, "She approved the invoice thirty times," and Ana said, "And then she read it," and we drove on.

---

The van picked us up outside Rotterdam.

I'm going to describe this accurately, because I've thought about it since and I'm still not sure what it was. A white van, the kind with no windows in the back, that came onto the motorway behind us at a junction and stayed there. Not close. Three cars back, then two, then three again. For forty kilometres. Ana saw it before I did; she said, "There's a van," in the same voice she'd use for *there's a sign*, and I turned round and looked and Mora, who had been asleep, lifted her head and looked too, and did a thing I'd never seen her do, which was go completely silent and still, watching out of the back window, with her ears up.

"What do we do?"

"Nothing," I said. "We drive. We do the next one. If it's following us it'll follow us to a letterbox, and that's a fingerprint, and they don't do those."

"And if it's not following us?"

"Then it's a van."

It stayed with us for forty kilometres. At the Delft junction it indicated, and came off, and was gone, and Mora watched the empty road behind us for another minute before she lay down again, and I found I'd been holding the envelopes in my lap with both hands like a child with a bag of sweets.

"That was a van," Ana said.

"That was a van."

"I'm going to decide it was a van."

"That's the right decision."

We didn't talk about it again. I've since come to think that it was exactly what it looked like, which is a van, and that the forty kilometres were the longest of my life, and that both of those things are true, and that this is what it's like to live in the world they've made: you can never again see a van.

---

By eleven we'd done nine. Two in the city, done at dusk on the way out, through letterboxes; Maastricht; The Hague, three, one of them the dressing gown; Zeeland, the dark farmhouse; Breda, a man who opened the door himself and read the first page standing there in his socks and said "Fuck" very quietly and then "Thank you" and shut the door; Utrecht, through the door, no light. Nine. Two left, and they were both north, Groningen and a town near it, and between us and them was the depot.

"Midnight," I said.

"I know."

"You don't have to come in."

"I'm not coming in. I'm sitting in the car park with a Doberman and the engine running, and if you're not out by one I'm coming in with the Doberman." She took the exit for the business park. "That's not a negotiation."

The shed looked the way it had looked on the morning of the drill, low and long and grey behind its car park, with the foundation's name on the sign and the smaller name under it, and no lights in the forty-desk floor, and one light, upstairs, behind the glass door.

Aylin let me in at midnight exactly. She didn't say anything. She'd propped the outer door with a fire extinguisher, which was so unlike her that I understood it as a statement, and she led me through the dark floor, past desk forty, up the stairs, and her badge made no sound at all against the reader at the glass door, because it didn't need to: it was her door. Nobody had ever thought to make it answer to anyone else.

The reading room was lit by the one lamp on the long table. The tray was gone. The visitor book was on the table, open to the front, 1931, with a card index drawer beside it and, beside that, a flat scanner of the kind libraries use, already warm.

"Forty minutes," Aylin said. "He comes at one on Sundays. He doesn't sleep."

"The old man?"

"He likes to read at night." She sat down at her desk, with her back to me, and picked up a card, the way she had on the morning of the drill, and I understood that this was the deal: she would not watch, and she would not help, and she would not stop me. "Ninety-four years, Mr Klippe. One page a year, mostly. It's not a long book."

It wasn't. Ninety-four pages. I scanned every one. Name, date, shelfmark, outcome. *Deceased. Seated. Quiet. Declined. Quiet. Quiet. Seated.* The same four words, in a dozen hands, from 1931 to December, and in all ninety-four years I counted eleven *Seated*, and I counted *Deceased* until I stopped counting, and I did not read the names, because I had forty minutes and because I was afraid of what I'd recognise.

And under my feet, through the floor, the whole time, something hummed.

I'd heard it on the morning of the drill and taken it for air conditioning. It wasn't. It was lower than that, and steadier, the sound of a large thing kept running in a room below a room, and standing at the long table at half past midnight with a dead man's visitor book under a scanner I understood for the first time that the seats were not a list. They were a place. It was there. Under the carpet tiles and the laminated cards and desk forty, something was being kept warm for the day the payroll needed doing on a Wednesday, and I had sat on top of it eating a sandwich.

I finished at twenty to one. I put the book back exactly where it had been, open at the front, and I put the scanner's memory card in the envelope with the last two sheets, and I stood up, and Aylin, at her desk, with her back to me, said, "The last column."

"Yes."

"What does it say next to you? Now?"

I looked. December. My name. *Pending.*

"Still pending," I said.

"Good," said Aylin, and turned a card over. "I'd like to be the one who writes the next word. Whatever it is. I've earned that."

I went down through the dark floor and out past the fire extinguisher into the car park, and the car was there with its engine running and its lights off, and a Doberman stood up in the back as I crossed the tarmac and watched me all the way to the door, and Ana had the car moving before I'd closed it.

"Well?"

"Ninety-four years. Four words. I've got all of it."

"And the floor?"

"It hums."

She didn't ask what that meant. She drove north.

---

Groningen at five. A letterbox on the nice street, two doors from a bookshop with a bell on the door chosen for its sound. The last one was in a village beyond it, a house with a long drive and a light already on in the kitchen, because farmers get up, and I walked up the drive at six in the morning in the first grey with an envelope in my hand and a thousand kilometres behind me, and a man came to the door in a jumper with a mug of coffee, and looked at me, and looked at the envelope, and said, before I'd opened my mouth:

"You're the one Anneke said."

"Yes."

He took it. He didn't open it. He looked at the car, at the end of his drive, with its lights on in the grey and a shape in the back window with its ears up.

"That your wife driving?"

"All night."

"And a dog."

"And a dog."

He nodded. He had the face of a man who'd sat on a lot of boards and read a lot of envelopes and had stopped, some years ago, expecting any of them to contain anything.

"I'll read it," he said. "Before the meeting. I'll read it in the car." He looked at the envelope. "Anneke rang me at four this morning. Four. She's been on that board for four years and I've never known her ring anyone. She said: whatever he gives you, assume it's true, and then check it. She said that was the whole thing. Assume it's true, and then check."

"That's the whole thing."

"Then go home," he said. "You look like a man who needs to be told that by somebody. Go home. Sleep. We'll do our bit." And he went in, and I walked back down the drive in the grey, and got in the car, and Ana looked at me, and I said, "Twelve," and she put her head down on the steering wheel for a moment, just a moment, and then she lifted it and started the engine.

"Mum's," she said. "Buddy. Bed. And then tomorrow you go and hit a ball with a stick."

"Monday."

"Monday you hit a ball with a stick and keep your phone face down and you let twelve strangers decide what you've been doing for a year. That's the deal. That was always the deal." She pulled out onto the road. "You don't win this one, James. You've done the part you can do. You just have to not be in the room."

Mora lay down in the back with a sigh. The sun came up somewhere behind us, flat and pale over the flattest country on earth, and my wife drove us south, and I slept.
