# Monday

The sixth green at seven in the morning in December is the loneliest place I know, and I went there on purpose.

Nobody plays in December. The course is open because the course is always open, and the greenkeeper waves at you from the shed with the expression of a man who's seen your kind before, and you have the whole flat windy acreage to yourself, and the flags snap, and the grass is grey with frost until the sun gets at it. I'd rung ahead and asked if I could bring the dog in the cart, which is not allowed, and the woman in the shop had said, "Buddy? The old one? Go on," because they'd known him for years and because nobody was going to see.

He rode beside me in the cart with a blanket over him, with his chin on the edge, watching the fairways go by like a man revisiting a town he used to live in. He couldn't walk a hole any more. He hadn't been able to since the night at the gate. But he could ride, and he liked the air, and he liked, I think, being somewhere that smelled of nothing but grass and cold.

I put the phone face down on the bench by the sixth green. I'd promised Ana. Face down, on silent, and I'd look at it when I'd finished the hole, and not before.

Then I took the putter and a ball out onto the frost and started knocking it around, badly, the way I do, while twenty kilometres away twelve people walked into a room.

---

I'd like to tell you what happened in the room. I can't, because I wasn't there, and this book has a rule about that: I tell you what I saw. What I know I know from Anneke, from a phone call, and from Pieter, who heard it from someone who heard it from someone, which is how the bank hears everything, and the two versions agree, so I'll give you the shape.

The foundation's annual confirmation was item four on the agenda, after the quarterly numbers and a thing about the Antwerp office. It had been item four for eleven years. It took, usually, ninety seconds: the chair read the names of the trustees, somebody proposed, somebody seconded, hands went up, next item.

This year, when the chair read the names, a man from Breda said, "Before we vote," and put a sheet of paper on the table.

And then a woman from Maastricht put one next to it.

And then, Anneke said, there was a silence of the kind she'd only heard in a boardroom twice before, both times during the financial crisis, and in the silence nine more sheets came out of nine more folders and were laid on the table, not dramatically, just put down, the way you put down a card when everyone's already seen it. Eleven sheets. And the chair, who had not received one, because he was the twelfth and because I'd run out of night, looked at them, and looked at Anneke, who had been on the board for four years and had never once spoken before the vote.

She said: "I approved this invoice thirty times. I'd like to read it into the minutes." And she read the line. *Continuity reconciliation, recall index, per flagged person.* And she said the number of persons on the last invoice, and she said what it had been on the first, and she said that she had asked the foundation's director, on Saturday, by telephone, what the line reconciled, and that he had said it was a matter of heritage, and that she had asked him to define heritage, and that he had not.

Then she asked for a vote to withhold confirmation of the trustees pending an audit, and the man from Groningen seconded it before she'd finished the sentence, and it carried eleven to one, and the one was the chair, who, Anneke said, voted against it not because he disagreed but because he hadn't read anything and was the kind of man who wouldn't vote for a thing he hadn't read, which she said she respected, and which she said she intended to fix by giving him the sheet herself at lunch.

Then she proposed that, pending the audit, the bank exercise its right under the funding agreement to nominate interim trustees. And she proposed names. And one of the names was hers.

That carried too.

By half past nine the foundation that had been counting the questions of a four-hundred-year-old bank for thirty-one years had new trustees, and the old ones had been thanked for their service, and the old man had been, in the language of these things, invited to step back, and the quarterly schedule that Anneke had been due to sign at ten was sitting on her desk unsigned, and would stay unsigned, she said, until somebody could explain to her satisfaction what a *visit* was.

Nobody shouted. Nobody was arrested. Nothing was exposed. It was a vote at item four and a list of interim appointments, and the minutes, when I saw them, ran to a page and a half, and you could read them from start to finish and not know that anything had happened at all.

That's what a win looks like, when it's real. It looks like a form.

---

I'd holed out on the sixth by nine, in eleven strokes, which is a record and not a good one, and I'd sat down on the bench next to the phone and not turned it over, and Buddy had gone to sleep in the cart, and the sun had come round enough to start melting the frost in a line across the green, and I'd watched the line move.

The phone rang at twenty to ten. I let it ring twice. Then I turned it over.

"Mr Klippe."

"Mrs de Wit."

"It's done. You'll hear the details from someone else; I'm not going to give them to you, because you weren't there and I'd like that to stay true. I'm the chair. The trustees are out. The schedule isn't signed." A pause. "I'm going to tell you the terms, and you're going to agree to them, and then I'm going to hang up, because I have an audit to commission and I don't like telephones."

"Go on."

"One. The job goes. The nightly one. I don't know how, yet; I'm told it's in the backups. It'll take Frank a month. You will not touch it. You will not go near it. You'll open a ticket like a normal person and Frank will close it like a normal person and that'll be the first time in thirty-one years that's happened and I'd like it to be boring."

"Agreed."

"Two. The reconciliation line stops. It won't stop this quarter; there's a contract. It steps down. I've looked at the arithmetic. It steps down by exactly one unit on the next invoice, and I'll initial that one myself, and I'd like you to see it, because I think you'll want to."

"Yes."

"Three. The building. The lower floor. I don't know what's down there and I've decided that I don't want to know this week. The seats stay. The continuity contract is a real contract and the bank needs a recovery site and I'm not going to be the head of risk who cancelled the disaster plan because an engineer heard a noise. The annex is being rewritten. I'm rewriting it. There'll be a column for dependants and it'll be filled in by HR, not by a foundation, and there'll be a line about animals and it'll say something different."

I didn't say anything to that. I couldn't.

"Four." Her voice changed, very slightly. "You don't write about it. Not the bank, not the foundation, not the names. Not the sheets, not the schedule, not the book. You've done something that worked, Mr Klippe, and it worked because it was quiet, and the moment it isn't quiet it stops working and becomes a story, and you know better than anyone what happens to a story. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you the price of the three things above."

"I understand."

"And five, which is the one I expect you to argue with. The man who sells us the seats. Kalder."

"Yes."

"His company's contract is being reviewed as part of the audit. He's been informed. He didn't come in this morning. He's not answering his phone." A pause. "I've met him twice. He asked after my daughter both times. By name." Another pause. "He's not my problem any more. He's not the bank's. He may still be somebody's, and I'd like you to hear me say that I don't know whose, and that I've chosen not to ask."

"Part of this," I said, "was him. Gone."

"I know. You said so in December. In the room." A breath. "I can't give you that. I can only give you the five things. Take them."

I looked at the dog asleep in the cart, with his blanket and his grey eyebrows and his chin on the edge, and I looked at the line of melted frost moving across the green, and I took them.

"There's one more thing," I said. "That I'd like. It's small."

"Say it."

I said it. It wasn't about the bank, or the foundation, or the job, or Julian Kalder. It was small, and it was mine, and I'm not going to write it down, because it's the one thing in the whole of this that I did for no reason I could put on a sheet.

Anneke was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Yes. That I can do," and I heard her write something, and then she said the only other thing she said that morning that wasn't a term.

"They asked why we're not stopping you. The old ones. On the way out. One of them asked me that, in the corridor, as if it were obvious that we should. And I'd thought about it, Mr Klippe, because it's my job to think about that kind of thing, and I gave him the answer, and I'll give it to you once and then never again."

"Go on."

"A body is a fingerprint. He told me that himself, in a meeting, four years ago, as a joke, I thought. The whole method is that nobody ever sees a hand. And a man who has given three hundred people a sheet of paper with his name on it, and driven his wife round the country at night, and sat in a reading room and said no, is the most fingerprinted thing they've ever had. If anything happens to you now, three hundred people will know who did it, and they're the three hundred who do the work." A pause. "You made yourself too visible to vanish. That's the whole trick. I don't think you planned it."

"I didn't."

"No. I didn't think so. Goodbye, Mr Klippe. Go home. There's frost."

She hung up.

---

I sat on the bench for a while longer. The greenkeeper came past on the mower and lifted a hand and I lifted one back. The line of melted frost reached the far edge of the green and the whole thing went from grey to the colour of green that looks freshly painted, the colour it had been, I remembered, on a morning in April at a cemetery, when I'd thought that was the first lie.

Buddy woke up. He looked at me. He looked at the course. He made the small noise that means he'd like to get down, and I lifted him down, carefully, all twenty-eight kilos of him, and set him on the grass, and he stood there with his legs not quite trusting him and his nose up in the cold, and then he took four steps onto the green, slowly, and stopped, and stood in the sun.

I didn't call him back. The greenkeeper, on his mower, at the far end of the fairway, didn't stop either. He just looked, and looked away, and let an old dog stand on his sixth green on a Monday morning in December as if it were nothing, as if dogs stood there every day, as if the whole flat frozen world had decided, just for once, to leave it.
