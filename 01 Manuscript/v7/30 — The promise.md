# The promise

It was four in the morning and I was on the kitchen floor, which by now you'll have gathered is not news.

What was news was that I was frightened. Not the interesting kind. Not the kind I get at work when a system starts telling me what it is, which has a pleasure in it I've never admitted to anyone. The other kind. The kind from a corridor when I was sixteen, which I'd spent twenty-five years making sure I never felt again by the simple method of never being responsible for anyone.

Four hours earlier a man had stood in my garden with a beer and said *Deventer*.

He'd said it lightly. He'd been saying everything lightly all evening, to everyone, and everyone had loved him, Ana most of all; he'd sat with her for twenty minutes by the back door while the sausages burned and listened to her talk about her grandmother and the knitting, and I'd watched her face do the thing it does when someone is actually listening, and I'd been grateful to him, I want that on the record, I'd stood at my own barbecue being grateful to him. And then later, by the fence, with Frank telling the gorilla-mask story for the third time, Julian had finished one of my sentences. One of my private ones. A thing I say in my head and had never said aloud, and he'd said the end of it, smiling, like we'd always shared it. And when I'd stared at him he'd shrugged and said he must have heard me say it on the course. And then, a minute later, nothing to do with anything, he'd mentioned a town. A small one. In the east. Where a man I never met had died in a blue chair.

I have never been to Deventer. I have never said the word Deventer. I know where it is because Beard Man told me, once, in a market, what town the professor's daughter sold curtains in, and I wrote it down, and it has been in a folder in a bag ever since.

Buddy wouldn't go near him. All evening. Buddy, who goes near everyone, who has pressed his old head against the leg of every plumber and courier and Jehovah's Witness that has ever crossed our threshold, had stood in the kitchen doorway and watched Julian Kalder for four hours and had not once come out.

Ana found me at four. She came down for water and she didn't turn the light on, because she knows, and she sat down on the floor across from me with her back against the oven, and the dogs rearranged themselves around her the way they do, and she waited.

I didn't tell her about Deventer. I couldn't. I didn't have the sentence yet; I had a word and a feeling and a dog in a doorway, and if I'd said it out loud at four in the morning it would have sounded like exactly what she'd been afraid, for months, I was becoming.

So I said the only thing I had.

"One day," I said, "I'm going to need you to believe me."

She didn't say anything.

"I'm going to say something, and it's going to sound mad, and I'm not going to be able to prove it. And I'm going to tell you, and you're going to know what I mean, because I'll say it the way I'm saying this. And that day I need you to run."

"Run."

"Take the dogs and go. To your mother's. Wherever. Don't ask me, don't wait, don't pack. Go."

She looked at me for a long time in the dark. Then she said, in her ordinary voice, the one she uses for the shopping and the toaster and whether we're out of coffee:

"Run where? I've got a Doberman."

And I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me like something breaking, and Mora lifted her head at the sound and put it down again, and I sat on my own kitchen floor and laughed with my hand over my mouth so I wouldn't wake the street, and Ana watched me do it with her arms round her knees.

When I stopped she was still looking at me. And then her face changed, the whole of it, the way a room changes when someone turns the big light off and leaves the lamp, and she said, in the other voice, the one I've heard maybe four times in fifteen years:

"Okay."

Just that. Not *okay, what's going on.* Not *okay, but.* Okay.

I've thought since about what it cost her. To be asked that, by me, at four in the morning, with no reason given, and to say yes. I think it cost her more than anything I did afterwards cost me. I think it was the bravest thing anyone did in the whole of this, and nobody will ever count it, because it happened on a kitchen floor between two people and a Doberman and an old dog who had spent the evening in a doorway, watching a man who asked about him before he asked anyone's name.

She got up and got her water. At the door she stopped.

"He was nice to me," she said. "Julian. Tonight. He was really nice."

"I know."

"I liked him."

"I know. So did I."

She nodded. "That's the bit I don't like," she said, and went up.
