# The leak

The brewer rang them on a Tuesday afternoon, and by Thursday night the schedule had changed, and I know that because of the one thing I'd done that nobody, not even Ana, knew about.

I'd set a watch.

Not on them. I'm not a hacker; I keep saying it because people keep forgetting. On a date. The new schedule, the one from the phone, had a date at the top: the day it came into force, the day Anneke was due to sign it, a Monday three weeks out. I'd photographed it. And in our own systems, in the continuity programme's own calendar, which I had every right to read, that Monday had an entry, blandly named, a recurring quarterly meeting that had been in the calendar for eleven years and that I'd been invited to because I was on the programme now.

On Thursday night at eleven the meeting moved.

Not far. From three weeks out to four days out. To Monday. This Monday. The entry changed, in the calendar, the way entries do, with a little note that said *rescheduled by organiser*, and the organiser was a name I'd never heard of at an address that was the foundation's, and I was sitting at the kitchen table with a hundred and ninety sheets still to deliver when my phone made the small sound that means a calendar has changed, and I looked, and I knew.

The review had come forward. The brewer had rung, and somebody had listened, and somebody had looked at the number of helpers who'd suddenly stopped closing tickets in the east of the country that fortnight, which I imagine looked, on their dashboard, like a dial being turned the wrong way, and somebody had done what I'd have done, which is pull the date in before the thing spreads.

"Ana."

She came down. She looked at my face.

"Monday," I said.

---

I'm going to tell you what I thought in the next ten minutes, because it was the clearest I've ever thought and I don't expect to think that clearly again.

Monday was the schedule. Anneke signs, the quarter starts, the visits begin. I'd given Anneke everything on Friday and I didn't know yet what she'd do with it; she'd said *I'm going to read one*, and she'd taken the folder, and that was all. If she signed, the eleven addresses on the new schedule, plus whatever had been added since the brewer, got their visits, and I had a very good idea now what a visit was.

The board met on Monday too. Not about this. About the foundation's trustees, once a year, a formality, twelve people in a room approving a list of names they'd never met. That was the quarterly meeting in the calendar; that was what had moved. They'd pulled the foundation's annual confirmation forward to the same morning as the schedule, so that whatever the board voted, the schedule would already be signed.

Twelve board members. I had eleven sheets left and they were theirs, and I'd been going to deliver them next week, calmly, one at a time, the way I'd done the others.

I had four days. And they knew about me, properly now, not as a man with papers in a reading room but as a man with three hundred sheets in the east of the country, because the brewer had rung and I'd been in his yard.

"They'll come here," Ana said.

"Yes."

"Tonight?"

"I don't think so." I looked at the window, where the dog had been standing every evening for two months and where he was standing now. "They don't like fingerprints. A visit to the house of the man who's been handing out sheets, the week he hands them out, is a fingerprint. They'll go round me."

"Round you how?"

My phone rang.

---

It was Pieter. It was twenty past eleven at night and it was Pieter, who in eleven years had never once rung me outside working hours, and I could hear, before he spoke, that he was in his car.

"James."

"Pieter."

"There's a man at my door."

I put the phone on the table and put it on speaker and Ana sat down across from me and we both looked at it.

"Tell me."

"I'm in the car. I was coming home. I'm at the end of my street and there's a car outside my house that isn't ours and there's a man on my step talking to Marieke." His voice was very steady, the steadiness of someone who's decided to be steady for the length of a phone call and will pay for it after. "He's got a folder. He's got a—he's got the same kind of coat, James. As the one at the drill. In the car park."

"Can you see his face?"

"No. He's turned away. Marieke's got the door on the chain. She does that. I told her to do that, years ago, I don't know why, she thought I was mad." A pause. "He's just standing there. He's not pushing. He's *waiting*."

Ana's hand had found mine on the table.

"Pieter, listen to me. I'm going to tell you what to do and I need you to do exactly this, and I'll explain afterwards, and you can be as angry as you want."

"Okay."

"Drive up to the house. Park where you always park. Get out. Walk up to him like it's nothing, because to him it is nothing; he doesn't know you've seen a sheet, and if he did he'd be surprised you were calm, so be calm. Say good evening. Say, can I help you."

"And then?"

"And then let him in."

Silence on the line.

"James—"

"Let him in. Make him tea. Sit down with him in your kitchen with Marieke and the kids upstairs and listen to whatever he says. He'll have a reason. It'll be about the bank, or the programme, or your security clearance, or something about the migration. It'll be plausible. It'll be kind. He'll ask you things. He'll ask whether anyone's been in touch with you about your work, and whether anyone's given you anything."

"And I say—"

"You say nothing."

"I can't say *nothing*, he's—"

"You can. That's the one thing you can do. You answer every question with something true that isn't the answer. You've been busy. The programme's a lot of lists. You don't really follow the politics. You're very happy to help. You let him talk, and you nod, and you make a second cup of tea, and you say nothing, and in forty minutes he'll leave, because he's not there to hurt you, Pieter. He's there to find out what you know. And if you give him nothing, then nothing is what he's got."

I could hear him breathing. I could hear the indicator ticking in his car.

"Why would he hurt me? I'm on the list. You showed me. I'm *on* it."

"You're on it because you said leave it. You stopped saying it four days ago. That's why he's on your step."

A long pause.

"Marieke's not on it," Pieter said. "Not separately. She's a number. She's one of my three."

"I know."

"And if I'd—" He stopped. "If I'd closed your ticket. On Monday. Like always. He wouldn't be here."

"No."

"So this is you. This is because of your sheet."

"Yes," I said. "It is. And I'm sorry. And I'd do it again. And I need you to go and let him in and make him tea, because the alternative is that he waits on your step until he's been seen waiting, and then it's not a visit any more, it's a thing with a fingerprint on it, and I don't know what they do about those and I don't want to find out at your house."

The indicator stopped. I heard the car move.

"He's turned round," Pieter said. "He's seen the headlights. He's—" A breath. "He's smiling. Jesus. He's *waving*."

"Wave back."

"James."

"Wave back, Pieter. Park the car. Say good evening. And ring me when he's gone, from the kitchen, with the kettle on, and I'll be here."

The line went quiet for a second, and then I heard a car door, and footsteps on gravel, and Pieter's voice, from a distance, saying "Evening. Can I help you?" in a tone I'd heard him use in a glass box for eleven years, the tone of a man who's never once lost his temper at work and has saved it all for somewhere, and then the call ended.

---

He rang back at ten past midnight.

"He's gone."

"Tell me."

"He was from the foundation. Compliance. Routine, he said. A review of access on the programme, everyone who'd been added since the summer, nothing to worry about. He asked if anyone had approached me about the programme's data. I said the programme was mostly lists. He asked if I'd received any documents from colleagues outside normal channels. I said I got a lot of email. He asked—" Pieter stopped. "He asked about you. By name. Whether you were settling in. Whether I'd noticed anything. I said you liked lists." He laughed, once, not a good laugh. "He wrote that down. He had a little book. He wrote *likes lists*."

"Good."

"He asked after the kids. Their names. He knew their names, James. He asked if Lotte was still doing the hockey."

I didn't say anything.

"Then he thanked Marieke for the tea and he went. He shook my hand. He said if I thought of anything I should ring, and he gave me a card, and the card's got the foundation's name on it and a number, and the number's—" A pause. "It's the general number. The one with the music."

"I know the one."

"James, what the fuck is this."

"I'll tell you. Properly. Not tonight." I looked at Ana. "Tonight I need to know one thing. The man. Did you see his face in the kitchen?"

"Yes."

"Was it the man from the car park? At the drill? Julian?"

"No." Pieter sounded surprised at the question. "No, nothing like. Older. Grey. Nice voice. The kind of man you'd buy a pension from."

I closed my eyes.

"Okay," I said. "Thank you. Go to bed. Lock the door. You did it exactly right."

"I made him tea," said Pieter, in a voice of wonder. "He was in my kitchen asking about my daughter's hockey and I made him tea and said nothing and he *left*." And then, differently: "That's what you do. Isn't it. That's what you've been doing for a year. Sitting there saying nothing while they make themselves at home."

"Something like that."

"How do you stand it?"

"I've got a dog," I said, and meant it, and Pieter didn't understand, and said goodnight.

---

Ana took her hand off mine.

"Three addresses," she said. She'd been counting on her fingers; I'd seen her. "You said they'd go round you. Pieter's one."

"Pieter's one. There'll be two more tonight. People who got sheets and stopped. They're checking the ones nearest the top." I pulled the laptop over and opened the list, the three hundred and eleven, with the ticks beside the ones I'd done and the eleven at the bottom with no ticks that were the board. "And on Monday Anneke signs the schedule, and the board confirms the trustees, and all of this goes back to being nothing."

"Unless."

"Unless the board doesn't confirm them. Unless twelve people walk into a room on Monday morning each holding a sheet of paper that says what the foundation they're about to approve has been charging their bank for, per flagged person, for thirty-one years. And who's kept. And who isn't."

"Eleven sheets."

"Eleven. Anneke's got hers."

"In four days."

"In three. Monday morning's too late; they need to have read it. Sunday night."

Ana looked at the list. She looked at the dog at the window. She looked at the clock on the oven, which said twenty to one on a Friday morning, and she did the thing she does, which is put the kettle on, and while it boiled she stood at the counter with her back to me and said, "Where are they? The eleven."

"All over. Two in the city. Three in The Hague. One in Zeeland. One in Groningen. One in Maastricht, God help us. The rest in between."

"That's a thousand kilometres."

"About that."

The kettle clicked. She didn't move.

"Mum can have Buddy till Monday," she said. "Mora comes with us; she's good in a car and she's a Doberman and I'd like a Doberman in the car. We leave tomorrow after dark, because if they're watching the house they're watching it in daylight like normal people. We do the far ones first and work back. You read, I drive, and nobody reads me any signs about pumping stations."

"Ana."

"And on Sunday night, when it's done, we go to Mum's and we sleep, and on Monday morning you're not anywhere near that building, you're somewhere with a scorecard, because if this works I don't want you in the room when it does, and if it doesn't I want to know where you are."

I looked at my wife, in her dressing gown, at one in the morning, with a kettle behind her and a list of eleven strangers in front of her, planning a thousand-kilometre drive to deliver the only thing that could stop a machine she'd told a man to his face she didn't believe in.

"You don't believe any of this," I said.

"Not a word." She poured the tea. "Drink that. Then print the eleven. Then sleep. I'll pack."

Buddy got down from the window at last and came and lay across my feet, and I sat at the table and printed eleven sheets, one at a time, while my wife packed a bag upstairs and the Doberman watched the door, and somewhere in the east of the country two more people were making tea for a nice man with a folder and saying nothing, because I'd told them to, and I hoped to God that nothing was enough.
