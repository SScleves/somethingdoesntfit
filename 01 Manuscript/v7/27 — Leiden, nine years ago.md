# Leiden, nine years ago

The library was being restructured, which is the word people use when a library is being taken apart, and Julian Kalder had a clipboard.

He was thirty-four and good at this. The foundation sent him wherever a collection was being moved, because collections being moved are where things go missing, and because he had a gift, noted early and used often, for learning the names of everyone in a building in the first two days and using them, correctly, in the third. Porters, cataloguers, the woman who did the tea. He didn't do it as a technique. That was the thing his handlers had understood about him before he had: it wasn't an act. He liked people, briefly and completely, the way some men like weather.

The transfer took three weeks. Forty thousand volumes from a university annexe that was closing to a depot that wasn't, in numbered crates, by van, with a manifest. Kalder's job was the manifest. He stood at the loading door with the clipboard and ticked, and talked, and by the end of the first week the porters were telling him about their divorces.

The conservators worked upstairs. There were two of them. One was a man in his sixties who treated Kalder with the polite contempt of someone who knows what a clipboard means. The other was a woman of about thirty called Noor, who did not treat him with anything, because she did not, as far as he could tell in three weeks, treat people. She worked. She had a bench by the window with a lamp on an arm, and she sat at it with a scalpel and a bone folder and a sheet of something three hundred years old, and she did not look up when he came in, and she did not look up when he left, and on the second day he had learned her name and used it and she had said, "Yes," without turning round, and gone on with what she was doing.

He thought about her more than he thought about the manifest. He noted this, and filed it, as he'd been taught to file things about himself, under *attention*, and went on.

---

On the last day there was nothing left to tick.

The vans had gone. The porters had gone. The old conservator had shaken his hand with two fingers and gone. The building was empty in the way buildings are when the thing they were for has been taken out of them, and Kalder walked through it once, as he always did, checking, and found her still at her bench by the window, with the lamp on, doing nothing.

Not working. Sitting. Her hands were flat on the bench and the bench was clear.

He stood in the door. He said her name. She turned round, and looked at him, and he had the sensation, which he had not had before and has had exactly once since, of being looked at by someone who was not, at that moment, afraid of anything at all.

"Would you like some tea," she said. "There's a kettle. It's the last thing."

He said yes. He said it the way he said yes to porters.

She made the tea at a sink in the corner, in two mugs that had been left because nobody wanted them, and brought it over, and put his on the bench, and sat back down on her stool, and he sat on the one the old man had used, and she said nothing.

For twenty minutes. He timed it afterwards, from the clock on the wall, because he needed a number to put in the report. Twenty minutes, in an empty building, with a lamp on, with a woman who was not performing silence at him, not sulking, not waiting for him to speak, not doing any of the things that silence usually is. She was just there. She drank her tea. Once she looked out of the window at a tree. Once she looked at him, briefly, with something that might have been curiosity and might have been kindness and was, he decided later, both, and then she looked away again, and the clock went on.

And somewhere in the twenty minutes the thing went through him.

He has tried to describe it since, to people whose job it is to hear it described, and he has never done it well. It was not a light. It was not a voice. It was more like the moment on a long flight when you realise the engine noise has stopped and you cannot tell whether it stopped a second ago or an hour. Something that had been running in him for thirty-four years, so constantly that it had become the sound of being him, was not running. And in the quiet where it had been, he understood three things at once, completely, without words, the way you understand that you're falling.

That he was going to die.

That this was not a problem.

That it had never been a problem, and that everything he had ever done had been built on top of its being a problem, like a city on a fault.

He put the mug down. His hand was steady. She looked at him then, properly, and he saw that she knew, and that she had known it would happen, and that this was why she had asked him to stay, and that she would never say so.

"Thank you for the tea," he said.

"You're welcome," said Noor.

---

He drove to Zwolle that night because Zwolle was where you went.

The office above the courtyard was lit. He sat in the chair across from the man he reported to and made his report, which was mostly the manifest, forty thousand volumes, no discrepancies, and then he said that there was one other thing, and he said it in the voice you'd use for a discrepancy.

He told them. He gave the time. He gave the name. He described the twenty minutes as accurately as he could and watched the man across the desk write it down, and he watched the man's face, and he saw in it something he had not expected, which was recognition, and something else he had, which was arithmetic.

"And you're sure," the man said.

"I'm sure."

"And it's gone. The fear."

"Yes."

The man was quiet. Then he turned to the screen on his desk, which Kalder had never been shown and was now, without ceremony, being shown, and he moved something on it, and a date on it changed. Kalder watched it change. A review date, for a region, for a population. It came forward. Not by much. By a few weeks. Because of one conservator in Leiden and one man with a clipboard, two people who were, as of that afternoon, no longer afraid, and had therefore been counted.

"That's what we do," the man said. "You've seen it now."

Kalder said he understood. He did understand. That was the strange part, the part he has never managed to explain to the people whose job it is to hear it: he understood what the date meant, and what it meant for the woman at the bench, and he felt, about it, nothing he would have called fear and nothing he would have called guilt. He felt interested. He felt, for the first time in his life, that he could see the whole machine, and that it was beautiful, and that he was good at it.

He asked a question. He has thought since that it was the only decision he ever made.

"Who else?"

The man looked at him.

"On the list. For this region. Who else has it gone through." He heard his own voice and it was easy, it was the voice he used with porters. "I could go and see. I'm good at going and seeing. I'd know it now."

---

They gave him more names. He was right; he did know it now. That is the thing nobody tells you about the right column, the one that matters, the one that only the crossed can fill: it isn't a mystery. Once it has happened to you, you can see it in someone else's face across a room, the way a man who has broken his leg can tell, in a crowd, who has broken theirs. He saw it in a retired engineer in Zwolle. He saw it in a dealer's customer in Groningen who turned out not to have it yet. He saw it, nine years later, in a man on a golf course who asked about the dog before he asked anyone's name, and who didn't have it, not yet, but was going to, and Kalder could see the date on him like a watermark.

The seat came with the names. He hadn't asked for it. He had known, since the office in Zwolle, that it would come, because he had understood the machine and the machine rewards the people who understand it. A seat in the lower floor of a shed in a business park, with dependants per allocation, and he had no dependants, and he took it, and he has never once regretted it, and he will tell you so.

He went back to Leiden only once. Nine years later, on a Friday, with a clipboard, and a kind voice, and a list of her clients and her mother's care-home place. She made him tea again. She said nothing again. He timed it. It was shorter.

He had crossed, in an empty library, in twenty minutes, over a mug of tea that nobody wanted, and he had come back from the other side with the one thing the crossing is supposed to take from you still in his hand. Not fear. Fear had gone, and never come back. The other thing. The wanting.

He is the exception the book has to carry, and he knows it, and if you asked him he would say that he is not the exception. He would say he is the proof. That a man can see the whole of it and choose the seat. That seeing is not the same as leaving. That the organisation has always known this, and has built on it, and that it is the one true thing they have.

He would say it kindly. He would ask about your dog.
