# A pension visit, Zwolle

The man from the pension fund arrived at two, which was when he had said he would arrive, and he had a folder, and he wiped his feet.

Henk Boersma had been expecting a letter and had got a person instead, which he took as a courtesy. He was seventy-three and had worked thirty-one years for the same engineering firm in Zwolle, and he had started, lately, to say things at the bowling club that made the other men look at their shoes. Things about his father. His father had been a draughtsman, and had kept notebooks, and had once said something to Henk about a bridge that Henk had been turning over for sixty years and had recently started to turn over out loud.

The man from the pension fund did not know any of that, as far as Henk could tell. He knew about the indexation. He explained it twice, patiently, with a pen, and the second time Henk understood it, and the man did not make him feel stupid for needing the second time. He had a way of leaving a silence open that made you want to put something in it.

The man had brought his own coffee, in a steel flask, and apologised for it: an old habit from years of sitting in other people's kitchens, he said, he never liked to put anyone to trouble. Henk made coffee anyway, because Marijke would have, and Marijke was at her sister's in Meppel until Monday, which the man was sorry to hear, he'd have liked to meet her. He admired the house, which was a decent house and had not been admired in some years, and he asked about the photographs on the dresser, and Henk told him about the children, and about his father, more than he had meant to, and the man listened in the way that very few people listen, which is without waiting.

At some point, and Henk could not afterwards have said at what point, they were talking about cars.

---

The car was a 1994 Volvo estate in a dark green that Volvo no longer made, and Henk had bought it new, and it had done three hundred and ten thousand kilometres, and he could tell you what each of them had cost. He drove it twice a week. On Tuesdays to the shops, and on Sundays, while Marijke was at church, out along the canal road toward Hasselt and back, for no reason except that the Volvo liked a straight run and so did he.

The man from the pension fund liked cars. Not in the tiresome way; in the way of someone who had grown up with a father who kept one running past its time. He asked the right questions. He knew what the overdrive did. He laughed in the right place at the story about the mechanic in Kampen. And when Henk said, as he had said to a dozen people who had not heard it, that the car would outlast him, the man said, "I believe you," in a voice that made it a compliment to the car and not a remark about Henk.

Then he looked at his watch and apologised and said he had a problem that was not Henk's problem, and Henk said what is it, and the man said that he had a box to move, that afternoon, across town, a box of files from a colleague who had retired, and that the fund's car was a small thing with no boot to speak of, and he had been going to make two trips.

Henk said take the Volvo.

The man said no, he couldn't possibly.

Henk said he was seventy-three and the car was sitting in the drive doing nothing and he would be glad of the excuse to have someone else put petrol in it.

The man said, if you're sure. And he was back in forty minutes, which was less time than it would have taken, and he had put petrol in it, and he had also, Henk saw when he went out to look, run it through the wash on the way back, so that it sat in the drive in the late light the colour it had been in 1994.

"You didn't have to do that."

"It's a beautiful car," the man said. "It deserved it."

He left the flask. Henk noticed it on the table after the car had gone and thought, he'll be back for that, and put it by the door. The man had shaken his hand there. He said the indexation would come through in the new year and that Henk would get a letter, and that if he had any questions he should ring the number on the folder, and Henk said he would, and did not notice until a week later that the number on the folder was the general number, the one with the music.

He did not notice, because by then it did not matter.

---

On the Sunday Henk Boersma drove out along the canal road toward Hasselt, as he did, with the window down, as he did, and somewhere on the long straight after the pumping station the Volvo went off the road and into the water, at no great speed, in daylight, with nobody else on the road.

The police report said the driver was elderly and had been widowed that year and had, according to acquaintances, seemed distracted of late. It noted that the car was old and well maintained. It noted the absence of skid marks, which in a man of seventy-three, on a familiar road, in good light, it did not find remarkable.

The car was recovered. There was nothing wrong with it.

The folder from the pension fund was on the passenger seat, and the water had got into it, and the woman from the fund who came to the house afterwards to help Marijke with the paperwork looked at it, puzzled, and said that it wasn't theirs, and that nobody from the office had been out to Zwolle that month, and that it must have been one of those firms that go round the retired. Marijke said a man had come, a nice man, Henk had said so on the phone, he'd brought his own coffee. The flask was still by the door. Nobody came back for it, and she couldn't bring herself to throw it out, and it is there now, on the shelf above the coats, and she will tell you about it if you ask.
