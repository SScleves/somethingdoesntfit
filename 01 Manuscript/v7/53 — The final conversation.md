# The final conversation

Aylin booked it. That's the detail I keep coming back to. The Thursday after the vote I came up the stairs with nothing in my hands, and she looked up from the card index and said, "He's asked for you. He's got the room till four. I've put you in it," in exactly the voice she'd used in December, and I understood that nothing about her had changed and that this was, in its way, the point of her.

He was at the long table. Anneke was beside him, not by the window this time; beside him, with a notebook, the way a solicitor sits with a client who's agreed to talk. The tray was gone. The lamp was on. He looked, I'm sorry to say, very well.

"Mr Klippe. Sit. You've brought nothing."

"No."

"Good. I've had people bring things to this table for forty years and I've never once needed any of it." He folded his hands. "I've been retired. You'll have heard. Anneke has been very correct about it; I'm to be thanked, and I'm to have an office, and I'm to have no keys. I find I don't mind. I'd like to talk, because I enjoy it, and because it doesn't matter now, and because you're the only person who's ever sat where you're sitting and known what to ask. So ask."

I'd thought about this for a week. I'd made a list and thrown it away. In the end I asked the thing I'd wanted to know since a bar TV in a pub.

"Why does it never come out?"

---

He laughed, the small delighted sound. "Oh, that's the right one. That's the one nobody asks; they all ask *what is it*, which is boring. Why does it never come out." He looked at Anneke. "Write that down, it's a good question."

Then he told me. I'll give it to you the way he gave it, in order, because he was a man who'd thought about the order, and because the order is the method.

"It comes out all the time," he said. "That's the first thing you've got wrong. Everybody has. It has come out, in one form or another, every few years since the war, and every time it comes out we're there before it, telling it bigger. You've heard of soft disclosure. The idea that films and television have been easing people toward something for decades. Men in Black. The X-Files. The one about the eyes, the lovely one, with the iris that proves a soul can be tracked; I Origins, that was ours, we were very proud of it, it took a true thing and made it a romance. The one about the moon being a collector. The one about dreams within dreams." He smiled. "People think that's preparation. It isn't preparation. It's inoculation. You show people the truth as a film and they can never again hear it as a fact. Every man who finds the agency is now the man in the film who finds the agency, and everyone knows how that one ends. Dracula was ours, by the way. Not the book. The idea that you could put the dead walking in a theatre and have the audience go home and sleep. Walt was ours. Not knowingly. He was simply the best customer we ever had."

"That's the mask."

"That's the mask. The second thing is the mirror." He unfolded his hands. "Sometimes a person is handed a true thing. Not finds it. Is handed it, by accident, by something we don't control; there's more we don't control than you'd think. A writer in California was handed one, in 1974, a beam of pink light, he called it, and he spent the eight years he had left writing a million words trying to prove to himself that it hadn't happened. We didn't touch him. We didn't need to. The true thing, in a mind that's been raised to doubt, eats itself. He did our work for us, in longhand, and then he published the leftovers as novels, and now when anyone has the experience he had, they think of him, and they think *madman*, and they stop." He shrugged. "He was very good. I've read all of them."

"The internet."

"The third thing, yes. We were frightened of it. I was in this room when it arrived and I'll tell you honestly that for five years I thought it was the end. People could say things to each other faster than we could say the other version. And then the social part came, and then the machines that write, and it turned out that we'd misunderstood the whole four hundred years. We'd thought our job was to make people believe the wrong thing. It isn't. It never was. Our job is to make people doubt *everything*. And the machines do that for free. A person who doubts everything is a person who never finishes the sentence that begins *based on what*. You know that sentence. You've been finishing it since a funeral." He looked at me kindly. "You're very rare. I don't say that to flatter you. You're rare in the way a particular kind of misprint is rare, and we collect those."

Anneke wrote something. He waited for her to finish.

"Four. The bunkers." He said it with distaste. "You found the seats and you thought, as they all do, that the seats were the point. That somewhere below us a few hundred people were going to be kept alive through something, and come out, and inherit. That's what they're *told*. It's not what they're for. Nobody's being saved, Mr Klippe. Freezing is meaningless. The building below us is a very expensive way of making sure that the people who run things keep running them, because a man who's been promised a seat will do anything to keep it, and a man who's been given one will do anything to deserve it. The seats are fuel. They're how the engine turns over. The day it's all over nobody's going to come up out of the ground and start civilisation again. That's not how it ends. That's a film."

"How does it end?"

"Five," he said, as if I hadn't spoken. "The search. You'll have read about the search for a particular child, in a particular mountain country, who is supposed to be the same man each time. The tests. The objects from the last life, picked from a table. It's real. The search is real. I don't mean the religion is true, I mean the search finds something, often enough that the people who run it have never had to pretend. We've known that for three hundred years. And what did we do about it? Nothing. We let it stand. We let it be the most *public* thing of its kind in the world, with the robes and the ceremony and the tourists, because a thing that's done in robes can never again be done in a kitchen. You've seen a man pick his own coin from a table in a palace on television. So when a boy in a flat picks his dead grandfather's pen from a drawer and says *that's mine*, his mother thinks of the palace, and laughs, and that's the end of it."

I didn't say anything. I was thinking about a hand cracked at the knuckles, and a hospital corridor, and a coin in a vending machine that never came out.

"Six." He leaned forward slightly, and his voice dropped, not for secrecy, for pleasure. "The one you actually came for. What was in the books. The forty-one edits."

---

"You think we took out a fact," he said. "A sentence that said *knowledge was given*, changed to *arose*. The professor thought so. He had forty-one of them and he thought he'd found the shape of a cover-up, and he had, but he'd found the shape of the plate, not the thing printed on it. We didn't take out a fact, Mr Klippe. We took out a *method*. In every one of those forty-one books, if you read the sentence before the edit and the sentence after, the thing being described isn't history. It's instruction. How a thing is passed. Not the knowledge; the passing. The hand. And every one of them, read correctly, is a page from the same manual, the one every tradition on earth once had and every tradition on earth has now lost, which is the manual for dying."

He let that sit.

"How to do it without fear. How to keep what you are across the gap. How to find, on the other side, the people you loved, and the dog, if you want the dog; the old texts are very clear about animals, which is one reason they had to go. It was never a secret. It was *ordinary*. It was what your grandmother's grandmother was told at the bedside by someone who'd been told it at a bedside. And in every one of the forty-one books there was a trace of it, a sentence that said *this was given to them*, by which the author meant, whether he knew it or not, *this was taught*. And we changed it to *arose*. Because a thing that arose is a thing nobody can teach you."

"And you kept it."

"We kept it." He sat back. "That's six. We took it out of every book in the world and we kept it, and we use it. Every one of the people below us, the ones who matter, the ones with names rather than codes, has had it. That's what the right column is. Not fear leaving; fear leaving *and being taught what to do next*. We're not monsters. We're the last people on earth who still know how to die properly. We simply decided, a long time ago, that it was too dangerous to let everyone."

Anneke had stopped writing.

---

I said the thing then. I hadn't planned to. It came up out of a kitchen floor, four nights in November, the fourth night, the thing that happened, and I said it the way I'd say it to a dog.

"It's not for staying."

He looked at me.

"The remembering. What you took out. You're using it to come back. To keep your seat, lifetime after lifetime, the same few hundred, knowing what you are, finding each other. That's what the tiers are. That's what *they remember* means." I kept my voice flat. "But that's not what it was for. I've had it. Not from a book. On a floor, with a dog. And the thing you're taught, when you're taught it, isn't how to come back. It's how to *leave*. Remembering is so you don't have to do this again. You've got the manual and you've been using it to go round and round, in the same building, with the same names, because you're afraid of the one thing it's actually about, which is stopping."

The room was very quiet.

And then the old man did the thing I least expected, which was nod.

"Yes," he said. "That's seven. You've got there faster than most. Yes. We chose to stay." He spread his hands, a small gesture, almost apologetic. "What would you have done? You're handed the keys to the only door that matters, and on one side of it is everything you've ever been afraid of, and on the other is this room, and the people you know, and the work, and a chair you've sat in for forty years. We chose the room. Every one of us. For four hundred years. We've been very honest with ourselves about it, which is more than most people manage about anything. We chose to stay, and we made sure nobody else could leave, because a world where people could leave is a world where nobody does the payroll on Wednesday." He smiled. "You said it yourself, in December. The dog's the only reason you'd be worth a seat. Well. We're the people who took the seat and left the dog. That's all we are."

"And it never comes out."

"Eight. It never comes out because it *can't*. Think, Mr Klippe. You're good at this. Suppose it did. Suppose tomorrow every person on earth knew, for certain, as a fact and not a hope, that they go on, and that fear is what scatters them and love is what keeps them whole, and that there's a method, and it's simple. What happens on Thursday?"

I'd thought about it. I'd thought about it on a kitchen floor.

"Nobody goes to work."

"Nobody goes to work. Nobody pays the mortgage. Nobody does the thing they hate for forty years so that their children can do a thing they hate for forty years. The whole of it, every city, every road, every hospital, is built on people being more afraid of dying than of the life they're in. Take that away and you don't get heaven. You get collapse. You get killers who know they'll be back. You get savages who've been told the one thing savages should never be told, which is that it doesn't end." He shook his head. "We're not keeping it from you to be cruel. We're keeping it because the last time it was general knowledge, the world was very small and very poor and people died at thirty, and it was *fine*, it was fine for ten thousand years, and then somebody built a city, and a city can't run on people who aren't afraid. That's the deal. That's the only deal there's ever been."

---

"Nine," he said, and I heard Anneke's pen start again. "The new management."

He looked at her. She didn't look up.

"The bank has the foundation. The bank has the building. The bank is run, as of Monday, by a woman who reads things twice, and who will, in about eighteen months, understand what she's inherited, and will do, I'm fairly sure, exactly what we did, because there isn't anything else to do with it. It's not a thing you can switch off. It's a thing you can only run badly or well." He turned back to me. "She's offered you terms. I know what they are. I'd have offered the same. You've accepted them, and you were right to, and I'd like to add one thing to them, which isn't mine to add, but I'll say it anyway."

"Go on."

"Leave it."

The two words. In his voice, in that room, with the lamp on. The first lie I'd been told to my face, in a glass box, by a man who'd been told it himself.

"Not as a threat. As advice, from a man who's had the keys. You've done the thing. You've shown three hundred people the list. The machine in this region will stall for a generation and then start again somewhere else, under a different name, with a different word than *continuity*, and you'll be dead by then, and so will I, and so, I'm sorry to say, will the dog. You've bought time. That's all anyone's ever bought. Take it. Go to the market on Sunday. Play golf. Love your wife. And when the time comes, Mr Klippe, which it will, do the thing you did on the floor, and go. Don't come back. You're right that that's what it's for. You're the first person who's sat at this table and been right about that, and I find, to my surprise, that I'd like one of us to get it right."

He stood up. He was old and it took a moment. Anneke didn't help him and he didn't ask.

"The book," he said. "The one you asked her for. The thing you wouldn't say on the telephone." He looked down at me. "Write it. She's agreed; I've agreed; it costs nothing. A book that claims nothing and names nobody and asks the reader to look. We've been publishing those for four hundred years. It's the safest thing in the world." He smiled, the real one, the one I'd liked in December. "Nobody reads the middle."

He went out. Anneke closed her notebook and sat for a moment with her hand flat on it.

"Is he right?" I said. "About eighteen months. About you."

She looked at the door he'd gone through. She looked at the lamp.

"I approved an invoice thirty times without reading it," she said. "I've read it now. I don't know what I'll do in eighteen months, Mr Klippe. Nobody does. That's rather the point of the word *pending*." She stood up. "Aylin has the room till four. I'd stay, if I were you. It's a good room. It's the only one in the building that doesn't hum."

I stayed. I sat at the long table with nothing on it, under the lamp, in the quiet, and I thought about a manual for dying that had been taken out of every book in the world and used by a few hundred people to keep their seats, and I thought that the old man had told me nine true things and one lie, and that the lie was the last one.

It wasn't the safest thing in the world. It was the only thing I had. And somebody, somewhere, reads the middle. I'm proof.
