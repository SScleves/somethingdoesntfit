# Compartments

Kalder left at seven with a coffee in a travel mug and an apology, and we stood at the door and watched his car go, and when it had gone round the corner Ana said, "I liked him," in the voice of someone identifying a body.

We went back to the kitchen. The plates were still on the table. Buddy had taken up a position under his chair, Kalder's chair, and would not be moved from it, and in the end we left him there and sat at the other end, with the coffee, and Mora on the floor between us chewing something that had been, until recently, a tea towel.

"Explain it to me," Ana said. "Like I'm the dog."

---

I've since tried to explain it to cleverer people than my wife and I've never done it as well as I did that morning, and I think it's because she wouldn't let me use any words.

"There's a man at the top," I said. "An old man. He talks about the method like it's a vintage. He's never done anything. He's never visited anyone or closed a ticket or made a phone call. He sits in a reading room."

"Okay."

"Under him there are people like the one who was asleep at our table. A few dozen in the east of the country. They know what it's for. Not all of it; he didn't have the top of the folder. But they know it's a thing, and they know what the visits are, and they do them."

"The handlers."

"They've got codes. Not names. So that if one of them ever sat at a kitchen table and fell asleep, nobody could get the others off his phone."

"Which you did."

"Which I did, and I got codes."

Ana drank her coffee. "And under them?"

"Under them is everybody else." I picked up a plate and put it down again. "The dealer in Groningen. The man who drives the bus to the shed. Pieter. The woman who stocks the canteen. The brewer with the cellar. Hundreds of people, in one region, who do one small thing each, and every one of them was told one small reason, and the reason was believable, and the reason was different for each kind of person, and none of them know what the others were told."

"And none of them know what it's for."

"None of them. That's not a flaw, Ana. That's the design. The whole thing is built so that nobody who matters knows anything. Pieter thinks the job is a trap for leakers. The dealer thinks he's helping conserve old books. The brewer thinks he's guarding heritage. They can all be asked by a policeman, by a journalist, by me, and tell the complete truth, and it comes out as nothing. Because it *is* nothing. Each piece is nothing."

She thought about that.

"So it's strong."

"That's what I thought. For a month. I thought: you can't fight a thing where nobody knows anything, because there's nobody to catch." I leaned forward. "But turn it round. If nobody who matters knows anything, then nobody who matters has ever been *shown* anything. The dealer's never seen the schedule. Pieter's never seen the invoice. The brewer's never seen the list of who gets a seat. They've each been told a word and a reason, and they've been promised a thing, and the thing was a word too. *Continuity.* And they heard *you'll be kept*. And nobody has ever, in four hundred years, put the list in front of them and said: find your name."

"Because they'd stop."

"Because they'd stop." I sat back. "The strongest thing about them is the weakest thing about them. It's the same fact. It depends on people who don't know. The moment they know, it's just a few dozen men with codes and an old man in a reading room, and that's not an organisation. That's a club."

---

Ana got up and started clearing the plates, which is what she does when she's thinking, and I let her, and Mora followed her with the tea towel in her mouth in case it was wanted.

"Say what you're going to do," she said from the sink, with her back to me. "Plainly. No analogies. No systems."

"One page per person."

"Go on."

"Everyone on the helper list in this region. Every name. One sheet of paper each. On the sheet: what they were told. In their own words, from his phone. What they actually did; the ticket they closed, the call they made, the invoice they signed, from their own records, which I can get because it's all in the systems I've been reading for eleven years. What it was part of; the schedule, one line, their line. And at the bottom, the seat list. The real one. With a space where their name would be."

"And then?"

"And then I give it to them."

She turned round. "You walk up to Pieter and hand him a sheet of paper."

"After hours. At his desk. I say: this is what you've been doing. This is what you were told. This is who's kept. You're not on it. Your children aren't on it. And I walk away."

"He'll go straight to them."

"Some will. That's fine. That's good, actually; that tells them it's happening. But most won't. Think about what the sheet says, Ana. It says *you were lied to by the people you'd be reporting me to*. Where does Pieter go with that? Up? To the people who told him the job was a honeypot and left his kids off the list?" I shook my head. "He goes home. He sits in his kitchen. And the next time the ticket comes in, he doesn't close it. He doesn't do anything dramatic. He just *doesn't*. And the dealer doesn't ring. And the brewer asks a question. And the canteen woman says to the bus driver, did you get one too."

"And it stops."

"It doesn't stop. It stalls. Everywhere a helper stalls, the handlers have to go and do it themselves, and there are a few dozen of them, and there are hundreds of helpers, and they can't. They can't run it on their own. It's too wide. That's what I found out on the phone. It was never strong. It was *wide*, and wide only works if the width doesn't know it's being stood on."

Ana dried her hands. She came and sat down again, across from me, and Buddy came out from under Kalder's chair at last and lay down between us with a grunt, and she looked at him, and then at me.

"How many sheets."

"In our region, with what I've got? About three hundred."

"Three hundred people. One at a time. By hand."

"Yes."

"In how long?"

"Before the next schedule. The one on his phone. Three weeks."

She was quiet for a while.

"You can't drive and read," she said. "You read when you drive, you nearly killed us on the A28 reading a sign about a pumping station."

"I know."

"So I drive."

"So you drive."

"And Mora comes."

"Mora comes."

"And Buddy stays with Mum, because he's old and this is going to be a lot of car and he's done enough." She put her hand flat on the table, the way the dealer had put his on the ledger. "And you tell me every single thing. Not forty per cent. Not sixty. If I'm driving, I'm in. All of it."

"All of it."

"Say it."

"All of it, Ana."

She nodded. She looked at the plates in the rack, and at the chair at the end of the table where a man had slept with his mouth slightly open, and at the dog, and she said the only thing I hadn't thought of, which is what she's for.

"The ones who do go to them. The ones who report it. What happens to you then?"

I didn't have an answer. I had an old man's voice in my head saying *a body is a fingerprint*, and a seat list I wasn't on, and a word in a notes column that had been *companion* and was now, on the new schedule, nothing at all, and I didn't know if nothing was better.

"I think they've got a rule," I said. "I think they don't like fingerprints. I think I've been counting on it for a month without saying so."

"That's not an answer."

"No."

"Then we go fast," said Ana, and got up, and started looking for the printer paper.
