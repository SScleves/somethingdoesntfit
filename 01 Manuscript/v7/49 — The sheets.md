# The sheets

A sheet looked like this. I'm going to show you one, because I made three hundred and eleven of them and they were all the same shape, and the shape is the argument.

At the top, a name. Then four short sections, in this order, with a line between each.

**What you were told.** One sentence, in the words from the phone. *The nightly job is a security honeypot; leave it in place.* Or: *Certain editions are tracked for conservation; please telephone if asked for.* Or: *Your cellar holds heritage material; access is restricted for its protection.*

**What you did.** Their own records. The ticket they'd closed, with its number and date. The call they'd logged. The invoice line they'd approved, with their initials. The access they'd granted. Taken from systems they had the right to look in and never had, and printed with the reference numbers, so they could check.

**What it was part of.** One line from the schedule. Their line. The date their ticket was closed, or their call was made, set beside the date on the schedule that it had moved, and the address it had moved, and, in the margin, in my handwriting, one word for what had happened there. *Heart. Sold. Fire. Quiet.*

**Who is kept.** The seat list. The real one, from the annexes, for the four companies and ours. Names, dependants per allocation, the line about animals. Printed small, two columns, a page and a half. And at the top of it, in the only sentence on the whole sheet that was mine:

*Your name is not on this list. Please check.*

That was it. No theory. Nothing about books or sentences or what happens when we die. I'd learned that from Frank, in my own kitchen: don't give them the story, give them the invoice. A sheet made no claim they could argue with. It was a diff. Here is what you were told, here is what you did, here is what it was for, here is who's kept, and the space between the last two was theirs to fall into.

Three hundred and eleven. Three weeks. Ana drove.

---

I'll tell you about five of them. The rest were variations, and some of them I'll never know how they landed, because the whole point of a sheet is that you hand it over and walk away.

Pieter first, because he had to be, and because I owed it to him.

I did it at his desk, after seven, when the floor was empty and the cleaners had gone through. He was still there because he's always still there; Pieter works late the way other men drink, steadily and without enjoyment. I put the sheet on his keyboard and sat down in the chair beside his desk, the one people sit in when they've come to resign, and I didn't say anything.

He read it. I watched him read the first section and recognise the sentence, because it was his, from a meeting I'd never been in, and I watched his ears go red. I watched him read the second section and nod, because the ticket number was right, and the date was right, and the closing note was his. I watched him read the third, and stop, and read it again, and look at the word in the margin, which for his line was *fire*.

And then he turned the page and found the list, and he found his own name, which was there, which I'd left there on purpose, and I watched his face start to clear, and then I watched him read the number beside it.

*Dependants per allocation: 3.*

"I've got two," he said. It came out very quietly. "Two kids. Lotte and Bas. And Marieke. That's three." He looked up. "That's three with Marieke."

"Yes."

"So that's—" He stopped. He put his finger on the number as if it might change. "That's us. That's all of us. Why does it say three like it's a—" He couldn't find the word. I had it; I'd had it for a month. *Allocation.* But I didn't give it to him. He'd get there.

"Read the sentence at the top, Pieter."

He read it. *Your name is not on this list. Please check.* He read it twice. Then he said, "But it is. I'm on it. I'm right here."

"You are. I know. I put that sentence on every one. Now read the names around yours."

He read them. He knew most of them. Heads of department. Two board members. Anneke. The forty from the drill, and then the others, the hundred and twenty, and the people he reported to and the people they reported to, and no one, not one person, from the floor he managed. Not Frank. Not the six people in the glass box. Not the woman at the next desk who'd been at the bank twenty-two years.

"They're not on it," he said.

"No."

"My team's not on it."

"No. You are. Because you said *leave it*. That's what the seat's for, Pieter. It's not a reward. It's a retainer."

He sat there for a long time with his finger on the number three. Then he said, in a voice I'd never heard him use, "What do I do," and I said the thing I'd decided to say to all of them, because it was true and because it was the only instruction that couldn't be reported upward as a threat.

"Nothing. That's the point. Next time, don't."

I left him there. I don't know how long he stayed. The next morning he said good morning to me in the glass box exactly as he always had, and I thought I'd lost him, and then, four days later, a ticket I'd opened against page 212 as a test came back not closed. Not actioned either. Just open. Sitting there. For the first time in eleven years Pieter had failed to tidy something, and I knew what it had cost him, and I said nothing, and neither did he.

The dealer in Groningen I did by post, because he'd earned the courtesy of not having me in his shop again, and because I wanted him to be able to read it alone. I put a note in with it. *You asked who sent me. Nobody. This is what the number in your ledger does.* Two weeks later a parcel came. It was the ledger. The actual one, cloth spine, eleven years of his handwriting, with the number cut out of the back cover with a scalpel, cleanly, the way a conservator would do it, and a card that said, in the neat old hand, *I find I no longer need it.*

---

The brewer went to them.

I knew somebody would. I'd told Ana one in ten would, and it was closer to one in twenty, and he was the first, and I want to describe him because he wasn't a bad man and I'd like that on the record.

He had a family firm in a small town, four generations, with a cellar under the brewhouse that went back to the seventeenth century, and twenty years ago a foundation had come to him with a grant and a request: the cellar was heritage, it was dry and cool and stable, would he allow part of it to be used for the storage of archival material, under seal, with access restricted to the foundation's own staff. He'd said yes. He'd have said yes to anyone with a grant and a kind word about his great-grandfather. His sheet said *heritage*, and his record was a key register with his signature on it four times a year, and his line on the schedule was a date on which a van had come, and I'd watched the van, from a lay-by, with Ana reading the pumping station sign out loud to keep me from doing it.

He read the sheet standing in his own yard with the smell of malt coming off the brewhouse. He read it carefully, all of it, and he looked at the seat list, and he looked for his name, and it wasn't there, and he looked up at me with an expression that I didn't understand until later, which was not anger and not fear. It was embarrassment. The embarrassment of a man who's been told, in front of a stranger, that he's been had.

"Thank you," he said, formally, and went inside.

He rang them that afternoon. I know because of what happened on the Thursday, which I'll get to. But I want to say this about him: he rang them because he'd been told, for twenty years, that the foundation was the kind of institution you rang when something strange happened, and a stranger in his yard with a sheet of paper was something strange. He wasn't betraying me. He was doing what the word *heritage* had taught him to do. He was being a good helper, one last time, and it was the last time, because the people he rang could not explain the sheet to him, and he was a man who'd run a business for forty years and knew what it sounded like when somebody couldn't explain an invoice.

---

Anneke de Wit I'd left till the end of the second week, because she was the one I was afraid of, and because her sheet was the longest.

Head of risk. The silent woman by the window. Her record wasn't a ticket or a phone call; it was thirty quarterly approvals of a line that said *continuity reconciliation (recall index), per flagged person*, with her initials against each, going back eleven years to the day she'd got the job. Her sheet had the whole spreadsheet on it. The six steps. The red cell. And her seat, which was on the list, with a dependants number of one, which I'd checked against the intranet and which was correct: a daughter, at university, in Utrecht.

I didn't go to her office. I asked for a meeting, through her assistant, under the programme's name, and I got twenty minutes on a Friday, and I walked in and put the sheet on her desk and sat down, and she read it, and she did not say a word for eleven minutes. I counted. She read the invoice page, the one with her initials on it thirty times, and she turned it over and read the back, which was blank, and turned it back and read it again, and I understood that she was doing what I do: she was reading the middle. She was looking for the place where her own signature stopped being a formality and started being a thing.

When she spoke, it was the first time I'd heard her voice.

"Thirty," she said.

"Thirty."

"I approved this thirty times." She looked at the red cell. "This is you."

"That's me."

"The week you opened a ticket." She was very still. "And the other steps. Before my time, most of them. Who were they?"

"I don't know all of them. I know one. A physicist in Utrecht, in 1993, whose paper got filed under instrumentation. That's a step. A professor who found forty-one edits and wrote to a post box in March. That's the one before mine."

She looked at me for a long moment with the face I'd seen by the window in the reading room: not hostile, not frightened, assessing, the face of a woman whose job is to look at a thing and say what it will cost.

"You were in that room," she said. "In December. You put these on the table and he laughed."

"Yes."

"I didn't."

"No. You turned your head."

"I turned my head because I recognised my own initials, Mr Klippe, on a sheet of paper, in a room I'd been told was about an archive." She put her hand flat on the sheet. "I've been on the foundation's board for four years. I was asked to join because the bank funds it and someone from the bank should sit there. I've sat there. I've approved the minutes. I've never once been told what the reconciliation line reconciles." She stopped. "That's not an excuse. I'm head of risk. It's the opposite of an excuse."

"I'm not here for an excuse."

"What are you here for?"

"For you to read the rest," I said. "There's a schedule. There are four other companies. There's a list of three hundred people in this region who were each told one small thing and a word, and the word was continuity, and not one of them is on the list you're on. I've given them their sheets. I've got eleven left, and they're the board. Your board. The bank's. Twelve people who vote on the foundation's trustees once a year and have never seen an annex." I looked at her. "I'd like you to read the rest before Monday, because Monday is when you sign next quarter's schedule, and I'd like you to know what you're signing."

She was quiet. Then she said: "Give me the rest."

"All of it?"

"All of it. Tonight. Here." She looked at the red cell one more time. "I've approved thirty of these without reading. I'm going to read one."

And Aylin.

I'd put her last on the list, and then I'd nearly taken her off it, because she was the one I liked and because her sheet was the thinnest: a woman who sat at a desk in a reading room and kept a card index and booked appointments for dead men. Her record was the diary. Her line on the schedule was the word *conservation* against a transfer of records from an archive in Apeldoorn, and her seat wasn't on any list, and her word was *heritage*, and I couldn't make the sheet say anything that would hurt her.

I gave it to her anyway, on the Thursday, at the desk, when I came up the stairs for the appointment she'd booked me into without my asking.

She read it. She read it the way she did everything, calmly, like a woman who's seen a lot of paper. And when she got to the end she looked up and said, "What do you think I'm conserving, Mr Klippe?"

I said I didn't know.

"No. You don't." She got up. She went to the card index, the wooden one, and she didn't open a drawer. She reached behind it and took out a book. A big one, bound, the kind with a ribbon, and she brought it to the desk and opened it and turned it round to face me.

It was the visitor book. Every person who had ever sat at the long table, in her handwriting and the handwriting of whoever had kept it before her, going back, when I turned to the front, to 1931. Name. Date. Shelfmark requested. And in a final column, headed *Outcome*, a word.

I read down the page she'd opened it at. The professor was there, in March, with a date and the shelfmark that was on the last page of his thin book, and the word in the last column was *Deceased*. Above him, a name I didn't know and the word *Seated*. Above that, *Quiet*. *Quiet*. *Seated*. *Deceased*. *Declined*. *Quiet*.

And at the bottom, in December, in Aylin's hand: my name. The date. No shelfmark; I hadn't asked for one. And in the outcome column, in fresh ink, one word.

*Pending.*

"I keep the book," said Aylin. "That's what I conserve. I've kept it for nine years and I've never been told what the last column means and I've never asked, because I was told it was heritage, and because it's very old, and because somebody has to." She looked at the sheet I'd given her, still on the desk. "You've just told me what it means."

"I didn't tell you anything. I gave you a list."

"You gave me a list I'm not on." She closed the visitor book. "I've had that book under my hand every working day for nine years, Mr Klippe. I know every name in it. I know which ones came back." She put the book down in front of me. "Copy it. Not now. He's upstairs. Come at midnight; the door's on my badge. Copy it and give it to whoever you're giving things to."

"Why?"

She looked at me as if the question were in bad taste.

"Because it's the last column," she said. "Because I wrote *pending* next to your name in December and I'd like, for once, to know what the word turns into before it does."
