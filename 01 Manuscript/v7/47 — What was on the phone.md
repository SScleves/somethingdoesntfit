# What was on the phone

He slept with his mouth slightly open, which made him look younger, and his phone was face down by his plate, where he'd put it when he sat down, the way he always did, the way you put something down when you want everyone to see you're not looking at it.

Ana was standing by the sink with her arms folded and her mother's pill bottle still in her hand. She hadn't moved since he'd gone. Neither had Buddy, who had stopped growling the moment Kalder's head went down and now stood in the doorway watching the sleeping man with an expression I can only describe as professional.

"How long," I said.

"Mum takes one and she's out for seven hours. He's had two." She looked at the bottle. "In the wine. I didn't—" She stopped. "I didn't plan it, James. I want that clear. He asked if Buddy was okay *now*, and you'd never told him Buddy was ill, and I was standing by the cupboard, and the bottle was there, and I thought: he's going to sit here being lovely until one of us says something we can't take back, and I'd rather he slept."

"You improvised."

"I improvised." She put the bottle down very carefully. "Is that a crime?"

"Probably."

"Good," said Ana, and sat down, and put her face in her hands, and started, very quietly, to shake. Not crying. The other thing. I've seen it in people after outages, when the system's back up and there's nothing left to do with the adrenaline.

I didn't go to her. I'd like to say I did. I looked at the phone.

---

I need to explain something about myself that I've never said plainly, and this is the place.

I'm not a hacker. I can't code; not really, not the way Frank can, not the way the people who built the thing I found can. I've spent eleven years watching other people's systems and I couldn't build one of my own to save my life. Everything I know about getting into things I know from the outside: from watching, for a decade, how people get in, so that I can tell when they have.

And one of the things I know, from a hundred incident reports, is that nobody breaks into a phone. They don't need to. People unlock their phones for you. They leave them face down on tables. They fall asleep with their hand on the cloth beside a glass of wine, thumb up, in a kitchen, in December, at a house they've been visiting for months because they liked the dog.

I picked up his hand. It was warm and heavy and it didn't resist. I turned the phone over and put his thumb on it, and it opened, and I put his hand back exactly where it had been, and I sat down with another man's life in my palm, and I looked at the clock on the oven.

Forty minutes. I gave myself forty, because at forty I'd have to decide whether to stay or go, and I didn't want to decide that with his phone in my hand.

---

I'm going to tell you what was on it in the order I found it, because the order is how I understood it, and because the thing I understood wasn't on any one screen.

No messages. That was the first thing. Not deleted; absent. The messaging app was there and it was empty, the way a show house is empty. Whoever he talked to, he didn't talk to them here.

Then a folder. No name, a grey square, the kind of thing you'd scroll past. Inside it, three things that looked like spreadsheets and one that looked like a map.

The first spreadsheet was people. Not names. Codes: two letters and three digits, a region, and a status, and beside each one a short note in the register I'd come to know from the schedule, the register of a man confirming a delivery. *Active. Active. Reassigned. Active. Retired (natural).* Forty-odd rows. I didn't know what I was looking at until I found his own: a code with a region that was ours and a status that was *active* and, in the note column, the only one with anything personal in it, a single word that I understood immediately and have not stopped understanding since.

*Hands.*

Handlers. The people who do the visiting. By code, by region, for the whole of the east of the country, and I had a dozen of them, and I didn't know a single name, and I understood that this was on purpose and that the purpose was that nobody, not even the man asleep at my table, should ever be able to give the names, because he'd never had them.

The second spreadsheet was the schedule. Next quarter's. The one after the one I'd had in my bag for a month. Addresses, dates, a column headed *Visit*, a column of notes. I read it with my heart doing something I didn't like. Our address wasn't on it. I read it again. It wasn't. The eighth, ninth and tenth from the old schedule were, carried over, with new dates, and nine new ones, and beside three of them the word *pension*, and beside one the word *companion*, and I photographed every row with Ana's phone because mine was in the hall and I wasn't leaving the table.

The third was seats. Not ours. Four companies. Four annexes, like the one I'd found in our system, with names and *dependants per allocation* and the line about animals, and the companies were a shipping firm, a hospital group, an energy company and a university, and the total across the four, when I added the column in my head, was a number of people that would fill the shed in the business park three times over.

So there were other sheds. Of course there were.

---

And then the fourth thing, the one that looked like a map and wasn't, and I want to tell you about this one slowly, because it was the thing I didn't expect and it was the thing that told me how to win.

It was a list of helpers.

Not handlers. The others. The people who make the phone calls and close the tickets and sign the invoices and catalogue the records and drive the vans. The dealer in Groningen. The site manager in the high-visibility jacket. Pieter. Hundreds of them, in our region alone, and they weren't by code. They were by name, because nobody had ever thought a helper needed protecting. They weren't the ones who knew anything.

And beside each name, in a column with no heading, was a word. One word per person. And when I'd read enough of them I realised that the column had a heading after all, it just wasn't written down, and the heading was: *what we told them.*

*Honeypot.* That was Pieter, and about a dozen others in the bank, and it meant they'd been told the orphan job was a security test, a trap for leakers, and that they were helping catch bad people by leaving it alone.

*Disaster recovery.* That was the site manager, and the bus driver, and the people who stocked the canteen in the shed, and it meant exactly what it said, and it was true, and that was the genius of it.

*Conservation.* That was the dealer in Groningen, and a woman in Deventer who turned out to be the professor's neighbour, and a records clerk in Apeldoorn, and it meant they'd been told that certain old books and papers were being tracked for their own protection, and that a telephone call now and then was a kindness to the past.

*Heritage.* A brewer in a small town whose family firm had a cellar. A man who owned a quarry. The site in the business park was listed under heritage too, and so, I saw, was a building I'd never heard of near Nijmegen. It meant: you're looking after something old and important and we'd be grateful if you didn't ask what.

Four words. Four small, believable, flattering reasons, and against every single name in the fifth column, the same word, over and over, down the whole list like a watermark:

*Continuity.*

That was the promise. That was what they'd all been told they were part of. Not saving the world; nobody believes that. *Continuity.* The thing that goes on. The payroll on Wednesday. And every one of them, I understood, sitting at my own kitchen table with a sleeping man's phone, had heard that word and had taken it to mean, without ever once examining it, *you'll be all right.*

I looked for the names on the four annexes. I looked for Pieter's helpers, the honeypot dozen, on the seat lists. I looked for the dealer. I looked for the site manager. I looked for the brewer.

None of them. Not one. Hundreds of people who'd been told *continuity* and had heard *you're kept*, and the lists of who was kept had been in the next folder along the whole time, and not one of their names was on it.

---

The oven clock said thirty-one minutes.

I put the phone down. I put it face down, exactly where it had been, at the angle it had been, and I sat back, and Ana lifted her face out of her hands and looked at me.

"Well?"

I didn't know how to say it. I've thought about it since and the only way I can put it is this: for a month I'd thought the thing I'd found was a machine, and machines are strong, and you don't beat a machine, you leave it, you go around it, you take the seat. And in thirty-one minutes on a stranger's phone I'd found out that it wasn't a machine at all. It was a few dozen people who knew, standing on a few hundred who'd been told four different lies, standing on everybody else. And the few hundred had never once been shown the list they weren't on.

"It's not strong," I said. "It's wide. That's different."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means I know what to do." I looked at the man asleep at my table. His breathing hadn't changed. In a few hours he'd wake up with a headache and a blank where the evening should be, and he'd check his phone, and it would be exactly where he'd left it, and he'd never know, because the one thing a man like that never considers is that somebody might do to him what he does to everybody else, which is simply pay attention. "It means I've got the paper, Ana. I've got what every one of them was told, and I've got the list they're not on. And nobody's ever put those two things side by side in front of them. Nobody's ever needed to. They never asked."

Buddy came out of the doorway, finally, and walked, slowly, past the sleeping man's chair without looking at him, and lay down across my feet.

"So what do we do," Ana said.

I'd been waiting fifteen years for someone to ask me that in a voice that meant it.

"We make him comfortable," I said. "We let him sleep. And in the morning we're very surprised he dropped off, and very nice about it, and we send him home with coffee." I looked at the phone. "And then I'm going to need a printer. And three weeks. And you're going to have to drive."
