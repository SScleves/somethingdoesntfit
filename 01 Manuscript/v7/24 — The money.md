# The money

I'll tell you the rule first, because it's the only thing in this chapter I'm sure of, and it isn't mine.

When the files are gone, look for the receipts.

Everybody knows the famous version. In 1973 the head of an intelligence agency ordered a programme's records destroyed, every page, and they were, and four years later twenty thousand pages turned up anyway, because somebody had filed them in the finance department under *invoices*. The men who burned the files were thinking about what the files said. The men who kept the receipts were thinking about whether the money added up. Those are different kinds of men, and the second kind always wins in the end, because the second kind is boring, and boring is what survives.

I didn't know I was following the rule. I thought I was doing my job.

---

The continuity programme runs on contracts. That's what it is, under the lists: a set of agreements with other companies that say, if this happens, you will do that, and here is what we pay you per quarter for the promise. Somebody has to read them. Nobody wants to. Saskia gave me the folder on my second Monday with the face of a woman handing over a chore she'd been dreading, and said, "See if anything's expired," and I said I would, and I did, and nothing had, and that's where a normal person stops.

I read the invoices.

I need you to understand that this is not detective work. It's the opposite. Detective work is looking for the thing. What I do is read everything in the order it comes, at the same speed, with the same attention, whether it's the twenty-page master agreement or the line that says *parking*. I don't skim. I can't. I learned at ten that the error is always in the part you were sure of, and I've been reading the boring part ever since, like a man who's been bitten once and now checks every dog.

The invoices came quarterly from four suppliers. Three of them were exactly what you'd expect: the recovery site, the data people, the company that tests the phones. The amounts went up a little every year with inflation and down a little when someone renegotiated, the way money breathes.

The fourth was a foundation.

I knew the name. It was on the sign at the entrance to the shed in the business park, above the smaller name on Julian's lanyard. The foundation owned the building; the bank rented seats in it through Julian's company; that much was on the master agreement and made sense. But the foundation also invoiced the bank directly, separately, every quarter, for something else.

One line. The same line, every quarter, for as far back as the folder went, which was eleven years.

*Continuity reconciliation (recall index). Per flagged person.* And a number of persons, and a rate, and a total.

I read it the way I read the sentence on page 118. First time, nothing: a service line, consultancy-shaped, the kind of thing that gets approved because it's been approved before. Second time: *per flagged person.* Third time, out loud, in the empty office at seven in the evening, like a man being punished: *recall index.*

I didn't know what a recall index was. I knew what *flagged* meant. I'd been flagged. There was a file on my laptop with a hundred and thirteen lines in it and a two-word reason beside each one.

---

Here's what I did next, and it took two evenings, and Ana thought I was working late, which was true.

The folder went back eleven years. The finance system went back thirty-one. I pulled every invoice from the foundation since the first one, which was dated the year the bank moved into the recovery site, and I put the numbers in a sheet, quarter by quarter, and I looked at them the way I look at anything that breathes.

They didn't breathe. That was the first thing. Inflation goes up by two or three per cent and wobbles. These numbers didn't wobble. They sat flat for years, perfectly flat, the same number of flagged persons every quarter, and then they stepped. Up. Once. By a clean amount, a round multiple, like a dial being turned one click. And then flat again, for years. And then another step.

Six steps in thirty-one years. Each one a multiple of the one before, give or take nothing. Nobody negotiates a contract like that. Contracts drift. These were set.

I looked at the dates of the steps. I'm not going to give you all of them, because you'd want to know what they mean and I didn't, then. I'll give you the last one.

The last step was dated the Monday of the week I opened my ticket.

Not the week I asked in stand-up. The ticket. The thing I'd typed into a system on a Thursday afternoon a week after Pieter said *leave it*, a two-line request to investigate an unowned job, which I'd opened anyway, because a dealer in Groningen had told me to be plain about my reasons and I'd taken it as advice. That Monday, the number of flagged persons in a four-hundred-year-old bank had gone up by one click of a dial, and the foundation had sent an invoice for it, and somebody in Finance had approved it, because it had been approved before.

I sat with that. The office was dark except for my screen. Somewhere down the corridor a cleaner was doing the thing with the bins.

They hadn't just counted me. They'd billed for me.

---

The second contract I found the next night, and it was easier, because by then I knew what I was looking for, which is the only kind of looking I distrust.

It ran through Julian's company. Work-area recovery; seats, annual; the straightforward one, the one the bank thought it was paying for. Forty desks in a shed, tested once a year with a sandwich. The master agreement was twenty pages and I'd read them all on the Monday and they were fine.

The annex wasn't in the folder. Annexes never are. They live in the system, attached to the contract record, and you need a role to open them, and on my second Monday on the programme I hadn't had the role. By the second Thursday I did. Saskia had added it with a sigh, because I'd asked, and because she'd learned in two weeks what Ana learned in two months, which is that it's quicker to give me the thing than to explain why not.

*Annex C. Seat allocation.*

It was a list. Of course it was a list. Everything in this story is a list; I've come to think that's not a coincidence but a fact about how these things are done, that if you want to do something enormous and have nobody notice, you do it as a list, because nobody reads lists.

Names. Our names. The forty from the drill, and then more, a hundred and twenty, with a column headed *role*, and a column headed *dependants per allocation*, with a number in it, two or three or four, and a column headed *notes*.

My name wasn't on it. I checked. I want you to notice that I checked, and that this time I didn't feel disappointed.

Pieter's was. *Dependants per allocation: 3.* He has two children.

And under the table, in the small type where the terms live, among the clauses about access and badging and the hours of the canteen, one line, which I read three times and then photographed with my phone, because I'd stopped trusting systems to still contain things in the morning:

*Companion animals are not accommodated.*

---

Ana found me on the kitchen floor at eleven.

I hadn't meant to be there. I'd come home, I'd eaten, I'd said work was fine, and then at some point I'd gone to the kitchen for water and the folder was on the table and I'd sat down on the floor with my back against the cupboard the way I do, and Buddy had come and put his chin on my leg, and I'd been there for a while.

She didn't say anything. She sat down on the floor too, across from me, with her back against the oven, which she hates, and Mora came and lay between us like a border.

"Show me," she said.

So I showed her. Not all of it; the forty per cent, which had grown. I showed her the sheet with the steps. I showed her the invoice line, and I watched her read *per flagged person*, and watched her get it, faster than I had, because Ana is faster than me about people and this was, in the end, about people. I showed her the annex. She read the names. She found Pieter. She looked at the number beside him and then at me and didn't say anything, and then she read the small type, and I watched her find the line.

She read it twice. Then she looked at Buddy, who had fallen asleep with his chin on my leg, and at Mora, who was upside down between us with her legs in the air, and then she looked at me, and her face did a thing I'd never seen it do, which was go completely still.

"Not accommodated," she said.

"No."

"They've got a plan. For something. With desks and a canteen and a number of dependants. And the dogs aren't in it."

"No."

"And you're not in it."

"No."

"But Pieter is."

"Pieter said leave it."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she said, in the other voice, the one she uses maybe twice a year: "What are they planning for, James."

And I said I didn't know. Which was true. I had a sheet of numbers that stepped like a dial, and a receipt for my own curiosity, and a list of my colleagues with their children counted, and a shed in a business park with a reading room on top of it that a dead man had an appointment in, and I didn't know what any of it was *for*. I knew what the files said. I'd been reading the files for a month. It was the receipts that had finally told me something, and what they told me was only this: that whatever it was, it was old, and it was paid for, and it had been paid for by us.

Ana got up. She put the kettle on, which is what she does, and while it boiled she stood at the counter with her back to me and said, "The golf man. Julian. He sells them this."

"He sells them the seats. Yes."

"Does he know what's in the annex?"

I said I didn't know that either.

She turned round. "You said he remembered about my grandmother. In October. Six months after you'd mentioned it once."

"Yes."

"Nobody remembers that," Ana said. "I'm good with people and I wouldn't remember that." The kettle clicked. She didn't move. "Men don't remember that unless it's their job."

I didn't have an answer. I had a feeling, and the feeling was the one I get at work when two graphs that shouldn't be related start moving together, and I'd learned long ago that the feeling is usually nothing, and that the times it isn't are the only times that matter.

Buddy woke up and looked at me, and then at Ana, and then he got up, with the grunt, and went and stood at the window. At eleven at night. Watching the street.

"He does that now," Ana said. "I told you."

"You did."

I got up off the floor and went and stood next to the dog, and looked out at nothing, at a quiet street with parked cars on it, and I put my hand on his head, and I thought about a line of small type that said he wasn't accommodated, and for the first time since a Tuesday on this floor I was not curious.

I was frightened. And I want it on the record that it was the dog that did it, not the invoice. The invoice was just the receipt.
