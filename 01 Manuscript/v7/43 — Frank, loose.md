# Frank, loose

Frank came round on a Thursday because I asked him to, which I'd never done, and he knew it, and he brought beer anyway, which is how I knew he was worried.

Ana was home. She'd been home a week, with Mora, who had eaten a cushion at her mother's and shown no remorse, and the house had gone back to being a house: two dogs, a kettle, somebody upstairs. I'd forgotten how much of my thinking I do against the sound of another person in a building. On my own, for those weeks, I'd thought very clearly and very fast and I'd got to some places I wouldn't have got to otherwise, and I'd also sat on a kitchen floor for four nights, and I'd stopped being able to tell which of those was the point.

Frank sat at the kitchen table. Buddy went and lay under it, across his feet, which he does to people he's decided about, and Frank said, "Oh, hello," and went very still the way people do, and I thought, good, that'll keep him here.

"So," Frank said. "You've got the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You've had the face since about March. Pieter's noticed. Pieter thinks you're job-hunting." He opened a beer. "I told him you don't job-hunt. You'd have to talk to people."

"Thank you."

"So what is it. And if it's the Egyptians, I'm going home."

---

I'd thought for a long time about how to do this. I'd thought about doing it the way I'd done it for Ana, at her mother's, with every object on a tablecloth, and I'd decided against it, because Ana loves me and Frank is a sceptic and those need different things. Ana needed to see that I'd labelled what I couldn't prove. Frank needed to see one thing he couldn't argue with, and nothing else, and to be allowed to argue with it.

So I put one sheet of paper on the table.

It was the spreadsheet. Thirty-one years of quarterly invoices from a foundation to a bank, one line per quarter, *continuity reconciliation (recall index), per flagged person*, with the number of persons and the rate and the total, and the six steps marked in yellow, and the last step, the Monday of the week I'd opened a ticket, marked in red.

"What am I looking at."

"Money."

"I can see it's money. Whose?"

"Ours. The bank pays this. Every quarter. For thirty-one years. To a foundation that owns the recovery site. For a service called reconciliation, billed per person, and the number of persons goes up in steps, and the steps are clean multiples, and the last step was the week I asked about an orphan job in stand-up."

Frank looked at it. He's good with numbers; it's why he's good at his job and why he was hired to replace mine. He looked at the steps and I watched him see what I'd seen, which is that money doesn't move like that unless somebody's turning a dial.

"Per flagged person," he said.

"Yes."

"Flagged how?"

I told him about the list. I'd decided to, in the car back from Zwolle. Not the file; the shape. A nightly job. A hundred and twelve lines. Two words each. My own number on it the night after I'd opened my mouth.

He didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "The job I don't remember."

"The job you don't remember."

"I've thought about that," Frank said. "The stand-up. I've gone back over it. I remember the stand-up. I remember Pieter being Pieter about something. I don't remember you saying anything about a job, and I've tried, and I can't get it, and that's not—" He stopped. "That's not like me. I remember stand-ups. I remember what people had for lunch."

"I know."

"So either you didn't say it, or I've been—" He made a small gesture at his own head and didn't finish, and Buddy shifted under the table, and Frank looked down at the dog and then back at the sheet of paper, and I saw him decide to stay on the paper, because the paper was a thing he could do something with.

---

What happened next I hadn't planned, and I want to describe it accurately because it's the only time in this whole story that two people who like each other talked about the actual thing.

Frank asked what the foundation did. I told him what I knew: an archive, a reading room, a building on top of a shed. He asked why an archive would want a list of people who'd asked questions. I said I didn't know. He asked what questions. I said, as far as I could tell from the two-word reasons, any question, about anything, as long as it was the kind of question that doesn't have an answer in the building. Old books. Dead people. What happens after. He asked why anyone would count that.

And I said, because I'd been sitting on it for a month and it had to come out somewhere: "Suppose you wanted to know how many people were awake."

"Awake."

"Not asleep. Not—" I didn't have the word. I've still not got the word; I've since learned that everyone who's thought about this has a different one and that the different words are half the problem. "Suppose there's a thing most people don't look at. Death. What it is. Whether there's anything. And suppose most people have agreed not to look at it because looking at it is unbearable, and the agreement is what holds everything together. Work. Mortgages. Showing up on Monday."

"The wall of comfort," Frank said. He'd heard me say it. Everyone's heard me say it.

"And suppose somebody, somewhere, thought it was important to know how many people had stopped agreeing. Not to punish them. Just to know. The way you'd want to know how many people in a building had stopped believing in the fire alarm."

Frank drank some beer. "That's a thermostat."

"What?"

"You're describing a thermostat. A thing that measures a temperature and does something when it crosses a line. You've got the measuring. You've got the invoice for the measuring. What's the something?"

I didn't answer. I had the schedule in a folder in a bag and eleven addresses on it and I wasn't going to put that on the table, not yet, not with Frank, who had children.

He saw me not answer. He let it go. That's the thing about Frank that nobody who's only met him at a barbecue would guess: he knows when to stop pushing, and he does it as a kindness, not a tactic.

"Okay," he said. "Let me do you the favour of taking it seriously for ten minutes. Then I'm going to tell you what's wrong with it."

---

He took it seriously. I'd never seen him do that before with anything of mine, and I'd like it on the record that he was better at it than I was.

He said: suppose the count is real. Suppose there's a nightly job in a bank that lists people who've asked. Fine. You can't read minds. All you can read is what people write down. So the thing you're counting isn't *awake*; it's *awake and talking*. The quiet ones never appear. Which means whatever the thermostat does, it does it to the people who said something, and leaves the people who didn't. "Which is a very old idea," Frank said. "You don't need a conspiracy for that. Every institution on earth does it by accident. The people who speak up get managed."

He said: suppose it's not an accident. Then the interesting question isn't who's counting. It's why the count would need to be *secret*. "If I wanted to know how many people in a building were scared of dying, I'd run a survey. Nobody runs a secret survey unless the number itself is dangerous. Unless the thing they're afraid of is the number getting out." He tapped the sheet. "Which fits your steps. The steps aren't inflation. The steps are the number crossing a line, and somebody paying more because it did."

And then he said the thing I've thought about every day since.

"You know what this reminds me of? Not the books. The other thing. The thing you and I never talk about because it makes us look like the people we take the piss out of." He turned the beer bottle on the table. "There's a version of physics, respectable people say it, Nobel people, where the world only resolves when it's looked at. The electron doesn't have a position until you measure it. Fine. Everyone's heard that. But there's a stupider version that I've never been able to get out of my head, which is: what if it's not the electron. What if it's everything. What if the world is only rendered where there's an observer, like a game only draws the room you're standing in."

"You've been reading."

"I read things. Don't tell Pieter." He almost smiled. "The point is: in a system like that, the system knows who's logged in. It has to. It's rendering for them. It knows which rooms are being looked at. What it *can't* do—" he held up a finger "—is see what they see. It knows you're in the room. It doesn't know what you're thinking about the furniture."

"A count without a camera."

"A count without a camera." He sat back. "That's your list, James. A hundred and twelve people logged in. The system doesn't know what they found. It just knows they looked."

Buddy sighed under the table, enormously, the way he does when humans have been talking too long.

---

"Now," Frank said. "What's wrong with it."

He told me. He was thorough. He said that every step on my spreadsheet could be a renegotiated contract, that foundations are badly run and bill in round numbers because somebody's nephew does the accounts, that *flagged* is a word consultants use for anything, that the orphan job could be exactly what Pieter said, that my badge number appearing on a list the night after I'd asked about the list was the single most obvious thing a diagnostic tool would do, because I'd *asked about it*, and that if I showed this to a psychologist they'd nod a lot and ask about my father.

"And the thing with the book," he said. "The sentence. Transmitted, arisen. I believe you found it. I believe it's real. I think it's a variant reading and a dead scholar's second thoughts, and I think you've built a cathedral on it because you needed somewhere to put what you felt at that funeral, and I say that as your friend, and as someone who's watched you do it with smaller things for three years."

I didn't argue. I'd heard it all before, in my own head, in my own voice, which is better at it than Frank's.

"That's the honest version," he said. "I don't believe it."

"Okay."

"I don't believe any of the story." He put his finger on the sheet, on the red cell. "I believe the invoice."

I looked at him.

"That's the bit I can't do anything with," Frank said. "I've sat here for forty minutes trying to make that number be something boring and I can't. Consultants don't bill in clean multiples for thirty-one years. Nephews don't do accounts that neat. And nobody, nobody, steps a contract up by exactly one unit on the Monday of the week a monitoring engineer opens a ticket about a cron job, unless the unit is the engineer." He took his finger off. "Everything else you've said, I can explain. That, I can't. And I've learned, from watching you, that the thing you can't explain is the thing."

"The memo's the table," I said.

"Don't quote me at me."

---

He left at eleven. At the door he said, "What are you going to do with it."

"Show the people on the list what they're paying for."

"Which list."

"The one with the children on it."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him decide not to ask, and I was grateful.

"Pieter's got kids," he said.

"I know."

"Is Pieter on it?"

"Yes."

He nodded slowly. He looked back into the kitchen, where Buddy had got up from under the table and was standing at the window now, in the dark, watching the street.

"He does that," Frank said.

"He does that."

"Since when?"

"Since a man started walking down it."

Frank looked at the dog for a while. Then he said, "I still don't believe it," and he went out to his car, and sat in it for a minute before he drove away, and I know that because I watched him from the window with my hand on the old dog's head, the two of us standing there like a pair of guards who've never been told what they're guarding.

Ana came down. "Well?"

"He believes the invoice."

"Is that good?"

"It's Frank," I said. "It's the best he's got. It's better than I had."
