# The corporate day

The bank has a golf day, which it has never once had.

I need to explain that, because it's the kind of sentence that gets you a look. Every spring an email goes round about the golf day. Every spring there's a form, and a date, and a course an hour away with a name like a retirement home, and every spring, two weeks before, a second email goes round saying that in light of the current environment the golf day has been postponed, and nobody is surprised, because the current environment is always in light of something. I've worked there eleven years. I've filled in the form eleven times. I don't know why. I think it's the same reason people buy lottery tickets, which is that filling in the form is the enjoyable part and you'd rather not have it taken from you by realism.

This year, nobody postponed it.

I found out on the Thursday, when a calendar invitation arrived with a tee time on it, an actual tee time, 09:40, and a note from the organiser saying I was in a three-ball and to please confirm my handicap. I sat at my desk and read it twice. Then I did something I'm not proud of, which is that I got up and went to the window and looked out at the car park for a while, the way you do when something good has happened and you don't want anyone to see your face.

It had been a month since the list. Three weeks since Groningen. I'd told Ana, that night, as much as I could put into sentences, which turned out to be about forty per cent of it, and she'd listened, and asked two questions, both of which were better than any I'd asked myself, and then she'd said, "Okay. So what do we do," and I'd said I didn't know, and she'd said, "Then we go to bed," and we had. It was the single most useful conversation of my adult life and it had lasted eleven minutes. Since then I'd been living in the forty per cent and watching the other sixty from a distance, like weather.

And here was a day of golf. A whole day. Outdoors. With a scorecard, which is a document that makes no claims about anything except how badly you played.

I confirmed my handicap. I rounded it down, which is a lie, but a traditional one.

---

The course was an hour north, flat and windy, the kind of Dutch course that's a polder with ambitions. Buddy wasn't allowed, which I'd known and had still, on the morning, half thought about arguing. Ana walked me to the car with Mora at her heel and said, "Try to speak to a human," and I said I'd try, and she said, "A human, James, not a man who wants to tell you about his new wedges," and I said that was a very narrow definition of human, and she kissed me and said go.

I was in a three-ball with two men from Treasury. I'd never met them. I knew within forty seconds that I wasn't going to meet them now either, because the first thing one of them said, as the cart girl went past with the drinks, was something about her that I'm not going to write down, and the second one laughed, and they both looked at me to see if I'd laugh too, and I did the thing I do, which is smile in a way that could be read either way and look at my glove.

Fine. I've played with worse. You play your own ball. That's the whole beauty of golf and the reason I never did team sports: nobody else's behaviour changes your score. I teed off, badly, and walked after it, and the Treasury men talked to each other about a boat, and the wind came across the flat land with nothing to stop it, and for about six holes I was as close to happy as I'd been since a Tuesday on a kitchen floor.

Then I saw him on the practice green.

---

I'm going to be careful here, because I've since gone back over this moment more times than is healthy, and I want to tell you what I saw and not what I know now.

What I saw was a man from the spring. The stranger the starter had paired me with on a Wednesday in April, the one with the swing I'd have paid for, who'd asked about Buddy before he asked my name. I'd driven home that day giddy. I'd told Ana about him, which I never do; she'd said, "You've made a friend," in the voice she uses for the dogs when they've done something clever, and I'd pretended to be offended and wasn't.

He was on the practice green in a company polo, putting, with a vendor's lanyard round his neck, the blue ones they give the sponsors. He looked up as my three-ball came past on the way to the seventh. He saw me. And his face did the thing that I would like, more than almost anything, to have been real, which is that it lit up.

"James!"

He came over. He shook my hand with both of his. He asked after the dogs, by name, both of them, and I felt the Treasury men behind me recalibrating something.

"What are you doing here?"

"Working, I'm afraid." He lifted the lanyard like it was a joke. "I sell you people things."

"What things?"

"Continuity. Dull stuff. If your building burns down on a Tuesday, who does your payroll on Wednesday. Where do you all sit. Who's got the keys." He grinned. "I'm the man who sells the bank its seats. Disaster recovery. Lobbying, mostly, if I'm honest; I talk to people like your head of risk about things that will never happen until they do. It's a living. The golf's the only good part."

I said I'd never known that about him. He said I'd never asked, and that most people didn't, and that he'd found that the less you said about continuity the more people trusted you to provide it. He said it lightly. Everyone laughed. I laughed.

He knew the organiser, it turned out. Of course he did. And when I came off the ninth the three-ball had been rearranged, the Treasury men paired off with each other and a sponsor, and I was walking to the tenth tee with the man from the spring, whose name, I should say, was Julian, and whose surname I didn't catch that day because he said it the way people say surnames they expect you to forget.

---

The back nine was the best golf I've ever played. I'd like to say that was the company and it probably was.

He was good, properly good, and generous with it the way good players sometimes are and usually aren't. He didn't give me tips. He watched me hit a seven iron thin into a bunker and said, "I'd have done exactly the same," which was a lie and a kindness in the same breath. He talked about nothing. The wind. The course. A client in Antwerp who'd insisted on a recovery site with a view. He asked about work, and I said it was fine, and he let it go, and I noticed him letting it go, and I liked him for it.

At the fourteenth he asked about Ana.

Not *how's the wife.* He asked how she was doing, since the spring, since her grandmother. He remembered. I'd mentioned it once, in April, on a tee box, in the half-sentence you give a stranger, and he'd kept it, and here it was, in October, offered back to me carefully like something I'd lent him.

I told him more than I'd told anyone. Not about the book; I had enough sense left for that. About Ana. About how the grief had changed shape, from a thing that lived with us to a thing that visited, and about the knitting she hadn't finished, and about how I'd been bad at it, at the funeral, at saying the thing people say. He listened. He didn't fill the silences. And when I was done he said, "You're hard on yourself, James. Most people never notice they're lying at a funeral. You noticed and it's still bothering you in October. That's not a defect. That's the thing most people are missing."

I three-putted the fourteenth. I didn't care.

---

There was a dinner afterward, in the clubhouse, the kind with a raffle. He had to leave before it; he had a thing, he said, another client, the glamorous life. We stood in the car park in the last of the light with the wind dropping and he said we should play again, properly, not at a corporate thing, and I said yes before he'd finished the sentence.

"Bring the old boy," he said. "Buddy. He can ride in the cart."

I said he'd like that.

"And James." He had his car keys in his hand and he turned them over once. "If work's ever not fine. You've got my number now." He nodded at my phone, where he'd put it, on the thirteenth, while I was looking for a ball. "Continuity's a small world. Sometimes I hear things before they happen."

I said I'd remember that. He drove off in something German and quiet, and I stood in the car park a while longer than I needed to.

---

Ana rang while I was still there.

"Did you speak to a human?"

"I did."

"A real one?"

"Julian. The golf man. From the spring. He was there. He works for a vendor, he sells us our—" and I stopped, because I was about to say *continuity* and the word had arrived in my mouth with a weight it hadn't had an hour before, and I couldn't have told you why. "Disaster planning. Boring. He asked after you. He remembered about your grandmother."

"He remembered?"

"He asked how you were doing. By name. Six months later."

There was a pause.

"That's nice," Ana said, and it was, and it was also the voice she uses when she's filing something. "Was he nice?"

"He was great. I played the best back nine of my life."

"Then I'm glad." Mora barked in the background at something only Mora could see. "Come home. Buddy's been at the window since four. He does that now. Did you know he does that?"

I said I didn't.

"He stands at the window and watches the street like he's expecting someone. I don't know who."

I drove home in the dark with the clubs in the back and the wind pushing the car around on the dyke road, and I thought about a man who remembered everything you told him and asked about your dog before he asked your name, and I thought, that's what a friend is, that's what they're like, I'd just forgotten, and I believed it, and it was the last time for a long while that I was simply happy.

I want that on the record too. It was a good day. I'm not going to let what came after take it off me.
