# Leiden, Friday

He came at two, because two is when people have eaten and not yet started wanting to go home, and he had a clipboard, and he wiped his feet.

Noor's studio was the ground floor of a house on a street of houses, with a bench by the window and a lamp on an arm, and she was at the bench when he knocked, and she saw him through the glass, and she did not pretend not to know him.

"Julian."

"Noor." He smiled. It was a real smile; that was always the problem with him. "It's been a while. May I?"

She let him in. She has asked herself since why she let him in, and the honest answer is that it did not occur to her not to. He was a man she had made tea for once in an empty building, and he had been, that afternoon, the only person in nine years who had sat with her in silence and come out the other side changed, and some part of her had been glad of him ever since, the way you are glad of a stranger who once held a door.

He admired the studio. He meant it. He asked about the sheet on the bench, which was a seventeenth-century letter with a tear across the signature, and he asked the right question, which was not *what is it* but *what will you do*, and she told him, because it was interesting and because he was interested, and he listened without waiting.

Then he opened the clipboard.

---

He did not threaten her. She has told this to one person, and she told it to him carefully, and the care was in that sentence: *he did not threaten me.* He did something that she still, a year on, cannot find the right word for, and the nearest she has come is *itemised*.

He had a list of her clients. Not a long list; she is a conservator in a small city and there are perhaps forty people and institutions who send her work, and he had them, with dates, with what she had done for each and what she had charged. He read a few aloud, not all, in the tone of a man confirming a delivery address. The university library. Two auction houses. A museum in Haarlem. The archive of a foundation whose name she knew, because it was the foundation that had closed the library nine years ago, and because, she understood now, watching him turn the page, it had been her best client ever since.

He had her accounts. Not the figures; the shape. He knew which quarter had been thin and which client had paid late, and he mentioned the thin quarter, lightly, as one mentions weather.

He had her mother.

Not a photograph. A name, and the name of the care home in Oegstgeest, and the name of the ward, and the fact, which Noor had not told anyone, that the place had come up after a nine-month wait and that there were, she had been told, four people behind her on the list. He did not say anything about the four people. He said he was glad her mother was settled. He said it in exactly the voice he had used, nine years ago, to say *thank you for the tea*.

And then he closed the clipboard, and put it on the bench beside the seventeenth-century letter, and he said the only thing that could, if you were being very generous, be called the point.

"There's a man called James," he said. "I think you've met. I like him. I'd like him to be all right." He turned his tea mug a quarter turn on the bench, which was a thing he did. "The best way for him to be all right is for everyone who's spoken to him to have spoken to him about paper. Which is what you do. Which is all you've done." He looked at her. "Isn't it."

She did not answer. She had learned, from a professor, a long time ago, what a silence is for.

He waited. He was good at waiting; she had taught him, in a way, which was a thought she put down and has not picked up. And when it was clear that she was not going to speak, he nodded, as if she had, and said, "Good. That's good, Noor," and stood up, and thanked her for the tea.

She had made him tea. She noticed that only after he had gone. At some point in the afternoon, without deciding to, she had got up and filled the kettle and made him tea in the mug she keeps for clients, and he had drunk it, and she had not been afraid of him for one second of it, and that was the thing she could not forgive herself for.

---

He left at twenty to three. He had timed it; she saw him glance at the clock on the wall as he picked up the clipboard, the way you check a parking meter.

She sat at the bench until it got dark. She did not work. The letter with the torn signature lay where it was, and she looked at it, and thought about a man who had once sat in an empty library and been made new over a mug of tea, and had gone out into the world with the new thing in him, and had used it, every day since, for this.

The professor had been right. He had been right about everything, which was his tragedy and hers. *Never publish, never ask, always hide.* She had kept the first two for nine years. She had broken the third twice, once in her kitchen and once on a Wednesday night on the phone, for a man with a dog who had asked her a question on a forum, and this was the invoice.

Her phone was on the bench. There were two messages on it from James, from that morning, after the call: *thank you*, and then, an hour later, *are you all right? ring me*. She had rung him on Wednesday night and said the name, Kalder, the man by the heater, he oversaw the library, and she had been, for the length of that call, the professor's student and not his warning. She looked at the second message for a long time. Then she turned the phone off, and did not turn it on again.

Quiet is a thing you can be made. She knows that now. She knew it before; the professor told her. But there is a difference between being told a thing by a dead man and having it done to you, on a Friday, by someone kind, with a clipboard, who wipes his feet.
