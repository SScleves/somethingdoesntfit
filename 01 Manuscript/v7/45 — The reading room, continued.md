# The reading room, continued

The generator stopped.

I don't know how long it ran. Long enough for the tray to stop vibrating, which it had been doing, very slightly, against the table; I'd been watching the ledger shiver. The old man had waited for it to finish the way you wait for a lorry to pass before you go on with a sentence. The woman by the window hadn't moved at all.

"Tests," he said. "They test it on Tuesdays. I've asked them not to and they tell me it has to be Tuesdays. One doesn't argue with engineers." He smiled at me. "Does one."

I had a bag by my chair. I'd brought it up the stairs past Aylin, who had looked at it and said nothing, and I'd put it down beside the chair the way you'd put down a dog you'd been told to leave outside and hadn't. I reached into it now.

"You said everyone who gets this far finds the seats."

"I did."

"I didn't come about the seats."

I put the first sheet on the table, next to the ledger, on the professor's side of the tray.

---

I'd thought about how to do this for three weeks and in the end I did it the way I'd do it at work, which is in the order the evidence arrived, with the newest first, because the newest is what they'd least expect me to have, and because I wanted to see which one made the woman by the window move.

Page 212. A photocopy of one page from a four-hundred-page ruleset shipped with a monitoring product sold to every bank in the country, and on it, in the vendor's own language, a rule telling the tool never to report jobs of exactly one shape, and the shape was the job I'd found, and under the rule a review date, end of quarter. "A blind spot," I said. "Sold as a default. Every company that bought the tool bought it. Nobody who bought it knows it's there."

He glanced at it. He didn't pick it up. "Products are complicated," he said. "I'm told."

The accounts. The foundation's own, published, as charities must. Thirty-one years of grants, and beside each year a number I'd got from somewhere else, and the pattern that fell out when you put them side by side: that every grant to a documentary company, a publisher, a festival, a retreat in the hills with a name like a yoghurt, fell in a year when the other number was low. "You fund nonsense when too few people are asking," I said. "And you stop when enough are. It's a thermostat. You've got a heating half."

He looked at that one a little longer. "We fund a great many things," he said. "Some of them are embarrassing. That's philanthropy."

The hearings. I didn't have paper for this one; I'd watched it on the bar TV at the pub a month earlier, a parliament somewhere releasing files, a general saying that forty per cent of the sightings had no explanation and that the public could draw its own conclusions, and the ticker underneath already saying *no evidence of*. "Every admission comes bundled with a finding of nothing," I said. "Every time. For seventy years. That's not how disclosure looks. That's how a thermostat looks when the needle's high."

He said nothing to that. He looked, if anything, pleased, the way a teacher looks when a student has got to the bit of the syllabus the teacher likes.

The forum. My own account, twelve years old, with my deleted question restored and a paragraph about bloodlines added that I'd never written, and my correction underneath, seen by eleven people. "You farm them," I said. "The places where people ask. You don't shut them down. You make them stupid, so that anyone who asks a real question is standing next to a man in a tinfoil hat."

The invoice. The thirty-one-year sheet, with the steps in yellow and the last step in red. I put it down and I said the date, and for the first time the woman by the window turned her head.

Not much. A few degrees. Enough that I saw her face properly for the first time, and it was a face I knew from a photograph on an intranet page: Anneke de Wit, head of risk, who had approved that invoice, or whose department had, thirty times, and who was sitting in a reading room on a Tuesday in December watching an engineer from her own bank put it on a table.

"Per flagged person," I said. "The last step was the week I opened a ticket."

She didn't speak. But she looked at the sheet, and she kept looking at it, and the old man noticed that, and something in the room changed temperature.

And then the last one, which I'd saved, and which I put down without a word: the schedule. The quarterly field schedule, with its column headed *Visit*, and eleven addresses, and what had happened at seven of them, in my handwriting, in the margin. Heart. Sold. Fire. Quiet. Quiet. Quiet. Quiet. And the eighth, with a date nine days from the day I'd got it, and in my handwriting beside it: *I was there. Nobody came. Why.*

---

He read the schedule. He read all of it. He took longer over my handwriting than over the typed part, and when he was done he put it down square on the tray beside the ledger and he sat back and he did something I hadn't expected, which was laugh.

Not at me. It was a small, delighted sound, the laugh of an old man who's been shown a card trick and seen how it's done and likes it more for that.

"Oh, this is very good," he said. "This is very, very good. Anneke, isn't this good?"

She didn't answer.

"You've done the modern part," he said to me. "That's the part nobody does. They all come in here with the old stuff. The pyramids. The flood. The thing in Turkey. They sit where you're sitting and they tell me about Sumerians, and I listen, because it's polite, and it's never once been interesting, because the old stuff is a *result*. It's what's left when the thing has been done. You've brought me the thing being done. Page two hundred and twelve." He said it like a vintage. "A rule in a product. I didn't know that one myself, I'll be honest. I'm told things, I don't read manuals."

"Is it yours?"

"Mr Klippe." He folded his hands. "I'm going to do you the courtesy of not insulting you, and I'd ask the same. You've put seven pieces of paper on this table and asked me, in effect, to confirm each one, and I'm not going to, because I don't need to, because you already know, and because each one of them is a thing that could be explained in a court by a bored man in an hour. A vendor's default. A charity's grants. A forum. An invoice for a service you don't understand. A list of addresses and some sad things that happened in the east of the country in an autumn." He tilted his head. "You know what they are. I know what they are. What they are *in a room* is nothing."

"And the schedule."

"The schedule is a schedule." He said it gently. "We move records for conservation. We visit people. Sometimes the people we visit are old, and sometimes old people die. You went to the eighth address and nobody came. Did it occur to you that nobody came because you'd gone?"

It had. It had occurred to me on the drive home.

"I'll tell you what I will confirm," he said, "because it's old, and because it's true, and because I'm vain and you've earned it. The method. You've got the method right, and almost nobody does."

---

He talked for a while then. I'm going to give you the shape of it and not the words, because the words were his and he enjoyed them too much, and because I don't want them in your head in his voice.

He said: stories were always the tool. Not force. Force is expensive and leaves marks and makes martyrs, and a martyr is a story you can't control. What you want is to own the story, and the way to own a story is to be the one who tells the other version. Every time a thing is seen that shouldn't be, you don't deny it. You tell it back, bigger, sillier, with a man in a mask standing next to it, until the true version can't be said in a room without the room thinking of the mask.

He said: every new way of telling stories had frightened them, for a while. Print. The newspapers. Radio. Film. Television. Each one, for a decade or two, had looked like the end, because each one let people say things to each other faster than the other version could be told. And each one, in the end, had become theirs. Not by taking it over. By *using* it. By being the best customer. "The cinema," he said, "was the greatest gift we were ever given, and we didn't give it to ourselves. We just bought the most tickets."

He said: the internet had frightened them more than anything since the printing press, for about five years. And then it had turned out to be the best thing that had ever happened to them, because it didn't make people believe the wrong things. It made them doubt everything. And a person who doubts everything is a person who will never, ever, finish a sentence that begins *based on what*.

"That's the method," he said. "It hasn't changed in four hundred years. We're not clever. We're consistent."

---

"Now," he said, and he unfolded his hands, and I understood that the part he'd enjoyed was over. "You've put your papers on my table. Let me put mine."

He didn't put anything down. He didn't need to.

"There are seats in the building below us. You've sat in one. Forty of them belong to your employer, under a contract you've read, with an annex you've read, and the annex has names on it and a column that bothers you. I'm going to tell you something about that column, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then you're going to go home, and I'd like you to think before you answer, because I'll only make it once."

"The column."

"The column is real. The building is real. The thing the building is for is real, and it is not what you think, and I'm not going to tell you what it is, because you'd tell your wife, and she'd tell her mother, and it would be in a forum by Thursday next to the bloodlines." He smiled. "What I'll tell you is that it's old, and that it's coming, and that we have never once, in four hundred years, been wrong about the date. The people on that list are not being rewarded. They're being *kept*. Because somebody will need to do the payroll on the Wednesday, Mr Klippe. That's all continuity ever is."

"And the offer."

"Five seats." He said it flatly, like a price. "You. Your wife. Her mother, if you like, I'm told they're close. Two more, your choice; a friend, a colleague, the man who sells curtains, I don't care. Five names on Annex C, dependants per allocation, in a building you've already got a desk in."

I waited.

"No dogs," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm told it's a question of air."

---

I'd like to tell you I thought about it. I didn't, and that's not courage; it's that I'd already thought about it, for three weeks, on a kitchen floor, with the dog in question asleep across my feet, and I'd found that there was a sentence underneath all the other sentences, and the sentence was Ana's, from a kitchen floor at four in the morning, and it was *run where, I've got a Doberman.*

"No," I said.

He nodded, as if I'd confirmed a delivery time. The woman by the window had turned back to the window.

"May I ask why? Not to persuade you. I'm curious. Most people, at this table, take a day."

I looked at the tray. At the ledger from 1931, open at the page where one line was struck through and another written beside it. At my seven sheets of paper. At a book I'd never seen before, on the far corner of the tray, thin, with no title, that had been in my kitchen until a night in November when a dog went down at the gate.

"Because you asked about the dog before you asked my name," I said. "All of you. Every time. It's the first thing you do. And I've worked out why."

For the first time, the old man didn't have a face ready.

"You count people who've stopped being afraid," I said. "That's the right column. That's what the handlers are for. And you can't see it directly, so you look for the thing that goes with it. And the thing that goes with it is love. Not the big kind. The stupid kind. The kind that gets up at two in the morning because an old dog's legs have gone. Because a man who loves a dog that much has already done the arithmetic about losing it, and come out the other side, and that's the arithmetic you're afraid of." I stood up. "You can't have the seat without the dog. Not because I'm brave. Because the dog's the only reason I'd be worth a seat."

I picked up my bag. I left the papers on the table. They were copies.

At the door Aylin looked up from the card index and said, "Mr Klippe. Same time Thursday?" in the voice of a woman who books rooms, and I said yes, and I don't know why I said yes, and I went down the stairs past the glass door and through forty desks and out into a car park where it had started, very lightly, to snow.

Buddy was in the car. He'd got up. He was standing on the back seat with his nose against the window, watching the door I'd come out of, and when he saw me his tail went, once, and he lay down again, with the grunt, like a foreman who's seen the shift end.

I sat in the driver's seat for a long time before I started the engine.

I'd said no. I'd said it to a man who had never been wrong about a date. And I had, in my bag, a copy of a schedule with three dates still on it, and one of them was ours, and the word in the notes column was still *companion*, and now they knew that I knew, and I'd given them, for free, the one thing I'd kept back from everyone, including Ana, including Frank, including myself until I'd heard it come out of my mouth in that room.

I'd told them what I loved.

They already knew. That was the whole point. But I'd confirmed it, out loud, to their faces, and in my work that's the one unforgivable sin. You never tell a system what you're watching for. It'll give you exactly that, and nothing else, forever.

I drove home in the snow. Part of me, the part that counts things, was already counting the days.
