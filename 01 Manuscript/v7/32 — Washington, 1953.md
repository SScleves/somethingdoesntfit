# Washington, 1953

They met on a Wednesday in January, in a room that belonged to the Central Intelligence Agency, and they were done by Saturday.

Five men. H. P. Robertson, who chaired it, a physicist from Caltech who had spent the war on weapons and the peace on intelligence. Luis Alvarez, who would win the Nobel Prize fifteen years later for something else entirely. Lloyd Berkner, Samuel Goudsmit, Thornton Page. Physicists, an astronomer, the kind of men who are asked to sit on panels because their names end arguments. Two Air Force officers sat in to brief them, and a young astronomer from Ohio State called Allen Hynek, who was the Air Force's consultant on the subject and who would spend the rest of his life changing his mind about it.

The subject was flying saucers. The Agency called them unidentified flying objects, which was newer and sounded less like a joke, and the reason the Agency was interested at all was not that it believed in them. It was that the previous summer, in July, so many people had reported them over Washington that the reports had jammed the channels the Air Force used for real things. Radar operators at National Airport had tracked them. Jets had been scrambled. The newspapers had the story for a week. And somebody at the Agency had looked at the volume of traffic and thought, not *what are they*, but *what would happen if the Russians did this on purpose, the night before they did something else*.

So: five scientists, a long weekend, and a question that was really about the telephone.

---

They watched films. That is documented, and it matters, because the films were the best evidence the Air Force had. One had been taken by a Navy warrant officer in Utah, over Tremonton, the summer before: a cluster of bright dots moving against a blue sky, which Navy photo analysts had spent a thousand hours on and concluded were not birds, not balloons, not aircraft. The panel watched it, and watched it again, and decided they were birds. Seagulls, probably. In the sunlight.

They heard the cases. They heard about radar returns and weather inversions. They heard Hynek, who was not yet the man he would become, say what the Air Force had hired him to say. They were, by every account including their own, unimpressed, and they were unimpressed quickly, in the way of clever men who have been asked to look at something beneath them and intend to be fair about it for exactly as long as it takes.

By Saturday they had a report. It was two and a half pages. It was classified, and stayed classified for twenty-two years, and when it was released, in 1975, people who had spent those twenty-two years being laughed at read it and understood, for the first time, that the laughing had been a decision.

---

The report's own words, because nothing else will do.

The panel found no evidence that the objects were a direct threat to national security. That was the headline, and it was true, and if the report had stopped there nobody would remember it.

It did not stop there. It found that the reports themselves were the threat. That the continued emphasis on them was *resulting in a threat to the orderly functioning of the protective organs of the body politic*. That the channels could be clogged. That the public could be made to panic, or could be trained to ignore real things.

And so it recommended a programme. Of *training and debunking*.

That is the word. It is in the document. *Debunking.*

*The "debunking" aim would result in reduction in public interest in "flying saucers" which today evokes a strong psychological reaction. This education could be accomplished by mass media such as television, motion pictures, and popular articles.*

It suggested who might help. Psychologists familiar with mass psychology. Advertising experts. It suggested, by name, that the Walt Disney company might be approached, because of its experience with the public. It noted that the amateur groups which had formed around the subject, the civilian clubs with their newsletters, *should be watched because of their potentially great influence on mass thinking*.

And it set down a principle that the Air Force would keep for the next sixteen years, in all its public statements, and which the people who ran those statements would later confirm was policy: that the burden of proof was on the sighter, not the explainer.

Then the five men signed it and went home. Robertson to Caltech. Alvarez to Berkeley and, in time, to Stockholm. None of them, as far as anyone has found, ever mentioned it again in public. It was a weekend's work. It was beneath them.

---

Here is what is not in the report, and should be said.

The men who carried it out were not told what it was. The Air Force officers who spent the next decade explaining every light in the sky as Venus or a balloon or marsh gas, with a straight face, to farmers and pilots and policemen who knew what Venus looked like, were not working from the Robertson report. Most of them never saw it. They were working from an instruction, handed down through three or four layers, that said: explain, reassure, reduce. They were given a reason, and the reason was clogged channels and Soviet psychological warfare and the public good, and the reason was believable, because it was partly true.

Hynek saw it. He was in the room. He went on explaining lights as Venus for another thirteen years, and then one night in Michigan in 1966 he said *swamp gas* to a room full of reporters about a thing he did not believe was swamp gas, and the room laughed at him, and something in him finished turning. He spent the rest of his life on the other side. He said later that he had been, in that room in 1953, *a lamb among wolves*, and that he had not understood what he was being used for. He may have been right. It does not matter. The point is that he did the work for thirteen years without needing to understand it, and that the work got done.

Twenty-two years classified. Two and a half pages. Five of the cleverest men in the country, a long weekend, and a recommendation that whatever people were seeing, the thing to do about it was to make them feel stupid for having seen it.

It worked. That is the part nobody argues about. For a generation, in the richest country on earth, a pilot who reported something he could not identify was ending his career, and knew it, and mostly didn't. The clubs with the newsletters became a joke, and the joke became the word, and the word is still the word.

If you want to read it, it is online. It is called the Report of the Scientific Advisory Panel on Unidentified Flying Objects, and it is dated the seventeenth of January, 1953, and it is two and a half pages long, and it has been public for fifty years, and it has never once been news.
