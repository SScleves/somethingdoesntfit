# The other ten

There were eleven addresses on the schedule and one of them was mine, which left ten, and I'd had the list for nineteen days before I let myself look at the other ten properly.

I want to explain the nineteen days, because they're not to my credit. For nineteen days I had a document that said eleven households in the east of the country were due a *visit* in the current quarter, with a date against each, and a column of notes, and I had looked at it the way you look at a bill you can't pay, which is quickly, and only at the line with your own name on. I'd been busy. Ana had gone to her mother's with Mora and I'd stayed with Buddy, because the schedule said *companion* in the notes against our address and because a man had told me once, on a golf course, that continuity was about knowing who's got the keys. I'd sat on a kitchen floor for four nights. Something had happened on the fourth that I'll get to. And when I got up off the floor the fear was gone, and what was left in its place, it turned out, was arithmetic.

Ten addresses. A date against each, and seven of the dates were already past.

---

I did it the dull way, because the dull way is the only way I trust and because it's the way a man who'd just stopped being afraid should check whether he was right to.

Public records. Death notices, which in this country are published in the regional papers and online, and which are searchable if you know the format and have the patience. The land registry, which tells you who owns a house and when it changed hands. The chamber of commerce, for anyone with a business. Court listings. Planning applications. The dullest layer of a country, the layer that never makes the news, the one where a life leaves marks the way a glass leaves rings.

I had names, or most of them. The schedule listed addresses, and addresses in this country are enough.

It took me three evenings and I'm going to give you the results in the order I got them, because the order is the point.

The first address was in Deventer. An older man, a widower, a retired teacher. Died in September. Heart. Found by a neighbour. The notice said *peacefully, at home*, and listed two children and five grandchildren and asked for donations to a heart charity in place of flowers. The date on the schedule was eleven days before the date on the notice.

The second was in Apeldoorn. A woman in her fifties. Not dead. A chamber of commerce record showed her small business, a translation agency, dissolved in October. A planning application in her name for a conversion of the house to two flats, withdrawn. The land registry showed the house sold in November, quickly, below what the neighbours had got. Then nothing. No new address in any record I could reach. The date on the schedule was in September.

The third was a farm outside Hardenberg. A man in his sixties. A fire. October. The regional paper had four paragraphs: an outbuilding, old wiring, nobody hurt, the farmer treated for smoke inhalation and released. And then, two weeks later, a death notice for the same man, which said *suddenly*, and nothing else, and listed no cause, and named a sister. The date on the schedule was the week before the fire.

Three. Each one plausible. I want to say that clearly, because it's what I said to myself at the time and it's true. A heart attack in a man of seventy-eight. A woman selling up and moving away. A farm fire and an old man who'd breathed smoke. If you handed those three to anyone, a policeman, a journalist, Frank, they'd shrug. Things happen. Three things happened in three towns in the east of the country in one autumn. That's not a pattern. That's a region.

The fourth, fifth, sixth and seventh addresses had gone quiet.

That's the only word I've got for it. No death notice. No sale. No dissolved company. But no activity either, nothing new in any record since the date on the schedule, and in two of the four cases a last trace that bothered me: a social media account that had posted every few days for years and stopped; a local sports club's newsletter that had listed a man as treasurer for a decade and in its December issue listed someone else, with no thanks and no farewell.

Four people I couldn't find and couldn't not find. Just quiet.

The eighth, ninth and tenth addresses had dates in the future. I wrote the dates down. I'm not going to tell you what I did with them yet.

---

I rang Ana from the kitchen floor with the laptop open and the sheet in front of me.

"Three dead or gone, four quiet, three to come," I said. "That's the other ten."

She didn't say anything for a moment. I could hear her mother's television in the background, a quiz, the one with the wheel.

"Say it again slower."

I said it again slower.

"And you're sure they're—" She stopped. "No. You're not sure. You can't be sure. That's the whole thing, isn't it. That's how it works."

"That's how it works."

"Three people in the east of the country died this autumn and that's a Tuesday. Four people stopped posting on Facebook and that's a Tuesday too."

"Yes."

"And you're going to tell me you know what a Tuesday looks like."

"I count things for a living, Ana. I know what random looks like. It's clumpier than people think. You get runs. You get three in a row and it means nothing." I looked at the sheet. "This isn't clumpy. This is every date on a list followed within two weeks by a plausible ending. Every one. The ones that aren't dead are gone. That's not a run. That's a schedule being kept."

The quiz audience applauded something.

"James."

"Yes."

"The eighth one. The next date. When is it."

I told her.

"That's nine days."

"Yes."

"And you've got the address."

"Yes."

She breathed out. "Then I suppose you're going to Zwolle."

I said I was going to Zwolle. I said I was going to Zwolle because the farm outside Hardenberg and the teacher in Deventer were past helping, and the four quiet ones were quiet, but the fourth address, the first of the quiet four, was in Zwolle, and it had something the others didn't, which was a second name on the land registry. A wife. And wives, in my experience, answer doors.

"Take Buddy," said Ana.

"He's old."

"He's old and he sits in the car and he makes you look like a man with a dog instead of a man with a question. Take Buddy."

I took Buddy.

---

Zwolle is a nice town and the house was in the nice part of it, a decent house on a street of decent houses, and there was a green Volvo in the drive, old, immaculate, the kind of car that gets washed on Sundays.

I'd thought about what to say for the whole drive and I'd thrown all of it away by the time I rang the bell, because all of it was a lie and I'd stopped being able to tell them. I'd decided to say I was from the pension fund, following up. I'd decided to say I was a journalist. I'd decided to say I'd known her husband. And standing on the step with an old dog sitting patiently at my heel I found that I was going to say the truth, or the forty per cent of it that fit in a doorway, and that I didn't know what that was until she opened the door.

She was small and neat and seventy, and she looked at me, and then at Buddy, and her face did the thing people's faces do when they see an old dog, which is soften before they've decided to.

"I'm sorry to bother you," I said. "My name's James. I'm trying to find out about something that happened to your husband. I think it happened to some other people too. I don't work for anyone. I've got a dog in case that helps."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at the Volvo in the drive.

"You'd better come in," she said. "He likes to be in the kitchen, does he? The dog. Henk's did."

---

I'm not going to tell you everything she told me, because she told it to me and not to you, and because some of it was just a woman talking about her husband to a stranger who'd turned up with a dog, and that's not evidence, that's grief.

I'll tell you the parts that were evidence.

A man had come. In the autumn. About the pension. He'd rung ahead and said when, and he'd arrived when he said, and he'd wiped his feet. He'd been kind. Henk had said so, that night, on the phone, when she was at her sister's: *a nice man, he listened, he liked the car.* He'd brought his own coffee in a flask and apologised for it. He'd borrowed the Volvo for an hour to move a box, and brought it back washed. And he'd left the flask.

She got it down from the shelf above the coats and put it on the table between us. Steel. Unmarked. The kind you can buy in any shop.

"Nobody came back for it," she said. "The pension people said they'd never sent anyone. I thought, well, somebody did. I kept it in case." She looked at it. "I don't know in case of what."

Three days after the nice man, Henk had driven out along the canal road toward Hasselt on a Sunday morning while she was at church, and had gone off the long straight into the water, in daylight, at no great speed. The police had said *distracted*. The car had had nothing wrong with it. She knew that because she'd had it checked, afterwards, by the mechanic in Kampen that Henk had trusted, who had gone over it for a whole day and come back and told her, in the voice of a man who wished he had something else to say, that it was the best-kept Volvo he'd seen in twenty years.

"He wasn't distracted," she said. "He'd been happy. That week. Happier than since his father. He'd been talking about his father again, the notebooks, the bridge, all of it, and I'd been—" She stopped. "I'd been telling him to leave it. At the bowling club they'd started looking at him. I didn't want them looking at him. I said, Henk, leave it, nobody wants to hear about your father's bridge. And he'd stopped, for a bit. And then that week he'd started again, and he was *happy*, and I thought, oh, let him."

Buddy had gone and lain down under the table, against her feet, the way he does with people he's decided about. She put her hand down and found his head without looking.

"Why would anyone want to hurt Henk," she said. It wasn't a question. It was the thing you say.

---

I didn't tell her. I want to be clear about that, and I want to be clear that I'm not sure I was right.

I had the schedule in my bag. I had her address on it, with a date, and the word in the notes column against her address was not *companion*. It was *pension*. I had three other households that had gone the way hers had, and four more that had gone quiet, and three dates still to come, one of them in nine days. I could have put all of it on her kitchen table next to a flask and said: your husband wasn't distracted, he was scheduled, and the man who liked his car was the schedule.

And then what. She was seventy. She lived alone in a decent house with a Volvo she couldn't drive. The one true thing I had to give her would have taken away the only thing she had left, which was the possibility that it was nobody's fault.

So I said I was sorry. I said her husband sounded like a man I'd have liked. I said I'd come back, if that was all right, when I knew more, and she said it was, and she asked if she could give Buddy a biscuit, and I said he'd never forgive me if she didn't.

At the door she said, "You said other people. That it happened to other people."

"I think so."

"Their wives. Did they keep anything? Like the flask?"

I said I didn't know. I said I'd find out.

"You should," she said. "Somebody should." And she looked at the Volvo, and at the dog, and at me, and she said, "You're not from anyone. I believe that. You've got the face of a man who's not from anyone." And she closed the door gently, the way you do when there's an old dog on the step.

---

I sat in the car for a while before I started it, with Buddy asleep on the back seat and the flask in my bag, because she'd pressed it on me at the end, *take it, I don't want it in the house now*, and I'd taken it.

I rang Ana.

"Well?"

"A man came about the pension. He brought his own coffee. He borrowed the car and brought it back washed. Three days later her husband drove into a canal."

Silence.

"He left the flask, Ana. He left it on purpose. He wanted it in the house. He wanted her to keep it and look at it and think a nice man had been."

"Why?"

"I don't know." I did know. I'd known since Noor's kitchen, since a style guide that left the typos in. You don't clean the plate. You leave the thing that makes it look like nobody was there. "So that it would be nobody's fault."

More silence. Then: "Did you tell her?"

"No."

"Good." A pause. "No. Not good. I don't know. Was that right?"

"I don't know either."

"Come home," Ana said. "I'm coming home too. Tomorrow. Mum's driving me mad and Mora's eaten a cushion." A breath. "And James. The next one. The nine days."

"Yes."

"You're not going to knock on that door with a dog."

"No," I said. "I'm going to find out what they've been told. All of them. Every helper on that list. I'm going to find out what they think they're doing." I looked at the flask on the passenger seat. "And then I'm going to tell them."

I drove home south in the dark past a long straight stretch of canal road with the water black beside it, and this time I slowed down.
