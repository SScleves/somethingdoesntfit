# Chapter One

It was a beautiful day, which felt like the first lie.

I had expected rain. You bury someone, the sky should have the decency to match. Instead the morning came up clean and gold over the cemetery, the grass so green it looked freshly painted, and somewhere behind the hedge two crows were going about their business as if only they knew something had ended.

Ana's hand was in mine. That's the part I remember with the most precision — not the coffin, not the priest, her hand. She wasn't squeezing it. That was the thing. Ana squeezes when she's nervous, or when the plane takes off and I'm nervous, or when the movie gets tense. She wasn't squeezing now. Her hand just lay in mine, open, like something that had been set down and forgotten, and I understood that my wife was somewhere I couldn't follow.

Her grandmother had helped raise her. Whenever the situation at home wasn't ideal, Ana would just go there. I knew the stories the way you know your wife's stories — the knitting, the movies, the lame TV shows they watched under one blanket, the way the old woman would undo whatever she'd just knitted and start it over again, while Ana just found it funny and never once got upset about it. For thirty-eight years that woman had been the fixed point Ana measured everything against. And now six men I didn't really care for were lowering the fixed point into the ground on green straps, slowly, the way you lower something you're afraid of dropping.

I stood there in my one dark suit and did the job you do at funerals when the grief isn't yours. You hold the coat. You keep the water bottle. You position yourself half a step behind and to the left, close enough to touch her, far enough not to crowd her. I was good at it, I think. I had no idea what I was doing.

People came past afterward, in that slow line that forms by itself, and they said the words. Everybody said the words.

*She's resting now.*

*She's in a better place.*

*She's watching over you, Ana. She'll always be with you.*

*This isn't goodbye. You'll see her again.*

*She loved you so much.*

And Ana nodded, and cried, and hugged them, and something in her actually seemed to take the weight of the words and hold it, like the words were bread. And I stood half a step behind and to the left, and I nodded too, and I shook hands, and when it was my turn to say something to Ana's mother I heard my own mouth say, "She's at peace now," in a warm and steady voice.

And a small, cold voice underneath it — a voice I've known my whole life — said: *based on what? What do you know? Are you going to tell yourself that when it's your turn to grieve?*

I want to be clear about something, because this matters for everything that came later. It wasn't cynicism, or me being cold. I wasn't standing at a funeral sneering at the comfort of grieving people. I would have given a year of my life for the words to be true — or for her grandmother to have had that extra year with Ana. But that was exactly the problem. This time I wanted them to be true so badly that maybe that's why it hit me. It was obvious, once you looked. But nobody was looking. Not the priest, not the aunts. That's just what you say when someone dies. We passed the words around without ever asking the simplest question: how do we know?

But I couldn't stop asking that stupid, out-of-place question. I never can. Ana calls it a quality, but Ana loves me, and love is a terrible auditor. Some days I can't decide if I like or hate the constant monologue in here — I catch myself asking the weirdest shit in the world, then asking whether other people ask themselves these things, then working out the reasons they might not. Okay. It's clearly a defect. But hey — it pays the bills.

I work in IT. If I say it right, and I'm lucky enough to have Ana or someone I care about next to me, I can "use" them — divert the attention, let them carry the table. But if I say it wrong and I'm alone, it's small-talk time. It's either that or get shit in the car on the way home for sitting alone at a table like a man quietly finishing his manifesto. Her words, not mine.

So I've become great at it. For two, maybe three solid minutes.

I lean in with the enthusiasm of a man who has never once been bored in his life and say: *tell me something about yourself — not work, not family, something about you.* Most of the time they hand me something I can compliment, or top up with some obscure fact that relates. It almost never fails. People light up like you've found a switch they forgot they had. It's a beautiful question. I stole it from a guy at work, a wannabe gigolo disguised as a spiritual being — but his version was designed to let him linger. Mine I rebuilt, tested, and deployed to let me walk away after three minutes: need to check on my wife, on the dogs, on whatever works that day. And the person on the other end still calls me charming, and never sees me as a threat.

My other move is the did-you-know. The Romans built a working steam engine and shelved it as a toy, because slaves were cheaper. Safe, small, useful for steering a table away from anything that matters. I've got hundreds — but proven ones only. The rule, and I kept it for thirty years, was never the ones I actually wanted to tell. Those I was embarrassed to know in that much detail. Those stayed in the folder.

I get called charming a lot. It feels weird. It's a small magic trick performed by a man who never learned — or liked — the actual magic. I studied conversation from the outside, like everything else, and got so good at the demo that nobody has ever asked for the full version. Some nights I'm not sure there is one.

The honest reason for all the deflecting isn't shyness. It's worse. Most things bore me, and I can't fake interest nearly as well as I fake charm. The renovation, the traffic, the series everyone's watching — my brain stamps them PROCESSED before the sentence ends. There are topics that could keep me happy at a barbecue past nine — what do you think consciousness is? Where do you land on the government quietly admitting its pilots chase things they can't explain? What do you *really* think happens to grandmothers when they check out? — but those are the topics that guarantee you eat alone at the next one. And I still get the car talk anyway. Just a different flavour: on a good night Ana doesn't call me embarrassing. She calls me Mulder.

Work is more of the same. I work with software that watches other software. Nothing I make makes anything. My tools are eyes. Somewhere out there ten thousand systems hum through the night, and my programs sit over them counting breaths, waiting for one to catch. When it catches, I show up. And here's the part that makes me the odd man out even at work, where you'd think I'd finally be among my people: the thing that's dying is never mine. Someone else built it. Someone else named it, loves it, defends it in meetings with their voice cracking. I arrive like a coroner — no history, no introductions, one night, one question: *why are you dying?* — and by Friday I've moved on to the next body, while the owner is still standing in the doorway, wondering why I couldn't just press the magic key.

Fuck. I don't hate my job at all. But fuck.

So there I stood, at the grave of a woman I'd loved mostly through my wife — to be fair, she was my favourite of the whole family — hearing people repeat to Ana and her family *she's at peace, she's in a better place, you'll see her again*, over and over and over... AND OVER again. And in the back of my head, the only thing I could think was:

*See her again — how?*

*Because if that were true, why are we sad?*

Don't get me wrong. It was not a rebellious how. It was a technical how. Where? In what form? By what mechanism? If there's a room at the end of everything where the dead wait for us — her grandmother young again, knitting on the couch — then fine. Wonderful. I'll take it. I'll take it today. But nobody in that line actually seemed to believe in the room. When you pressed the words, even gently, they came apart into something soft, something about energy, and memory, and *she lives on in you* — which, if you look at what most people actually believe, means: she's gone, and we've agreed to say it differently.

There were other options. The one half the planet quietly believes — the one Ana's grandmother herself believed, with her candles and her murmured prayers for the dead — was worse. You die, and then you're judged, and depending on the verdict you might see your loved ones again, or never. For eternity.

Or option number two: the wheel turns and you go around again. Some people say it like it's comfort. I stood at that grave and did the arithmetic for the first time in my life, actually did it, and it took about eleven seconds to see that it's not comfort. It's the opposite of comfort. Because you don't come back as *you*. You come back a stranger, with a stranger's face, in some other decade, some other country, speaking a language your grandmother never knew. And she comes back too — another stranger, somewhere else, on her own schedule. Two strangers who used to be everything to each other, dealt back into the deck at random. They could pass each other tomorrow in an airport and feel nothing. That's not reunion. That's a machine for making sure no one ever finds anyone again.

I stood there in the gold light, holding my wife's forgotten hand, genuinely shaken — not by the death, by the arithmetic. Nobody around me seemed to have done it. Or they'd done it and decided not to look. The wall of comfort hummed along: *you'll see her again, you'll see her again.*

And then the flash came.

I need to be careful here, because I know how this sounds, and at the time it was nothing — I filed it under nothing before it even finished. Grief does strange things, everyone knows that, and I hadn't slept properly in four days. But for a moment — less than a moment, the length of a camera flash — I wasn't standing on warm grass. My cheek was against cold mud. The sky was grey, low, wrong. And a hand was reaching for mine — a specific hand, cracked at the knuckles, a hand I *almost* knew, the way you almost know a word in a language you've forgotten — and then it was gone, and I was back in the sunlight with my heart going hard, and the crows were making a racket in the hedge, and the priest was saying amen.

Lack of sleep. That's all it was. That's what I told myself, and I'm telling you exactly what I told myself, because the difference between those two sentences turned out to matter more than anything else in my life.

The crowd thinned. People drifted toward the cars in twos and threes, lighting cigarettes, becoming ordinary again with a speed that struck me as obscene and also completely human. Ana stayed by the grave a while longer and I stayed half a step behind her, and when she finally turned around, her face undid me — because it wasn't destroyed. It was *hopeful*. Wet, wrecked, and hopeful. She looked at me and said, "She's okay now. Wherever she is, she's okay," and she needed me to say yes. With conviction.

Fuck.

I said yes. It was not the best yes. If I had to put a percentage on it, it sounded about 85% like a yes and 15% like a question. But it looked 100% like an *I don't know*. I suck.

Anyway. I said yes, and I held her, and over her shoulder I looked at the hole in the ground, and I have never in my life felt like a bigger fraud. Not because I'd lied — maybe it was even true, what did I know — but because I'd confirmed something I'd never checked. In my work that's the one unforgivable sin. You never tell people a system is fine because they need it to be fine — even if you don't care about them or the system. And I'd just done it to the person I love most in the world, at the worst moment of her life, because the alternative was to say *I don't know* at an open grave, and no one says that. No one ever says that. Which, when you think about it, is remarkable — the single biggest question there is, the only one guaranteed to apply to every one of us, and we've collectively agreed to handle it with inherited phrases nobody checks.

It was on the drive home that the dog finished me.

We were on the highway, Ana asleep against the window with her mascara dried in two grey lines, and I was thinking about who was waiting for us at home. Two dogs. Mora, six years old, a black Doberman, all ears and no brakes, who greets every return like a resurrection and always, always wants to eat. I wanted to call her Blade. Ana said that was fucked up. I'm still not sure why, and I've stopped asking, because the way she said it told me the answer was obvious to everyone but me. And Buddy. Old Buddy, grey to the eyebrows now, twelve and slowing, who last month stopped trying to follow me up the stairs and now just watches me go from the bottom step with a patience that breaks something in me every single time.

And the thought arrived, the way the worst thoughts do — complete, quiet, in my own voice:

*Everyone in that line promised Ana she'd see her grandmother again. Every religion on the menu has the reunion covered. But what about you, old man? Nobody even pretends there's a heaven for you.*

Nobody stands at a dog's grave and says *this isn't goodbye*. There's no wall of comfort for dogs. Twelve, thirteen years of the most uncomplicated love a person ever receives, and when it ends the whole vast machinery of human comfort just shrugs: *he was a good boy*. That's it. That's the entire theology. And if the machinery has nothing to say about Buddy — who loved us more purely than most of the people at that funeral loved anyone — then there are at least three options. One: the machinery is wrong. Two: the machinery knows something that makes dogs not matter. Three: the machinery was never about knowledge at all. It was about making the silence survivable.

I drove, and Ana slept, and for the first time in my life I actually dared to think about the unthinkable — the one thing I'd always thought *about* but never allowed myself to really *think*. Death. My own. My father's. And when the time comes, my mum. Buddy. Ana.

There are a dozen places I could have started this story. Other funerals. A hospital corridor at sixteen. Every one of them I got to face alone, and being alone with something means you're allowed to not look at it. This time I wasn't alone. Somebody I loved needed me to have an answer, out loud, with conviction — and that's when I found out I didn't have one. So this is where it starts. Not the first time I met death. The first time I had to be responsible in front of it.

I didn't make a vow. I'm not the type. Vows are for people who trust their future selves.

But somewhere between the cemetery and our front door, without a word, without a plan, in the driver's seat of my grey car with my sleeping wife beside me and two dogs waiting behind a door — one of them slower every month — the search began.

I just didn't know yet that someone had already noticed people like me.

And that they'd been noticing us for a very long time.
