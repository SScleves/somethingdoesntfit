# The schedule

I brought it home because I couldn't photograph it. That's the only reason. Meerwijk's old ledgers are in a room with a camera and a woman called Mrs Dekker who has been in that room since before the camera, and the one thing you can't do in that room is take a picture, and the one thing you *can* do, if you've got the role, is sign a document out for forty-eight hours in a folder with the house's name on it.

So I signed it out. Thirty pages. *Continuity transfer schedule, Q4.* The thing the monthly payment had been buying for thirty-one years, finally, in my hands: what the foundation did with the money.

I'd expected a list of bunkers. I'd been reading too much. It was a list of *transfers.* Dates, in the right-hand column, through the end of the quarter, accelerating towards it. In the left column, what was being transferred: records, mostly, by category, from one archive to another, from the house's own collections to a foundation depot I'd never heard of, with a reference number each, the way a careful institution moves things. *Deaccessioned.* *For conservation.* *For review.* Four hundred years of never throwing anything away, and here was the house quietly throwing things away, on a schedule, to a post box.

And in the middle column, under a heading that just said *Visit*, addresses.

Not many. Eleven, in the quarter. People, I understood, who'd be *visited*, in connection with the transfers; readers of the records being moved, I assumed, academics, dealers, the kind of people who'd notice a shelfmark had gone. One in Leiden, a street I'd stood on in the rain. One in Groningen, a shop with a website from 2004. One in a town four streets from a station.

And one that was ours.

Our street. Our number. In a column headed *Visit*, with a date, eleven days off, and a reference number, and in the far right, in the field where every other line had a category, one word that wasn't a category:

*Companion.*

---

I had it on the kitchen table because of course I did. I'd been reading it for two nights. I'd like to tell you I'd hidden it and she found it; I'd like to tell you I'd been careful. I left it on the kitchen table under the thin book with my coat over both because I'd gone to let the dogs out, and when I came back Ana was standing at the table with the coat in one hand and the schedule in the other, reading, the way she reads, better upside down than most people the right way up.

She didn't say anything for a long time. Then she put her finger on a line.

"Why is our address on this?"

"Ana."

"It's a work thing. I can see it's a work thing, it's got the bank's name on it. Why is our *house* on a work thing. In a column that says visit." Her finger moved. "What's companion."

"I don't know."

"You don't know."

"I've been reading it for two days and I don't know what that word means on that line, and I'm not going to pretend I do, I've stopped doing that, I'm telling you the honest version: I don't know."

She put the schedule down. She didn't let go of it. "Try."

And I had the whole shape. I need you to understand that I've had the whole shape since Leiden and that every time I've said *I don't know* to my wife in this story I've been lying by exactly the width of a word I was too frightened to say. *Companion.* I'd seen it before. On a website, under a photograph, in a caption the club secretary thought was funny. *J. Klippe and companion.* It means Buddy. It means the old dog. It means that whoever was scheduled to visit our house eleven days from now had been given the course's own phrase for the thing I loved, and had put it in the field where the category goes.

"It's the dog," I said.

Ana looked at me.

"The word. It's from the golf club website. It's what they call Buddy in the caption. Somebody who's read that caption has put it on this line, next to our house, next to a date." I heard my own voice and it was very flat. "I don't know who. I don't know what visit means. I think it's the man from the pub. I don't know that either."

"The man from the pub was in insurance."

"The man from the pub asked about Buddy before he asked my name."

She sat down. Not in a chair. On the floor, by the radiator, where Buddy was, and she put her hand on his head the way I do when I'm thinking, and I'd never seen her do it before, and I understood she'd got it from me, the way I'd got the chin-on-the-head from her, and that we'd been trading gestures for eleven years without noticing and that this was the first one I'd given her that she didn't want.

"James," she said.

That's the first time. Eleven years. Mulder, Scully, *you*, *hey*, *dickhead*, with love. Never James. It landed in the kitchen like a plate.

"I'm not leaving you. I want to be really clear about that, because you're going to do the thing where you decide what I meant. I'm not leaving *you.*" She stroked the dog. "I'm leaving the *address.* The address is on a list. I'm not stupid, I can read a list. And I'm taking Mora, because Mora barks at the postman and if somebody's coming to visit a dog I'd rather it was the one that bites." She looked at Buddy. "I can't take him. He can't do the stairs at Mum's. You know he can't."

"I know."

"So you keep him. And you keep *that*" — the schedule — "and the thin book and whatever's in the folder called Reading, and you sort it out, or you don't, and either way you ring me every night at nine and if you don't ring I'm coming back with my brother and a bat." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I don't believe any of it. I want to say that too. I don't believe there's a *they.* I think you've found a thing that doesn't fit, like you always do, and you've pulled it, like you always do, and this time something on the other end pulled back, and it's a *person*, not a *they*, and he was in our garden drinking our beer, and I liked him, and I'm so angry about that I can't see."

"Ana — "

"*Don't.* Don't do the voice. Don't tell me a did-you-know. You haven't told me one since the pub and I noticed, I noticed on the Tuesday, I noticed because it's the first thing you've ever stopped doing without telling me, and I've been waiting a week for you to *see* that I'd noticed." She stood up. "Help me pack the dog."

---

We packed the dog. Mora's bowl, Mora's lead, Mora's toy, which she carried to the car herself, wet, in a state of joy, because the car means the course or the vet and both are the best thing that has ever happened. Ana's bag took four minutes. She's always been able to leave a house in four minutes; it's the thing her grandmother taught her, she said once, the thing a woman learns when the situation at home isn't ideal: where the bag is.

At the door she stopped. The hat was on its hook. She looked at it for a second, and didn't touch it, and that was somehow the worst part.

"Nine o'clock," she said.

"Nine o'clock."

"Every night."

"Every night."

She put her hand on my face. Not squeezing. Then she took it away and went down the path with the Doberman pulling her towards the best thing that had ever happened, and I stood in the door with an old dog leaning on my leg and watched my wife leave the address.

The car went. The street was quiet. Somewhere, a bin.

Inside, on the kitchen table, under my coat, a schedule said somebody was visiting in eleven days, and the thin book under it said *Ask*, and I went in and sat down on the floor by the radiator, where she'd sat, and put my hand on the dog's head, and for the first time in my life there was nobody in the house to pretend for.

"Right," I said to Buddy. "Two options."

He didn't have a theory. Neither did I. We sat there anyway.
