# Leave it

Nothing happened.

I need to say that first, because you've been waiting for the thing, and I was waiting for the thing, and there wasn't one. Two weeks went by. The date on the bottom of a ruleset came and went on a Tuesday, and on the Wednesday I went to the course at seven with the dog, and the retirees were on the third, and the dew was on everything, and nothing had happened to anyone. The world did not end. The world has, as far as I can tell, never once ended, which is the main argument against it.

We went home. Ana and Mora and me and the dog, back to the address on the list, on the Monday after the Sunday, and Ana opened the front door with her own key and stood in the hall for a second and then said, "Right," and went to put the kettle on, and that was that. The hat went back on its own hook. Mora checked every room for the postman. Buddy went to the radiator, slowly, paying for every step, and lay down, and sighed, and I stood in the hall with the bags and thought: *this is enough. This life is enough.* And then: *don't go looking.* And then I laughed, out loud, in my own hall, because I'd had that thought once before, on the seventh, in April, and I'd written it down, and I'd gone looking anyway, and here I was.

Saskia called me in on the Thursday. The glass room. No ceiling this time; a smaller room, with a plant.

"The committee's read your report."

I'd written one. Forty pages. The line, the steps, the grants in the cold years, the job, the ruleset, page 212, the band. All of it, labelled, in order, with *I don't know* where I didn't. I'd written it the way I write everything, for people who'd never read it, and I'd sent it to her, and she'd sent it up, and it had gone to a committee that reported to a board that had never read anything I'd write.

"And?"

"They've decided to leave it."

I'd been expecting a lot of sentences. I hadn't been expecting that one, and I laughed, which she took for something else.

"I know," she said. "I know. You did the work. It's good work, James, I read it twice, I understood about a third of it and the third I understood frightened me. And they've thanked you, and they've noted it, and they've decided that the arrangement with the foundation is historical and the sums are immaterial and it's not a priority for the quarter." She looked at the plant. "That's a direct quote. *Leave it.* I wrote it down because I thought you'd want it."

"I do."

"You're not angry."

"No." I thought about it. "I think it's the right answer, actually. From their side. I think it's the only answer they've got."

She didn't understand that, and I didn't explain it, and she said the role was mine for as long as I wanted it and the house would be glad to have someone reading the ingredients, and I said thank you, and went back to my desk, and looked at the ledger.

The sum had gone *down.*

January. The first of the month. For the first time in thirty-one years, the payment to the post box had stepped *down*, by a clean multiple, the kind a committee agrees. Not to nothing. Not even to where it had been before July. One step. Somebody, somewhere, had been told that the index was lower than it had been, and had voted the money to match.

I sat and looked at that for a long time.

I'll never know. I'd like that understood, because it's the thing this whole story is about, and it's the thing I'm worst at, and I've made my peace with it in the way you make peace with an old dog on a bottom step. I'll never know whether the number went down because I'd stopped reporting, like Noor, like a man who's decided not to be believed. Or because a man in a good coat with a pen reached into a sheet in a depot and changed one word and left everything else untouched, the way he'd learned it, from a card, from a printer, from the thing that had been done to a dead man's book in 1931. Ninety-nine per cent. I've never got the last one. Neither did he. That's the arrangement.

The job still runs. Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds. Forty kilobytes to nowhere. I check it most nights, the way you touch your pocket for your keys. I'm in there, I assume. Somewhere in the forty kilobytes, a line, a code, a number that's stopped moving. *Reporting ceased.* Filed. Under whatever they file a man under when he's not warm and not cold and not on the dial.

I'm noise now. That's what I worked out, at the desk, with the ledger open. That's the verdict. The machine had read me for eight months and had tried correcting and laughing and buying and visiting, and had sent its best man to know what I loved, and in the end it had done the only thing it had left, which was the first thing anyone in it had ever said to my face.

*Leave it.*

Fine.

Frank moved something on his list.

He told me by the coffee machine, casually, the way he tells me everything. "The 1953 memo. I made a third column." He grinned. "*The table.* It's the only thing in it. You've ruined my document, mate."

"Based on what?"

"Based on a staircase," he said, and did the skull, and went.

And a parcel came. Not for me; for Ana, addressed in a conservator's hand, no return address, Leiden postmark. Inside, flat, between two boards, a page from something four hundred years old that had been torn once and wasn't any more, repaired so you had to hold it to the window to find the join. No note. Ana looked at it for a long time and said, "Who's this from?" and I said, "A woman who repairs paper," and she said, "Is she okay?" and I said, "She's quiet," and Ana said, "That's not the same thing," and I said, "No. It isn't. She's okay."

That's how I know. I told you I'd tell you.

On the Wednesday after the date, at seven, on the bench by the sixth green, there was a flask.

Standing up. Full. The coffee in it still hot, which meant whoever had left it had left it in the last half hour, and the course had been empty when I'd come through the gate, and the retirees were on the third, and there was nobody on the sixth, and nobody on the seventh, and nobody in the trees where my drive was going to go.

No note. Just the flask, a plain one, new, the kind a man buys on a Tuesday because he's said he's a flask man and finds he wants it to have been true.

Buddy got out of the cart. He hadn't got out of the cart on the course since the summer. He walked to the bench, slowly, paying for every step, and sniffed the flask, and sat down next to it, and looked at me.

I poured a cup. It was good coffee. Better than the shed's. I drank it standing on the sixth with the dew on everything and the old dog sitting by a bench, and I looked down the fairway towards the seventh, where a man had once chipped in from the fringe and said that he'd never be able to explain it to anyone.

He'd been right. He never would. Neither would I.

I never saw him again. I've looked, in the way I look: the consultancy was dissolved in February, the flat with the wrong name on the bell has a different wrong name on it now, and the only line in the world that has his code on it is in a box in a depot, under a car park, in conservation, and I've got the shelfmark in my wallet and I've never once written to ask. I could. Nobody ever has. I've decided that's his.

I poured a second cup. Buddy thumped twice.

"Right," I said. "Let's go and find my ball."
