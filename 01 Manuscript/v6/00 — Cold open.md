# Something Doesn't Fit

There were twenty things on the table and one of them was my wife's hand.

I'd counted. That isn't a figure of speech. I count when there's nothing else my hands can do, and there was nothing else to do with them in my mother-in-law's kitchen while a polite man in a good jumper poured himself a second glass of water from her jug. Two copies of a book nobody has read since 1926. A ruler. A dog lead with no dog on it. Three photographed pages in a plastic sleeve. A sheet of numbers with two columns. A transfer schedule folded in thirds, with an address on it that I'd have given a year of my life for Ana never to have read. My phone. A second phone. My keys. His keys, which he'd put down the way you put keys down in a house where you plan to stay a while. Salt. Pepper. A mug with a chicken on it. The tinfoil hat was not on the table. It was on the hook by the door behind Ana's head, where I'd hung it myself a week before, because it was the only thing I'd brought from the house that wasn't evidence, and I could see it over his shoulder every time he said the word *recall*.

He'd said it four times. He never said the other word. Neither did I. There was a rule in that room that nobody had explained to Ana and that both of us were obeying for her sake, or that's what I told myself at the time, and the rule was simple: the moment one of us said it plainly in front of her, she was in.

"It's a book thing," I'd said, when she asked.

"It is a book thing," he agreed, and smiled at her, and he meant it. That was the problem with him. He meant everything.

Ana's hand was on mine. Not squeezing. I'd learned, eight months before, in gold light, what it means when my wife's hand stops squeezing, and I had sworn, on a kitchen floor with an old dog watching, that I would never make her hand go like that again. And here it was, open on the table. Item twenty.

"What I'm offering," the man said, "is a budgeting thing. Honestly. It's seats."

"Bunks," I said.

He let that go, kindly. He let everything go kindly. He had the face of a man who has been forgiven for something large and has decided to spend the rest of his life passing the favour on.

Here's what I couldn't work out then, and I've had years to work it out since, and I still can't. One of the two men at that table was afraid. The sheet with two columns said which one. I'd have bet the house, which was the only thing I had left to bet, that the sheet was wrong.

The question was whether he'd bet too.
