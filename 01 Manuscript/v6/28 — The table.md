# The table

He came at eleven on the Sunday, which is when people come for coffee, and Ana opened the door, and I heard her say, from the kitchen, "You must be the one who talks," and I heard him laugh, surprised, pleased, and I knew before I saw him that she'd already won something.

"I'm Bram," he said. "I am the one who talks. I'm sorry about that. Is that tea?"

He was sixty, maybe. A good jumper, the kind a wife chooses. No coat; he'd left it in the car, I understood later, because a man without a coat isn't going anywhere, and a man who isn't going anywhere is harder to throw out. He had a folder under his arm and he put it on the chair, not the table, and he looked at the table, where I'd laid out the twenty things the way I'd laid them out for Ana, and he looked at them for a long moment with an expression I can only call *professional appreciation.*

"That's a very good bibliography," he said.

"Sit down."

"May I? Not there, I think. That's yours. Here." He sat where the light was on him, which I noticed, and filed, and then unfiled, and looked at. "And this is the foreman."

Buddy was under the table. He'd come in from the radiator when the door went, slowly, and put himself under the table by my feet, and he looked at Bram from there the way he'd looked at a man by a heater, and Bram looked back, and said, "Hello, old man," gently, and didn't try, and Buddy put his head down.

He'd asked about the dog *second.* After Ana. I noticed that too.

---

"I'll say what I'm not going to do," Bram said, with his tea, "so we don't waste the morning. I'm not going to confirm anything. Not the book, not the list, not the job at your old place or the line at your new one, not the pages from the dresser, not the gentleman from the golf course. I'm not going to tell you there's a *we.* If you say there is, I'll listen. I've found that it goes better if I listen." He smiled at Ana. "Your husband's going to think that's a technique. It is. It's also true."

"Then what are you here for?" Ana said.

"To make an offer. And to hear what James thinks he's found, because I've read the summaries and I'd rather hear it from the man." He turned to me. "Go on. You've laid it out. Walk me through it. Pretend I'm your wife."

"He's never once walked me through anything," Ana said. "I had to get it in twenty minutes with objects."

"Then I'm honoured," said Bram, and meant it, and that was the problem with him.

So I walked him through it. The sentence. The list. The pages. The job, and the line, and the steps, and the grants in the cold years. I did it the way I do the toaster, in order, labelled, with *I don't know* where I didn't, and he listened the way Dave had listened to Ana's grandmother, with his whole body, and when I'd finished he sat back and said nothing for a while, and then he said:

"You've got the band."

"Yes."

"Nobody gets the band. I've been doing this for twenty-two years and I've had four people in that chair who'd got the sentence and two who'd got the job and one who'd got the pages, and nobody's ever once sat there and told me it *heats.*" He shook his head. "Who told you?"

"A thermostat."

He laughed, properly, and Ana looked at me, and I saw her see that I'd made the one who talks laugh, and file it.

---

"I'm going to tell you a story now," Bram said. "I'd like you both to hear it, because it's the only part of this I actually believe, and it's the reason I do it, and I'd rather you heard the reason than the arithmetic. You can throw it out after. People do."

He put his cup down.

"Imagine a world where it's happened before. Not the end. The *shift.* Imagine that every few thousand years, something goes through a population, I'm not going to say what, you've put a hand on a tablecloth about it, I'm told, and people stop being afraid. Not all of them. Enough. And it's wonderful. It's the best thing that ever happens to anybody. And then, within a generation, every single time, the ones who've stopped being afraid build something faster than the ones who haven't can understand, and they build it *together*, because they're not afraid of each other any more, and then the ones who are still afraid, which is most people, because it's always most people, look at what's being built and they do the only thing frightened people have ever done with a thing they can't understand."

"They burn it," Ana said.

"They burn it. And the ones who built it. And it ends worse than it would have if nobody had woken up at all, because now there's a *giver.* There's a hand. There's somebody to blame, and somebody to follow, and a story about where the fire came from, and the next lot build a religion on the story and the one after that builds a war on the religion." He turned his cup. "So, a very long time ago, some people who'd watched this happen more than once sat down and asked a question. Not *how do we stop people waking up.* You can't. Nobody can; it's weather. The question was: *how do we stop the hand.* And the answer, after a great deal of trying, was: you take the hand out of the story. Every story. You don't deny the fire. You don't deny the flood. You just make sure that when a child reads about it, it *arose.* Nobody gave it. Nobody brought it. There's nobody to burn." He looked at the two books. "That's not a lie, James. That's a *style.* We don't change what a thing is. We change where it's kept. And we keep the number of people who've stopped being afraid inside a range where they can be laughed at instead of followed, because being laughed at is survivable, and being followed, I promise you, I have the records, is not."

"And the seats?"

"The seats are for the ones who've done the arithmetic and would rather not be laughed at. There aren't many. There's a review, periodically, when the range is exceeded, and I'm not going to tell you what a review is, because I don't know, I've never seen one, I've only ever seen the budget for it. And there's a provision, for the people who've helped, and their families, and the provision is called a seat, and I've got a folder with your name in it and a number next to it." He said the next part very quietly. "A population that remembers is not a population you can house. We've tried. It's not cruelty. It's capacity."

Ana said, "That's a beautiful story."

"Thank you."

"I half believe it. That's what's horrible. I'm sitting in my mother's kitchen half believing a man I've never met about the end of the world, because he's told it well and he's kind to the dog." She folded her arms. "What's the number?"

---

"Yourself," Bram said. "Ana. And three. Five seats, in total. The allocation is the allocation; I don't set it, I'm sorry, I did ask."

"And the dogs," I said.

He looked, for the first time, like a man who'd rather be somewhere else.

"Companions aren't seated," he said. "I'm sorry. It's a budgeting thing."

Ana's hand went to Buddy under the table without her looking. I watched it go. I watched her face not change, the way Dave's hadn't changed, and I understood that she'd learned it from him, on a terrace, and that she was using it now against the man who'd sent him, and I loved her so much that for a second I couldn't see.

"So that's the price," she said.

"That's the price. For the seats. I'd be lying if I said otherwise and I don't lie, it's the one thing I've got." He took the folder off the chair, finally, and opened it, and took out a single sheet, and turned it to the light, and slid it halfway across the cloth. "And this is the other thing. I was asked not to show you this. I'm showing you because you check paper, and I'd rather you checked it in front of me than found it later and thought I'd hidden it."

Two columns. A code on the left, a number, a date; the same code on the right, a different number. My week. I knew it before I'd read it, the way I'd known page 118. *J-0417.* April to now. The left column going loud in July and quiet in September. The right column going up, every week, and never once down.

And at the bottom of the right column, a second line. A different code. *H-114.* Same class as mine. A date beside it. Nine years ago.

"What's that?" said Ana. "Is that a book thing?"

I didn't answer. I'd stopped being able to. I reached across the table, past the ruler and the mug with the chicken, to the transfer schedule, folded in thirds, and unfolded it, and ran my finger down the lines to the one I'd read forty times and never once *looked* at: the depot transfer, this week, with a handler reference in the margin in a box I'd taken for a stamp.

*H-114.*

I put the two sheets side by side on the cloth and put one finger on each line, the way I should have read everything from the start.

Nobody said anything. Bram watched me do it. He'd known I would. That was why he'd brought it.

*James has hands.* He'd said it in a room with a long table; I knew that now without having been there, the way I'd known the kitchen was the kitchen. And he'd been able to say it, and Bram had been able to believe him, because nine years ago, in another kitchen, in Leiden, with a clipboard and a flask, it had gone through him too, and he'd *filed it.* His own. And watched the date come forward. And taken the seat.

The first person I'd ever met who'd seen what I'd seen on a floor was the one who'd been sent to count me.

That's the thing that hurt. Not the lying. I'd known about the lying since a terrace. It was that it hadn't made him *good.* I'd thought, I understood, sitting there with my fingers on two lines, I'd thought since the floor that the thing was a *key*; that once it had gone through you, you'd be one of us, whoever us was, the ones in the other column, Noor's column. And here was a man it had gone through nine years ago who'd spent them in a car park moving a date with a verb. It didn't make you good. It made you *known.* To the machine, and to yourself. What you did after was yours.

"He told me," I said, eventually. "At three in the morning. That he'd moved it. I didn't understand why he'd tell me."

"No," Bram said. "Neither did I, when I read it. I think he wanted somebody to have asked about the dog."

---

Here's what I'd worked out, in the car, on the way to a terrace, on a floor: he needed me to be afraid. Not of him. Of the thing. Because a seat is a rescue, and you can't sell a rescue to a man who isn't drowning, and every word he'd said since he sat down had been a very kind, very patient attempt to find out whether I still was.

"What are you afraid of, James?" he said. Gently. The way you'd ask a child.

"Nothing you've got."

"That's a line."

"It's a line. It's also the answer. I was afraid of one thing for twenty-five years and it went on a kitchen floor and it hasn't come back and I don't think it's going to. I'm afraid of *you*, a bit. The way you'd be afraid of a car. I'm afraid of a van on Noor's street. I'm afraid for him." The dog. "But the thing you count? The thing your forty kilobytes are for? I haven't got it. And you can't seat what I haven't got, and you can't laugh at a man who's stopped trying to be believed, and you can't vanish me, because a body's a fingerprint and your whole shop is built on never leaving one." I took my fingers off the two lines. "So I think you've driven out here on a Sunday to offer a bunk to a man who doesn't need rescuing, and I think you knew that before you got in the car, and I think you came anyway, because it's your job, and because you're decent, and because you wanted to see one."

Bram was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," he said. "That's about right."

"Then here's what I think you've got, and then you can go. You've offered me a seat. That's the obvious thing. That's the one for people who are frightened, and it's a good one, I'd have taken it in November, I'd have taken it on a terrace and thanked you. And you've told me a story about hands and fires, and that's the beautiful thing, that's the one for people who've seen through the first one, and I half took it, you watched me half take it, Ana said so, it's a *very* good story." I looked at him. "And there's a third thing, and you can't offer it, because it isn't yours. It's on a floor. You've been paying a post box for thirty-one years to count it and you've never once had it, have you. You've got the budget. You've never seen a review and you've never had the other thing either."

"No," Bram said. "Nobody at my level has. We call the seats *decanting*, among ourselves. It's an old joke. Older than me. I never understood it until about a minute ago."

---

He turned to Ana then. I'd known he would. It was the last thing he had.

"And you," he said. "There's a seat for you whatever he decides. I can do that. It's within the allocation. You don't have to be — " he searched for the word, the one he wasn't going to say — "*involved.* You'd just have to be on the list. You've been in the room; that's enough. I can put you on the list and you'll never hear from us again, and if there's ever a review, somebody will come, and you'll be alright."

"I'm not in," Ana said.

"You're in the room."

"I'm in my mother's kitchen. With my husband. And a dog you can't seat." She unfolded her arms and put her hand on mine, on the cloth, on item twenty, and she squeezed it. Once. Hard. The way she squeezes when the plane takes off. "I don't believe it. I want you to write that down, in your folder, in whatever column you've got. I don't believe a word of it. The world isn't a thermostat and there isn't a *them* and my husband hasn't got a soul that gets counted like a gas meter. I don't believe it." She looked at him. "And I believe him. That's the only thing I've got, and it's mine, and it's not on your list, and you can't put it there."

Bram looked at her for a long moment, and then at her hand on mine, and I watched him understand what I'd understood in the car: that the one person at the table who wasn't in the machine was the one who'd read the room, and that she wasn't afraid either, by *proxy*, through a hand, and that there was no column for that.

"No," he said. "I can't."

He put the sheet back in the folder. He closed it. He finished his tea, because he was a man who finished his tea.

"I'll report that you declined," he said. "Both of you. I'll report it accurately. I always have; it's the one thing I've got." He stood. "I don't know what happens now. I want you to hear that, because it's the truest thing I'll say today. I know what happens to a man who takes a seat and I know what happens to a man who shouts. I've never once seen what happens to one who just *sits there.* Nobody has. It's not in the folder." He looked at the dog. "If I had to guess, I'd say nothing. That's what happens to most things, in the end. You'd be surprised how often the answer that comes back is *leave it.*"

At the door he stopped. The hat was on the hook, where I'd hung it, on a hook it didn't belong on. He looked at it.

"That's very good," he said. "Who made it?"

"I did," said Ana.

"You should be careful with that," Bram said. "It works." And he thanked her for the tea, and went down the path without a coat, and got into a car, and that was the one who talks.

---

I sat at the table for a long time after. Ana didn't move either. Buddy came out from under it eventually and put his chin on my knee and looked at me like a foreman inspecting a site after the contractors have gone.

Twenty things on the table. One of them my wife's hand, and it had squeezed.

I'd thought, the whole time, since I'd first counted the things on that table, that the sheet with two columns said which of us was afraid, and that it was wrong. It wasn't wrong. It had said *me*, in April, and it had been right, and it had watched me go, week by week, like a bath, and it had been right about that too. It had never once been wrong about me.

It had been wrong about *him.* Not Bram. The other code. The one in my class, with a date nine years back, who'd known what the index counted before anyone told him, and had filed himself, and taken the seat, and come to a window at three in the morning to tell me so.

The sheet said he had hands. The sheet was right. What the sheet couldn't say, because it only ever measured one column, was what he was going to do with them.

Neither could I. I'd stopped checking people, and then I'd started, and I'd got him wrong twice. I was going to have to let the third one happen without me.

"Well," said Ana. "That got weird."

And she laughed. Not almost. The whole thing, the real one, with her head back, in her mother's kitchen, with a tinfoil hat on the hook behind her, and I laughed too, and the dog thumped twice, and upstairs the television laughed at something else entirely.
