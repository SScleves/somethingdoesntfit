# The vet

It started at two, which is the worst hour, and I knew that because a man had told me so over bad coffee in a shed.

The sound woke me. Not a bark. Buddy hadn't barked since the summer; he'd stopped bothering, the way he'd stopped the stairs. It was the other sound, the one every dog owner knows from the inside of their own sleep, and I was on the landing before I was awake and in the kitchen before I'd turned a light on, and he was on the floor by the radiator, on his side, with his legs going.

I'm not going to describe the next hour. I've read a hundred descriptions and they're all the same and none of them are any use. I'll tell you what I did. I got the blanket under him. I got the car keys. I rang the number on the fridge, the one Ana had put there in June in her careful hand, *EMERGENCY VET*, with a little heart, because she puts hearts on things, and a woman answered on the second ring and said *bring him*, and I carried a twelve-year-old dog to a grey car in the rain in my socks.

The vet was young and kind and unhurried, and *unhurried* was the thing. She didn't rush. She looked at him and listened to him and put a hand flat on his chest the way Ana puts a hand on mine, and she said, "Okay. Okay. Not tonight, I think." And then, because I must have had the face: "I mean it. I don't say that. He's old and something's upset him badly and he's dehydrated and I'm going to keep him for a few hours and give him fluids, and you're going to go and sit in that chair, and it's not tonight."

"What upset him?"

She looked at me the way vets look at you when the honest answer is *I don't know* and they've been trained not to say it. "Something he ate, maybe. Old dogs get into things. Did he get into anything?"

"He can't get into anything. He can't do the stairs. He drinks from a bowl by the back gate and eats what I give him."

"Then I don't know," she said, which I liked her for. "Sit down. I'll come and get you."

I sat down. There's a chair in every vet's waiting room at three in the morning and I've now sat in it, and it's the loneliest chair in the world, and I sat in it with my phone in my hand and did nothing with it for a long time.

Then I looked at the date. The schedule had said eleven days. It had been eleven days.

---

They let me take him home at six. I made a bed on the kitchen floor out of every blanket in the house, and I lay down next to him, because that's where he was, and I slept, badly, in my coat, and I woke twice and both times he was awake, watching me, his head on his paws, the way he watches me go up the stairs from the bottom step.

Neither of us could help the other. Neither of us left. I've thought about that floor a lot since and I think that's the most anyone's ever done for me.

At nine I rang Ana, because it was nine.

"He's okay," I said, first, before hello, because she'd hear it in my voice otherwise. "He had a bad night. He's okay. The vet said not tonight. She meant it."

Silence. Then: "What happened?"

"I don't know."

"James."

"I genuinely don't know. Something he ate. He can't have eaten anything. I don't know." I had my hand on his head. "The date was yesterday. On the schedule. The visit. I sat up till one and nobody came. Nobody knocked, nobody rang, the street was empty, I checked twice. And at two he was on the floor."

A long breath. "Are you saying somebody — "

"I'm not saying anything. I'm saying the date was yesterday and at two he was on the floor and I'm never going to know, Ana, and I'm telling you the honest version because you asked me for it in the kitchen and I'm trying."

She didn't say anything for a while. When she did, her voice was different. "Send me a photo of him."

I did. Asleep, on the blankets, grey to the eyebrows.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Nine tonight."

"Nine tonight."

---

I need to tell you about the photo, because the photo is what finally made me check a person.

I had, on my phone, from the barbecue, the pictures Ana had taken and sent me afterwards, *look at your face*, *look at Henk's hat*, that kind of thing. And in two of them, in the background, by the heater, there was Dave. And on Wednesday I sent one of those pictures to Noor, in Leiden, because I was asking her about the forum man. That was all. The lonely one, with the theories, who'd found our house from a picture; I wanted to know if she knew of him, if he was a known thing, if he was harmless. I cropped it badly. I left in the heater.

She rang me. She'd never rung me. We'd messaged and we'd sat in her kitchen and she had never once used a telephone, and it rang, and I knew before I answered that it wasn't about the forum man.

"The man by the heater."

"Dave. He's a — he was a golf friend."

"His name's Kalder."

I was standing in the kitchen. I sat down.

"Julian Kalder. He was in the department the month after Hendrik retired. Overseeing the transfer of the collection. He was in and out for three weeks, with a clipboard, and he was *very* kind about it. He learned everyone's names. He asked about my mother. He brought a flask." Her voice was perfectly steady and I could hear what it cost. "I haven't seen him for nine years and I'd know him in the dark. Why is he in your garden?"

I'm going to tell you how long it took me, because I want it on the record how slow I am.

It took me a full minute. Sitting in my kitchen with the phone against my ear and Noor breathing in Leiden and the dog asleep on the blankets, a full minute, to go from *Kalder* to the consultancy on the recruiter's email, to *Julian K.* who'd fixed my access in eleven minutes, to *the person who'd be embarrassed*, to a man in a good coat at a market who didn't haggle, to *Deventer*, to a flask. All of it. Every piece had been on paper and I'd checked every piece of paper and I'd never once put a face on any of it, because I don't check people. I check paper. It's the thing I'm worst at.

"He asked about the dog first," I said, eventually. "On the course. Before he asked my name."

"Yes," said Noor. "That's what he does."

---

I went to Leiden that afternoon. I left Buddy with the neighbour, who's fine, and I got the train, and I sat on it and I built, in my head, the thing I was going to say to her. I'd been building it since the phone went down. It went: *you know him. You can place him. You were there, in the department, with a clipboard, and you're a scholar, and you've got a name, and if you say it, publicly, with mine, then there are two of us, and two is a pattern, and a pattern is something a style guide can't reclassify.*

I'd built her into a witness. On the train. Without asking.

She let me say it. The whole thing, standing in her hall with my coat on, and she listened the way she listens, completely, and when I'd finished she said, "No."

"Noor — "

"No. And you knew I'd say no, you've known since my kitchen, I told you in so many words, and you built it anyway, on a train, because you needed somebody to *believe* you, and I'm not going to be that, James, because the reason I'm alive is that I never once needed it." She wasn't angry. That was the thing. "A reader who persists is to be offered the opportunity to publish. You've just offered me one. In my own hall. You've done their job for them, and you've done it because you're frightened, and I understand, and no."

I stood there.

"Ask me something else," she said. "You're good at it when you're not frightened. Hendrik said you'd be. Ask."

So I asked the thing I'd actually come with, under the thing I'd built. "The transfers. On the schedule. Records going from the house to a foundation depot. Where do they *go?* It's a reference number. It's not a place."

She looked at me for a moment. Then she went into the kitchen and came back with a slip of paper, and on it, in a conservator's hand, was a shelfmark. A letter, four digits, a slash, two more.

"That's the depot's accession format. The foundation publishes a catalogue. It's public. It's a legitimate institution with a car park and a sign; the best hiding place has always been a building with a reason to be there. That number is the item Hendrik tried to see in 2015 and was told was *in conservation.*" She put it in my hand. "You can't walk in. Nobody can walk in. But it's a catalogue, and a catalogue has an enquiry address, and nobody has ever once *asked.* Not for that item. Hendrik argued. He wrote them a letter about his rights. That's not asking."

*Ask.* The professor's word. In her hall. In my hand. And I didn't notice, I swear to you, I didn't notice it then.

"Why are you giving me this?"

"Because you asked instead of argued," Noor said. "For once. Go home to your dog."

---

She got the visit on Friday.

I know because she messaged me on Thursday night, one line, *there's a van on my street*, and then nothing. I rang. It rang out. I rang on Saturday and it went to a message, and on Sunday the number was dead, and on Monday her forum account, which was years old and had never said anything that mattered, had been deleted. Not suspended. Deleted. The way a line in a backup is deleted.

She's not dead. I want to say that as plainly as I can, and I can, because I know it, and I'll tell you how in a while. She's not dead. She's *quiet.* Polite men came in a van and were very kind about it and now there is a woman in Leiden who repairs paper and has never heard of me, and the shelfmark she gave me is on a slip in my wallet, and it was the last thing she ever gave anyone.

I'd gone to make her a witness. She'd given me a number because I'd asked. And then she'd gone dark, because I'd been to her house, with my face, on a train, and the people who read the column I was in had read it.

That's the fourth time my method failed. I'm counting. The one in five hundred, the sock drawer, the ticket, and her. Each time I'd done the thing I'm good at and the thing I'm good at had cost somebody.

Buddy was on the blankets when I got home. Better. Up on his elbows, supervising. He thumped twice.

I sat down on the floor with him in the dark and didn't turn on the light, and I didn't do two options, because I didn't have two. I had one dog, and one number, and one word, and eleven days had become nineteen, and the review date on the bottom of a ruleset was the end of the quarter, and the quarter ended in three weeks.

I'd run out of paper to check.

What was left, I understood, sitting there, was me. The one system I'd never instrumented. The thing that had been sending its own forty kilobytes to nowhere, every night, since I was six.

"Right," I said to the dog. "Okay."

And I closed my eyes.
