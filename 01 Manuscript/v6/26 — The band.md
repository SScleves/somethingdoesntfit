# The band

Three in the morning in a building with a ceiling you could land a plane in, and every screen on the wall of the monitoring room was flat, because at three in the morning nothing is happening in a finance house that's been going since wigs, and that's the point of three in the morning.

I had the ledger history open on one screen and thirty-one years of a payment on the other, and I'd been looking at the steps for two hours, the way I look at a value that's been climbing in a queue nobody reads, and I'd got nowhere, and I'd decided to stop getting nowhere and do the dumb thing, which is to ask the question underneath the question.

The steps went up. 1996, 2008, 2019, July. I'd known that for a month. Up, in clean multiples, when the index was up. Fine. That's half a thermostat. That's a thing that cools: the room gets too warm, the room gets a review.

Here's the dumb question. *What does it do when the room gets too cold?*

Because a thermostat that only cools is a freezer. You'd end up with a population that never woke up at all, and a population that never wakes doesn't *produce* anything, doesn't ask, doesn't post, doesn't sit on floors, and you'd have nothing to count. Whoever built this had been running it for a very long time and they weren't stupid. A thing like this has to *heat* as well. It has to have a floor.

So I stopped looking at the payment and looked at the foundation.

Meerwijk administers the foundation. That's the thing I'd walked past for two months. The house doesn't just pay the post box; the house *runs* it, as a client, the way it runs forty other charities with dull names, and a foundation's outgoings are a ledger too, and I had the role now, because a man who'd be embarrassed had given it to me in eleven minutes.

Grants. Thirty-one years of grants. Small, mostly. Heritage stabilisation, narrative-protective archaeology, a conservation covenant here, a digitisation programme there. And then, in certain years, something else. A production company, in 2003, a sum that would make a documentary. A publisher, 2011, a sum that would make a book with footnotes. A *retreat centre*, 2014, with a name I recognised from the forum man's bottle-of-wine monologue, where he'd been, twice, and come back with the Sphinx. A streaming channel. A festival. In 2017, a grant to a university library for the restructuring of its special collections under a digitisation programme, which is a sentence that sounds like nothing.

I pulled the years and I put them next to the index, the way you put page 118 next to page 118, and there it was, and I sat back in my chair in an empty room at three in the morning and said, out loud, to nobody, "Oh, you *bastards.*"

Every grant to noise fell in a year the index was *low.*

That's it. That's the whole machine, and I'd been looking at half of it since July. When the number went up, they brought the review forward and narrowed what people could see. When the number went *down*, when too few people were asking, when the room got too cold, they *paid for a documentary.* They funded the sock drawer. They seeded the phantom centuries and the bloodlines and the retreat and the Sphinx, not to hide the truth but to keep people *looking*, badly, in the wrong place, so that the ones who looked properly would always have a gorilla standing next to them and the count would never go flat.

A band. Not a floor. They'd never wanted nobody to wake up. They'd wanted *the right number* to, and to be laughed at, and to be seated, and to be counted, forever, in a range, like a room kept at nineteen degrees by a very old thermostat that somebody in Leiden in 1931 had set and nobody since had been allowed to touch.

And a man who's stopped being afraid isn't warm or cold. He's not on the dial. That's what Bram meant, and I hadn't met Bram yet. *You can't house that.* You can't ridicule it either, because it's not on a stage; and you can't seat it, because it doesn't want a seat; and you can't vanish it, because a body's a fingerprint and the whole point of the machine is that it leaves none. A calm man in a kitchen with a dog is a reading the instrument can't act on. He's the one thing the band wasn't built for.

I'd like to say I felt triumphant. What I felt was tired, and a little sick, the way you feel when you've finally found the value that's been climbing and it turns out to be the one everyone was most proud of.

---

There's a glass wall between the monitoring room and the corridor, so that the people who pay for the screens can walk past and see them being watched. At three in the morning the corridor's dark. I'd been there two hours and I'd never once looked at it.

I looked at it.

There was a man in a good coat on the other side of the glass. Standing. Not hiding; there's nowhere to hide in a corridor. Just standing, with his hands in his coat pockets, the way he'd stood on a sixth green with a hand up to wave me through, and he'd been standing there, I understood, for some time.

My heart did the one thing. The knock. Then slow.

I didn't get up. He didn't come in. We looked at each other through a wall of glass that the house had put there so that the people who paid for the watching could see it done, and he raised one hand, slightly, from the pocket, the wave, and I found, to my surprise, that I didn't want to hit him. I'd expected to. I'd rehearsed it, on a floor. It wasn't there.

He came to the door and opened it and didn't come through.

"Jim."

"Dave."

"Julian, actually. I think you've had that for a while."

"Since the vet."

He nodded. He stayed in the doorway. He looked, in the light from the flat screens, older than on the course, and tired in a way that a man can't fake, and I noticed that and filed it, and then I thought, *no, don't file it*, and looked at it instead.

"I'm not here to stop you," he said. "I'd like that understood. You've got the grants up. I can see it from here. You were always going to get the grants up; it's the only thing in the building that's got the other half on it. I'm here to tell you two things and then I'm going to a depot, because there's a transfer with my name on it and that's not a punishment, I'm told."

"Go on."

"Bram's coming to see you. Sunday. He's the one who talks. He'll come to your mother-in-law's, because that's where the wife is, and he'll bring a folder, and he's kind, and he means it, and he'll offer you what I was offered, with the arithmetic." He looked at the screens for a second. "Be there. Take the old dog. Don't take Mora; she'll be a problem and Bram doesn't like problems, he likes tea."

"Why are you telling me?"

He thought about it. I watched him think about it, which I'd never seen him do, because on the course he'd always already thought.

"Because the person who'd be embarrassed," he said, "is me."

And then, because he was who he was, because there was still, somewhere under the coat, a man who'd chipped in from the fringe and said nobody'd believe him: "And because you asked about the dog. At the barbecue. You asked whether I minded him not coming over. Nobody's ever asked me that. In nine years. Everybody asks why the dog doesn't like *them.*"

"What's the second thing?"

"The date's two weeks. Bram'll say two. It was three. I moved it." He said it flat, the way I'd said *it's the dog* to Ana. "With a line. I've moved it twice before, with a verb, by making you smaller than you were, and this time I moved it by making you exactly what you are, because he asked me straight and I'd run out of verbs. I thought you should know that it was me. Not the index. Me."

I sat there.

"That's all," Julian Kalder said. "Good game, Jim. Let's not play another." And he put his hand up, the wave, and let the door close, and walked away down a dark corridor past a wall of glass, and I watched him go the way I'd watched him go from a terrace, and I was sad, again, which I hadn't expected, again.

He'd moved it with a line. He'd told me. I didn't know why, and I'd stopped filing things I didn't know, and so I just sat with it, in the room with the flat screens, and let it be true that the man who'd been sent to know what I loved had just come to a window at three in the morning to tell me what he'd done.

*The person who'd be embarrassed is me.*

I drove to Ana's mother's. The house was dark. Ana was in it, and Mora, and Buddy on the blankets by the kitchen radiator, and the hat was on a hook it didn't belong on, and I stood in the hall and looked at them all for a while before I turned on the light.

Sunday. A man in a jumper. Tea.

Fine. Let him come. I had the other half now, and a wife who'd open the door, and a dog who'd made a decision about him before I had, and nothing left in me to seat.
