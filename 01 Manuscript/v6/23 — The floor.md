# The floor

I'm going to tell you what I did and what happened and I'm not going to tell you how, because I don't know how, and because every book that says it knows how is lying, and I've read them all now.

What I did was sit on the kitchen floor. That's it. Every door in the house shut. Phone in the drawer. Lights off except the one over the cooker, which Buddy likes, because it means food is possible. I sat with my back against the cupboard where the dog's biscuits are and my legs out and my hands on my knees, and I did nothing.

I'd read enough to know you're supposed to do nothing. I'd read enough to know that nobody can tell you what nothing is, and that the moment you try to do it you're doing something, and that this is the whole joke and nobody finds it funny. I didn't find it funny either. I sat there the first night for an hour and did nothing so hard that I got a headache, and Buddy watched me from the blankets with an expression I'd have described, if I were being honest, as *concern for a colleague.*

The second night, the same. The third night, I nearly didn't. I sat down, and the review date was in nineteen days, and Noor's number was dead, and my wife was at her mother's with a Doberman, and I thought, *this is the stupidest thing you have ever done. You're a man on a floor. Nothing is going to happen, because nothing is a thing that happens to other people, the ones on the forums, the ones with the glow. You check paper. Go and check some paper.*

And I didn't have any paper left. That was the thing. So I sat.

On the fourth night I stopped trying.

I don't mean I gave up. I mean something smaller and I can't make it bigger. I'd been *trying* to do nothing for three nights the way you try to fall asleep, which is the one thing that trying prevents, and on the fourth night, around one, with the cooker light on and the dog breathing, I thought: *fine. Nothing's coming. Sit here anyway, because the floor's warm and the dog's here and there's nothing on upstairs.* And I stopped waiting for something to happen.

That's the trick, if there is one, and nobody can be told it, and I've just told you, and it won't work, because I've told you.

---

The wall went.

Not *down.* I want to be careful. It didn't fall and there wasn't a light and nobody spoke. It was more like the thing that happens in my job when a system you've been watching for weeks, one that's been reporting itself fine, fine, fine, stops reporting, and in the silence you can hear what it was covering.

What it had been covering was *me.*

Hands. That's the first thing, and it's the thing I'd been told, by my own notes, since April, and had filed. In the cemetery I'd seen a hand, cracked at the knuckles, reaching for mine out of cold mud, and I'd filed it under lack of sleep, and I'd been right that it wasn't a dream, and wrong about everything else. It wasn't *a* hand. It was a hand I'd seen before. Many times. All my life. Reaching, in a flash, the length of a camera flash, in waiting rooms and on trains and once in the middle of a presentation to forty people, and every single time I'd done the same thing, the thing I'm good at, the thing the shrinks graduated me for: I'd looked away, and filed it, and been fine.

Not the first time.

Sitting on the floor with my back against the biscuits, I understood that I had been doing this since I was six. Misfiling. Thirty-five years of a very clean Record with every anomaly in it except the only one that was mine.

And then the lives.

I'm not going to give you the lives. Not because they're secret. Because they're *boring*, and that's the thing nobody tells you, that's the thing that makes it unbearable. I'd expected, if I'd expected anything, something with trumpets. What I got was short, and brutal, and ordinary. Somebody cold. Somebody in a field. Somebody very young, holding on to somebody else's coat. A door. A lot of doors. And love, every time, a different one, cut off mid-sentence, not by anything dramatic, by *administration*: by a war that was somebody's policy, by a winter that was somebody's harvest, by a cart, by a fever, by a man with a list. Over and over. Somebody's hand. Then nothing. Then somebody's hand.

Dealt back into the deck. I'd said that at a grave, in April, doing the arithmetic for the first time. *A machine for making sure no one ever finds anyone again.* I'd thought I was describing a belief.

I'd been describing a *mechanism.*

---

Here's where I have to stop and tell you about the kitchen, because this is a book about checking, and I checked.

After, when it had gone, whenever it went, I sat on the floor and I looked at the kitchen. On purpose. The way you look at a room after you've been told a system's fine, to see if it is. The cooker light was on. The clock on the oven said 02:14, which I'd like to tell you I made up, and didn't. The dog's bowl was where the dog's bowl is. Buddy was on the blankets with his head on his paws, watching me, awake, and had been, I think, the whole time. The fridge was humming the hum it has. My hands were on my knees. I was not glowing. I was a forty-one-year-old man in socks on a kitchen floor at two in the morning and the kitchen was exactly the kitchen, and I knew that if Ana had walked in at that moment she'd have seen a tired man and a dog and said *come to bed*, and she'd have been right.

Nothing had changed in the room. I want that on the record, and it's the last time I'm going to say that.

What had changed was that I wasn't afraid.

Not brave. I don't mean brave. I mean that a thing that had been running in me since I was six, since a corridor, a thing I'd never once heard because it had always been there, the way you don't hear the fridge, had stopped. And in the quiet I could hear what it had been: it had been fear. Not of the men in vans. Of the *thing*. The one thing. The one under the other ones. The one I'd asked a nurse about with my mouth shut at sixteen and asked a grave about at forty with my mouth shut and had built a hobby and a job and a persona and a folder called Reading in order never to hear myself ask out loud.

It was gone. Like a bath. I sat there and listened to it not be there and I could not, for the life of me, remember what I'd been so frightened of, and I knew exactly what I'd been frightened of, and both of those were true at once, and that's the closest I can get.

---

"Buddy," I said.

He lifted his head.

"I think I've got a soul."

And I was *embarrassed.* On the floor, alone, to a dog. My face went hot. I heard the sentence in the kitchen and it sounded like the forums, like the glow, like a man in a tinfoil hat, and I nearly took it back, and then I thought: *he's a dog. He's a professional. He's not going to tell anyone.*

"Sorry," I said. "I know. Bit much."

Two thumps of the tail. He put his head back down. He's heard worse.

---

Then the list re-read itself. I don't know how else to say it. I didn't get up and get it. I didn't need to. I'd read it thirty times; I had it. And sitting there I understood that I'd been reading it wrong, the way I'd read page 118 wrong the first three times, by its shape.

Forty-one entries. Forty of them about the beginning: a hand taken out of a story about where things came from. And one, twenty-six, about last Tuesday: two rooms, forty metres, something moved. I'd asked Noor why the same people would care about both, and she'd said it was the only question, and now I had it, and it was simple, and it had been on the floor the whole time.

They weren't two kinds of edit. They were one edit. *Transmitted* to *arisen.* *Shared* to *coincident.* *Remembered* to *imagined.* Every one of them took the same thing out: the thing I'd just felt go through me on a kitchen floor. The thing that goes between. Between the givers and the given. Between two rooms. Between a man and a hand in cold mud. Between whoever I'd been and whoever I was now, carried across, like a wet rope, from hand to hand. The one thing that can't be gated, because it isn't *information.*

They'd never been hiding history. They'd been hiding *this*. And the only way to hide a thing that isn't information is to edit every sentence in the world that says it's *transmitted*, so that nobody goes looking for the hand.

Thirty-eight, further down the list, I'd never understood. *The observer question.* A withdrawn paragraph in a textbook about measurement, 1971, *the observer* changed to *the apparatus.* I understood it now. I was the observer. I'd just observed. The apparatus was a job that ran at fourteen minutes past two, and what it counted, every night, in forty kilobytes, was how many of us had.

---

I stayed on the floor till it was light. Not doing anything. Just sitting with it, and with the dog, the way you sit with a system that's finally reporting the truth and you don't want to touch it in case it stops.

Somewhere in there I went back to a corridor. I knew I would; it had been waiting since the hand. Sixteen, a coin, four machines with four sounds. A man saying *leave it for now.* A nurse saying *he's in a better place* and a boy turning the sentence over once and putting it somewhere there was nowhere. I went back and I stood in the corridor and I took the hand while it still meant something, which I hadn't, and I said the thing I hadn't, which was nothing, just the sentence, out loud, *based on what*, to a kind tired doctor who couldn't hear me and wouldn't have known. And then I let it go. Twenty-five years. It had a container now. It was a kitchen floor and a dog, and that was enough, and nobody had told me it would be, and I'd never have believed them.

At seven I got up, slowly, with the noise men my age make, and let the dog out, and made coffee, and stood at the back door watching him drink from the bowl by the gate, and the morning was grey and ordinary and I'd have signed for forty more years of it.

At nine I rang Ana.

"I'm okay," I said.

Silence on the line. Then, in a voice I'd never heard from her, careful, the voice you use near something that might be real: "You sound okay."

"I am."

"What happened?"

"I sat on the floor."

"James."

"I sat on the floor for four nights and on the fourth one I stopped being afraid of the thing. The big thing. The one from the funeral. I can't tell you how and I'm not going to pretend I can. But I'm not afraid of it any more and I don't think I'm going to be again, and I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it in my voice." I had my hand on the dog's head. "I'd like to come to your mum's. Not to come back. To tell you. All of it. On a table. Twenty minutes. You can decide after."

A very long quiet.

"Bring the hat," Ana said.

---

I need to tell you one more thing about that night, and then I'll stop, because the rest is the book.

The clock on the oven had said 02:14. I noticed it, the way I notice things, and I filed it, under coincidence, where it belonged, because there are 1,440 minutes in a day and one of them has to be that one.

But I'd like you to hold it. Because somewhere, that night, a job with no owner ran for nine seconds and sent forty kilobytes to nowhere. And whatever it counted, it had just counted me.
