# Utrecht. A phone call. 1993.

Wim Koster waited until the corridor was empty before he closed his office door, which was a thing he had never done in eleven years as head of department, and he noticed himself doing it, and he did it anyway.

The paper was on his desk. Thirty-one pages, double-spaced, with the figures stapled at the back the way Verhoef always did it, because Verhoef believed the argument should stand without the pictures. Two rooms, shielded, forty metres apart. Two people who had sat together for twenty minutes beforehand and were then separated. A light flashed in one room. Something moved, a little, in the other. Not much. Verhoef had the decency to say *not much* in the abstract, which was more than most people managed. The effect was small and it was there, and it was there on four runs out of five, and the fifth run was the one Verhoef had written about at the greatest length, because he was that kind of scientist, and because he was a friend.

The telephone had a long cord. Koster carried it to the window.

"It's Wim," he said. "From the department. You said to call if."

"Yes." The voice on the other end was pleasant and belonged to a man he had met twice, at the foundation, over lunch, and whose surname he could never afterwards remember, which he had come to understand was deliberate. "Go ahead."

"There's a paper. A colleague. He's submitted it, I'm the internal reader." Koster looked at the bicycles in the rack below, the chained wheels, the one with no saddle that had been there since September. "It's good. That's the problem. It's careful."

"What's it about?"

Koster told him. The rooms. The light. The movement. He heard himself make it sound smaller than it was, and he heard the other man hear that, and neither of them remarked on it.

"Recall," the man said, after a moment. It was not a question. It was the sound of a word being placed in a column.

"He doesn't call it that."

"No. He wouldn't." A pause with paper in it. "Is he the sort who corrects himself in public, your colleague?"

Koster thought about Verhoef at the Christmas drinks, telling a room full of people that his own best-known result from 1987 was probably an artefact of the cabling, laughing at himself, buying the next round. "Yes."

"That's worse," the man said, mildly. "Alright. Here's what we'd like. Not the paper; the paper's nothing, the paper's four runs in a cupboard. The category. Does your department have a line for instrumentation?"

"Of course."

"Then it's instrumentation. Cable artefacts, shielding, that family. File it there. Review it there. Let it be read by people who read that line. It'll find its level."

"It isn't instrumentation."

"No," the man agreed. "But it will be. That's all we ever do, Wim. We don't change what a thing is. We change where it's kept." He sounded, briefly, as though he were quoting somebody. "You're not being asked to lie about anything. You're being asked to shelve."

Koster had been going to say something about Verhoef, about the fifth run and the length of the discussion of it, about what it was like to watch a careful man be careful. He found that he was looking at the bicycle with no saddle instead.

"And the other matter," he said.

"Yes. That's confirmed. Yourself, Marianne, the two children." The voice did something small and warm. "You asked about your mother."

"I did."

"It's a budgeting thing, Wim. I'm sorry. The allocation is the allocation. I did ask."

"I understand."

"I know you do. That's why we talk to you." A chair creaked, four hundred kilometres away, in a room Koster would never see. "You'll let me know when it's filed."

"Yes."

"Good man."

Koster put the telephone back on the desk and stood for a while with his hand on it, as though it might ring again and take it back. It didn't. He opened the door. The corridor was still empty. He walked down it, past the noticeboard with the Christmas drinks and the seminar on superconductivity, and he knocked on Verhoef's door, and when Verhoef looked up with that face, that hopeful ruined face of a man who has handed over the best thing he has ever done, Koster said, "It's very good, Anton. Really. I've got some thoughts on the shielding section," and Verhoef said, "I knew you would," and smiled, and pulled out the chair.

They talked for two hours. Koster was kind the whole time. It cost him nothing. That was the part he would think about, years later, in a chair of his own, when he'd stopped being able to remember the foundation man's name and had never once stopped being able to remember the bicycle.

*The paper was reclassified in March. It was not published. Verhoef kept his copy. His grandson kept it after him.*
