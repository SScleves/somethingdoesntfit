# The control case

One hit is a story. Two hits is a coincidence. You don't have anything until you've tried to make it go away and it won't.

That's not wisdom. That's the job. Somebody calls at four in the morning and says the system is dying, and the first thing you do, before you look at the system, is find out whether it's the system or the thing that's watching the system. Half the time the patient is fine and the monitor is broken. People hate that answer. It's the correct answer about half the time.

So before I let myself believe a word of what the kitchen floor had told me, I had to try to kill it. Properly. In writing.

---

The first thing I did was buy another copy.

Second impression, 1931, the Leiden printing. It's not rare; that's the point of it, it turns out, it was printed for the continent and the continent didn't want it, and you can have one for the price of a pizza from any of six Dutch dealers who'd love to see the back of it. Mine came from Zwolle in a padded envelope, smelling of somebody's attic, and I took it into the kitchen and laid it next to the other two and opened all three to page 118.

Blemish, top margin: present. Two typos: present. *Arisen among the first.*

So it wasn't my copy. It was the run. Every second impression ever sold said the new sentence. Whoever did it had done it once and let the presses do the rest, and that's a very particular kind of patience, and I wrote that down.

The second thing I did was the thing I didn't want to do, which is why I did it second. I needed a control.

The professor's crate had eleven other books in it, and nine of those I either owned or could get. If somebody had been quietly reaching into the plates of dead men's books, then a man who'd kept a first impression of Tresidder on purpose might have kept others on purpose too, and if I checked them and found more edits, I'd have a pattern, and if I checked them and found nothing, I'd have one strange book and a bad week. Either answer was fine. That's what a control is: the test where you're happy to lose.

It took three weekends. Ana called it the Summer of the Ruler. I did it the only way it can be done, which is page against page with a finger on each line, and I will say this for it: there's a kind of peace in it. Four hours with two copies of a 1931 survey of calendar systems is four hours in which nobody is dying.

Nothing. Nine books, nothing. Typos corrected between printings, the honest kind, with the little line on the copyright page to say so. One binding variant. A map re-engraved because the first one was ugly. The ordinary, decent drift of printed things.

And I was relieved, which surprised me. I'd wanted it to be nothing. Nothing is the result I know how to live with.

Then I put the nine back on the shelf and looked at the two I hadn't checked, because I couldn't. The thin one with no title. And the paperback of *Blindsight* read half to death, which I'd picked up and put down a dozen times by then because of four words in pencil beside a paragraph near the end. *Not nobody. Not you.* The professor's hand, underlined twice. I didn't know what it meant. I knew what it was like: it was like a man arguing with a book he'd lost the argument to.

---

I should say something here about the window, because it comes back later and I don't want it to arrive like a stranger.

Years ago, before Ana, a friend's garden, a summer, the kind of evening where somebody produces a bag and says it's natural. I was twenty-six and I'd read everything and I thought I knew what I was in for. I'm not going to describe it, partly because I can't and partly because every description I've ever read makes it sound like a film and it was nothing like a film. I'll say this much. For about an hour, the question I've been asking my whole life, the one under the other ones, stopped being a question. It wasn't answered. It was *irrelevant*, the way the colour of a door is irrelevant once the door's open. And then it came back, around midnight, the way a question does, and sat down in its usual chair, and I lay on my friend's lawn and understood that I'd been shown a window and not a way in, and that I'd spend the rest of my life being very careful never to confuse the two.

I filed it. Fourteen years, filed. I'm mentioning it now because when I finally opened the thin book, the first thing I felt was that lawn.

---

It was a Sunday. Ana was at her mother's. Mora was doing the thing where she lies in the doorway so that nobody can enter or leave without a negotiation, and Buddy was on the blanket, and I took the thin book down for what must have been the fifteenth time, and this time I opened it.

Not a book. A notebook, bound to look like one; the professor had had it done, or his grandfather had, because there were two hands in it. The first hand was older, the ink gone brown, careful, upright, the hand of a man who'd learned to write before biros. The second was the professor's, the one from the margins, the *no*, *just no* hand. The old hand had filled the first eleven pages. The professor had filled the next thirty. The last page was in a third version of the professor's hand, shakier, the hand of a man writing from a chair he didn't get out of much.

No title. No introduction. No thesis. Just entries, numbered, in a format that somebody had decided on in the nineteen-fifties and that nobody had changed since:

*Source. Page. Printing A → Printing B. Before. After.*

That's it. That's the whole discipline. Forty-one times.

I'm not going to go through them. I will later, and they'll earn it. I'll tell you what I did that Sunday, which is that I read the first ten entries with my heart going harder than it had any business going, and they were all books I'd never heard of, printed before the war, in English and German and once in Dutch, and in every one a sentence had gone in one printing and come out another with its middle changed, and in every one the typos were untouched, and in every one the author was dead or gone or had publicly, carefully, taken it back.

Then I got to eleven.

*Tresidder, H. M. Waters Before Memory. 1926. p. 118. 1st imp. → 2nd imp. (Leiden, 1931). "transmitted to the early" → "arisen among the first."*

In the old hand. The brown ink. The grandfather's.

I put the book down on the table and sat back and looked at it from a distance, the way you look at a thing you've just understood is older than you.

My sentence. Mine. The one I'd found on a kitchen floor with a wet rope on my chest at four in the morning, sure I was the only man alive who'd ever read page 118 twice. Found, and written down, in a notebook, by a man who'd been dead for fifty years, and then kept, and added to, by his grandson, who'd bought a second impression in 2011 from a dealer in Groningen specifically so that he'd have one that *did* say the new sentence, to hold against the one his grandfather had kept that didn't.

I hadn't found anything. I'd been *handed* it. Twelve books, "because twelve sounds better," and the thin one at the bottom, and the first impression two spines above it. I was the thirtieth man to walk past that sentence and the third to stop.

Two options, I told Buddy, because he was there.

One: the crate was an accident. A man died, his daughter kept the furniture, a dealer with a beard bought the books by the metre and a man with a hobby happened to be the one kneeling on the cobblestones that Sunday. It happens. It's the ordinary way that old things find new idiots.

Two: it wasn't.

I didn't like two. I liked it less than I'd liked anything in the whole business so far, because two meant I wasn't hunting. Two meant I was *being fed.* And I sat there and gave it fifteen per cent, and I'd like to tell you I was being generous.

Then I turned to the last page.

Coordinates. Two numbers, lat and long, in the shaky hand, and I knew without looking them up what part of the world they were in, because I'd read about a man walking east out of a Turkish town in 1929 and not coming back. A date, circled three times. And under it, one word, the only word in the whole book that wasn't a citation:

*Ask.*

The date was the previous March. I checked it against the dealer's letter, against what Beard Man had said about the estate, against the little I'd found online about a retired reader in comparative religion from Deventer who'd died at home. The circled date was three weeks after the professor's funeral.

He'd written down a day he wasn't going to see. And a word. And then, I suppose, he'd sat in his chair.

---

Ana came home at six with her mother's leftovers and found me at the table with the thin book closed in front of me and my hands flat on either side of it like a man keeping a lid on something.

"You've got the face."

"I know."

"Is this the measuring thing?"

"It's the measuring thing."

She put the leftovers in the fridge. She let Mora out. She came back and stood behind me and put her chin on top of my head, which she does, and looked down at the closed book.

"Are you going to tell me?"

"Yes," I said. "Not all of it. Not yet. I don't have it in a shape yet. But yes."

I felt her nod. "Okay, Mulder."

She went up to change. I sat there a while longer.

Here's what I didn't tell her, and it was the first thing that wasn't a small honest-marriage lie, so I'm going to say it plainly. The professor hadn't just found what I'd found. He'd found forty more. He'd been doing it for thirty years, with his grandfather's notebook, quietly, and nobody had laughed at him, because he'd never told anyone. And then one spring he'd written down a date and a word and died in a chair.

I'd spent three weekends trying to kill the thing. I'd failed. That was the control case.

And somewhere, on a server I couldn't find, at fourteen minutes past two, something was still sending forty kilobytes to nowhere.
