# A room with a long table

The table was long because the room had been built for twelve and was only ever used by two, and Bram Lievens sat at the end of it with his back to the window because he liked people to have the light, and because he'd been told once that it was kind, and he had taken it to heart.

"Sit anywhere," he said. "No, not there. That's where the chairman sits when there's a chairman. Sit here. How's the dog?"

"Which?"

"His. The old one."

"Slow. Happy. He puts his hand on its head when he's thinking and doesn't know he's doing it." He sat. "I've told you that."

"You have. I like hearing it." Bram had a folder and didn't open it. He never opened the folder in the first ten minutes; he'd explained once that the folder was for after, that the first ten minutes were for the man, and that he'd learned this from someone who'd learned it from someone, and that it was the only part of the job he'd have missed. "The access went through."

"Yes. Eleven minutes. He thanked me."

"He thanked *Julian.*"

"He thanked the name on the invoice. That's all anyone ever thanks." He put his hands on the table, flat. "He's read the ledger history. He's seen the steps. He'll have the dates by now; he's that sort. Ninety-six, oh-eight, nineteen. July."

"July," Bram agreed, mildly. "That was you, I think. The quarter."

"That was the index."

"The index is a number, Julian. Somebody has to read it to somebody, and somebody has to write the sum down, and that's me, and I write what I'm told." He smiled. He had a good smile, apologetic, as though the world had been arranged slightly to his advantage and he'd noticed and was sorry. "Tell me what he's doing. Not what he's stopped. You've been asked that before."

"He's reading. He's quiet. He's stopped calling his mother, which the wife has noticed. He plays on Wednesdays and he's happy on Wednesdays and he doesn't ask why. He's been to Leiden."

"We know about Leiden. Leiden's been quiet for nine years. Leiden's a model." Bram turned the folder a quarter turn on the table without opening it. "And the other thing?"

"No."

"Not yet, or no?"

"Not yet." He said it carefully. "He's close to it. He's got a chair in the kitchen he sits in with the dog. He says things to the dog. I've heard some of them. He's got all the parts of it and he's never put them together, because he's a man who checks paper, and it isn't on paper."

Bram was quiet for a moment, and then he did open the folder, and took out one sheet, and turned it round so that the light fell on it, and slid it halfway down the table, where it stopped.

"I'd like you to look at this and tell me what you see. Honestly. I'd like to know whether I'm reading it right."

Two columns. The left one was a code and a number and a date. The right one was the same code, and a different number, and the same date.

"It's the weekly."

"It's the weekly for one subject. His. Over since April." Bram sat back. "Left is reporting. Posts, enquiries, letters to dealers, visits to curtain shops. You can see him go loud in July and go quiet in September. Lovely. Textbook. You've done well. The right column is the *index*." He let it sit. "Which has gone up every week since April and has not come down once. Not when he went quiet. Not when the access was pulled. Not when you gave it back. Not when his wife stopped kissing his head, which, yes, we know, it's a small house."

"I don't understand the right column."

"No. Nobody at your level does. Nobody at mine did, for years. I'll tell you what I was told, which is more than I should, because I like you, and because I think it'll make you better at this." Bram folded his hands. "It was never belief. We don't count belief. Belief's weather; belief goes up every time somebody puts a light in the sky on television and down every time a magician's in the room. We've never been afraid of what people believe. The index counts the ones who've stopped being *afraid.* That's all. That's what the forty kilobytes are. A reading, every night, of how many people in a given population have, that week, stopped needing what we sell."

The man at the table didn't move.

"A frightened reader is a reader," Bram said. "He publishes, or he doesn't; he's loud or he's quiet; either way he wants something, and if a man wants something you can seat him. A man who's stopped being frightened doesn't want a seat. He doesn't want to be believed, even. He just *looks.* And you can't house that, Julian, and you can't buy it, and you can't laugh it off a stage, because he's not on the stage. He's in a kitchen with a dog. And the index goes up by one. And when the index goes up far enough, the people above me bring the review forward, and when the review comes, it comes for the population, not the man, because the man was never the point." He unfolded his hands. "That's the arithmetic. You've always known the arithmetic. You've just never known what it was counting."

"He's not stopped being afraid. He's terrified. He's not sleeping."

"He's terrified of *us*," said Bram. "That's different. That's new. Fear of us we can work with; fear of us is a man who'll take a seat. I'm talking about the other thing. The thing underneath. The one that was in him at his wife's grandmother's funeral and has been going out of him, a little, every week since, like a bath." He looked at the sheet. "He hasn't crossed. You're right. Not yet. But the index doesn't wait for the crossing. It counts the *slope.* And his slope is the steepest I've seen since Leiden, and Leiden went quiet nine years ago and we've never had a reading on her since, because she stopped *reporting*, and we only ever measured one column."

"So what do you want from me?"

"What I've always wanted. Be near. Know what he loves. And when he gets to the kitchen floor, which he will, I'd like to know the week." Bram slid the sheet back to himself. "Because that's when the review moves, Julian. Not when he finds the ledger. Not when he finds the style guide or the generals or whatever the Leiden woman put on her table. When he stops being afraid of dying. That's the only week that's ever mattered. Everything else is just a man reading."

He closed the folder.

"You've got a flask now, I hear."

"Yes."

"Good. I like that. Take him coffee." He stood, which meant it was over. "And Julian. The wife's going to find the schedule. They always do. It'll be an address. Let her. A frightened wife is a seat we don't have to pay for."

*The index was read on Monday. It was up. The review stayed at the end of the quarter, which was eleven weeks away, and the man with the flask drove to Zwolle and sat in a blue chair and did not turn the light on for longer than usual.*
