# Leiden

The message came through the forum, which I'd stopped reading, under the handle I'd had for twelve years and never once used for anything that mattered.

I should explain the handle. For twelve years, under a name that was a joke only I understood, I'd posted did-you-knows. The light kind. The steam engine. The calendar. A door ten metres up a wall. Never a theory. Never a question. A man standing at the edge of the strange and dark corner with his hands in his pockets, saying *huh, look at that*, and leaving. A few hundred people had followed it. Ana knew about it and thought it was sweet. It was the one place on the internet where I'd been, for twelve years, exactly as much of myself as I could afford.

And in July, after the week it vanished, I'd done the thing I'd deleted in the spring. I'd posted. Under that name. Not a question about the dead. A question about a sentence: *has anyone compared the two impressions of this 1926 survey, p. 118?* With the photographs. Dry as toast. Dull, correct, no theory, the Record's own format.

Three people had replied. Two said *interesting* and went away. The third was polite, long, and knowledgeable, and explained, courteously and fully, with reference to the ordinary practice of the trade, that variants of this kind were common, that the author had very likely revised the sentence himself before his departure, and that the Leiden houses of the period had been notoriously casual about recording such things. It was a lovely reply. I'd thanked him. And I'd gone to bed with a line from the third page of a style guide going round in my head like a song.

Six weeks later, a private message. No greeting.

*Whoever corrected you used a phrase my supervisor showed me on a photocopy in 2009. He said if I ever saw it in the wild I should assume the person who wrote it was not a scholar. Who are you?*

---

Her name was Noor Haverkamp and she lived in Leiden, which, if you've been paying attention, is where the plates were.

She had been Hendrik Maasen's last doctoral student. She'd left the university in 2017 when the library's special collections were "restructured" under a digitisation grant, which is a sentence that sounds like nothing and that she said in a tone I recognised, the tone I use for the word *migration.* Now she repaired paper for a living. Freelance. Which meant she could get into rooms I couldn't and knew what a page looked like under a light held at the wrong angle on purpose.

Her kitchen was small and the table was covered in a sheet of glass and under the glass, I understood after a while, was a page from something four hundred years old that she was keeping flat.

"Don't put your coffee on it."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Everyone dreams of it. It's a table." She sat. She was fifty, maybe, grey at the front, with the hands of a person who does one thing extremely carefully all day. "You want to know if you're mad."

"I want to know if I'm alone."

"That's a better question. The answer's no. Which doesn't help as much as you'd think." She pulled a folder across the glass, not opening it. "Hendrik had the list from his grandfather and he had me for four years, and he showed me the *Preferred Formulations* once, one page, a photocopy, in 2009, in his office with the door shut, and he said, Noor, if you ever see this phrasing in the wild, assume the person is not a scholar. And then he never mentioned it again and I thought he'd gone a bit strange, and then they restructured the collections and I understood what strange was for." She looked at me. "You were corrected on the forum by somebody using the preferred formulation for a bibliographical enquiry. Word for word. It's a template. They don't even change the template."

"Why would they?"

"Exactly. Why would they. Nobody's ever noticed it twice."

---

I'd brought the thin book. She'd known I would. She put on gloves to touch it, which told me more about her than anything she'd said, and she went through it the way I'd have gone through it if I'd been a better man, slowly, and she stopped where I'd stopped, at eleven, and then she stopped at twenty-six.

"This one," she said. "You've looked this one up."

"It's not a book."

"No." Entry twenty-six was a paper. *Verhoef, A. Utrecht, 1993. Withdrawn before publication. Reclassified: instrumentation.* And then, in the professor's hand, the only entry in the whole list with a note on it: *two rooms, 40 m, correlated. Grandson sent copy 2015. Same edit: "shared" → "coincident."*

"Anton Verhoef's grandson found Hendrik on a forum in 2015," Noor said. "Your forum. He had his grandfather's paper in a drawer, the original, with the word *shared* in it, and a copy of the version that went into the department's archive under instrumentation, with the word *coincident* in it, and he couldn't understand why anybody would have bothered. Hendrik could. Hendrik wrote to him. I scanned it." She closed the book. "It's the only entry on the list that isn't about the past. Everything else is a hand being taken out of a story about the beginning. This one's a hand being taken out of a story about *last Tuesday.* Two people in two rooms, and something moved."

"Why would the same people care about that?"

"That," said Noor, "is the only question. Hendrik spent his last three years on it. He thought he'd got it. He wrote a word at the end of the book."

"Ask."

"He asked. I've no idea who. And then he died in a chair, and his daughter sold his books to a man with a beard, and here you are in my kitchen." She took the gloves off. "So. Are you alone. No. Is that good. I'll tell you what I tell myself. I've never published a word of this. Not one. Not a forum post, not a footnote, not a conference paper with a careful title. I have the photocopy and I have what Hendrik told me and I have a job repairing paper, and I'm alive, and he's not, and I think those two facts are related, and I think you should think so too."

"The third page," I said. "*A reader who persists is to be offered the opportunity to publish.*"

"Yes."

"And then reviewed."

"Yes." She looked at me across four hundred years of paper under glass. "They don't stop you talking. They stop you being *heard.* And if they can't, they've got a word for what happens next, and it's a word from a budget meeting."

---

She had a book on the table. Not under the glass; on it. A paperback, French, with a title I could read and a text I couldn't, and a long section near the back that she'd tabbed.

"You know this?"

"I know of it. Generals. The nineties."

"Generals, an admiral, a report commissioned by nobody and published in a magazine, and the whole world laughed, and the last chapter is the one nobody reads, because by then they've got what they came for." She opened it at the tab. "The last chapter's a thought experiment. They put themselves in the shoes of whoever's out there, and they ask: if we had been watching a population for a thousand years, how would we manage contact? And they answer. In phases. They *write it down.* Covert observation for as long as you like. Then furtive appearances that can't quite be dismissed. Then influence, and I'm translating, *by providing sacred texts, by accentuating the intellectual and moral tendencies of selected individuals.* And then contact at the highest level of the states, kept secret, after which the contacted leaders run, their words, *information, disinformation and counter-information campaigns.*" She looked up. "They name the instruments. Science-fiction novels. Films. Advertisements. And I quote: *why not suitable sects?*"

I didn't say anything.

"They were thinking of aliens," Noor said. "That's the defence. They were writing about what *they* would do. Read the last paragraph." She turned the page and put her finger on it. "They say the only thing that would make contact *fail* is if the gods couldn't stay out of reach. If the population got close enough to see their faces, they'd stop being gods and start being *neighbours*, and the whole thing would collapse into, I'm translating, contingency." She took her finger off. "They were writing a manual. For themselves. In 1999. And they put it in a magazine, and everyone laughed, and it's been in print ever since, in plain sight, and the one thing it tells you, James, the one thing, is that the people running it are *terrified of being seen up close.*"

She said my name. I noticed. Nobody says it.

"So that's why I never published," Noor said, quietly. "Not because I'm afraid of them. Because I'm not *useful* to them unless I'm loud. A loud reader is a story they can manage. A quiet one is a neighbour."

---

I asked her the math on the way out, standing in her hall with my coat half on. I'd been carrying it for a month and she was the only person I'd met who could take it.

"One in five hundred," I said. "That's what I get. If it's real. One in five hundred people has been asked to do one small thing, once."

"That sounds low."

"Low?"

"You're counting the wrong column." She had her hand on the door. "You've worked out how many it takes to *run* it. Fine. Now do the other number. If this is a thing that gets done to people who notice, how many people have noticed? In a hundred years? Hendrik noticed. His grandfather noticed. Verhoef noticed, from the other end. I noticed. You. That's five I can name without trying, and I'm a woman who repairs paper in Leiden." She opened the door. The street was wet and ordinary. "One in five hundred is the ones who keep it running. The ones who've *seen* it, James. Do that column. And then ask yourself why there's a list of forty-one books and not a list of forty-one people, and whether that's because nobody made one, or because somebody did."

I stood on her step.

"You're asking the wrong column," she said again, kindly, and shut the door.

I walked to the station. It had stopped raining. I did the other column on the train, on the back of the ticket, badly, with a pen that kept giving up.

It wasn't low. It was the same number. The same ratio, from the other end: for every person who's been asked to do one small thing, there's one who's noticed something small and said nothing, and they don't know each other either, and the machine, if there was a machine, had been counting only one of those columns, and I had a feeling it was the wrong one.

Not alone, she'd said. Which doesn't help as much as you'd think.

She was wrong about that, as it turned out. It helped exactly as much as I'd thought. It just took a year.
