# A street. Night.

He walked the street twice, once in each direction, because a man who walks a street once is going somewhere and a man who walks it twice has lost something, and people forgive the second.

The Doberman didn't start up. He'd expected it to and had planned for the wait, and the silence told him what he'd already been told on Tuesday, which was that the wife had gone and taken the loud one, and he stood for a moment at the corner with his hands in his coat and let the information settle, the way you let a drink settle, before you decide what it's for.

The lights were on. Kitchen, and one upstairs. Through the kitchen window, the end of a table, a coat over something on it, a shelf by the far wall with a gap on it where a sleeve of pale green had been in a photograph on the wife's phone and wasn't now.

The old dog was at the window.

Not barking. Standing, with its front feet on the low sill where the radiator was, looking out, at him, steadily, the way it had looked at the heater all afternoon in October and never once at him. He looked back. He'd always liked dogs, and the liking was not a tool, whatever the people above him assumed, and the old dog knew it wasn't, and had decided that it didn't matter, and he respected that more than he respected most of what he'd been told in rooms.

"Hello, foreman," he said, quietly, to the glass.

The dog didn't move.

He went to the car. He'd parked it at the far end, under the one streetlight that was out, which he'd arranged in September with a catapult and a ball bearing because it was a long job and a man thinks ahead. He sat. He didn't start the engine. He took the flask out of the glovebox and found it empty and put it back.

Eleven minutes.

He didn't look at his phone. He didn't look at the house. He looked at the windscreen, at the drops on it, and he thought about a sentence, the way he sometimes did, turning it to see if it had a side he hadn't seen. *A frightened wife is a seat we don't have to pay for.* It had a side. The side was that the wife had gone, and she had taken the loud dog, and the man had stayed with the one that couldn't do stairs, and that a frightened man alone in a house with an old dog is a man you can seat, or it is the other thing, and nobody at the long table had ever told him which, because nobody at the long table knew.

At nine minutes the light upstairs went off.

At eleven, the kitchen.

He got out of the car. He walked to the end of the street and along the back lane, where the gates were, counting, and stopped at the fourth, and looked at the latch, which was the kind that lifts, and at the gap under the gate, which was the width of a hand, and at the bowl just inside it that the old dog drank from in the day.

He stood there for a time that he did not measure.

Then he walked back along the lane, and round, and to the car, and drove to a flat with a name on the bell that wasn't his, and fed a cat.

In the morning he wrote four lines. *Visit completed. Companion present, wife absent. Subject not approached. The old dog is not a problem.* He read the last line twice before he sent it, because it was true, and because he had learned that the true lines were the ones that got read.

*The old dog was sick that night. It began at two. Nobody in this book will ever say what from.*
