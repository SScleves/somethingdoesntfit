# Enjoy it while it lasts

Meerwijk has a building with its name on it and a lobby with a ceiling you could land a plane in, and on my first morning a woman called Saskia walked me through it at the pace of somebody who'd walked a great many people through it and had stopped noticing the ceiling in 2009.

"You'll sit with Infrastructure," she said. "You don't report to them. You report to me, and I report to a committee, and the committee reports to the board, and nobody on the board has ever read anything you'll write. That's the arrangement. That's why you're here. You're the person who reads it."

"Reads what?"

"Everything." She stopped at a glass door and badged it. "The brief is simple. We have four hundred years of records and forty years of systems and nobody who's looked at the second as a whole. We want to know what we've got. Not what it does. What it *is.* You notice things. We've been told that. Notice."

I'd like to tell you I was suspicious. I was *flattered*, which I'm told is the same thing from the other end.

---

Here's what you need to know about a place like Meerwijk, and it took me a month to see it.

Nothing is thrown away. A finance house that's been going since people wore wigs does not delete; it *accumulates*, and the accumulation is the point, it's what they're selling, four centuries of never having lost a thing. Which means that under every system there's an older system, and under that an older one, and at the bottom, in the oldest layers, there are things that were set up by people whose names nobody knows for reasons nobody can find, and that still run, every night, because turning off a thing nobody understands is the one sin the house doesn't commit.

I read the ingredients. All of them. It's a bigger list than my old place by a factor of twenty and I read it the way I read the other, completely, with a growing sense of being the only person who ever had, and three weeks in, at eleven at night, in a building with the lights on timers, I found it.

Not `rcl-agg`. They don't call it that here. Here it was a *line*, in a ledger, in a system so old that the ledger was still called a ledger, and the line was a monthly payment, modest, unremarkable, the kind of sum a house like this spends on stationery, going out on the first of every month for thirty-one years to a foundation whose registered office was a post box, under a description that somebody had typed once in 1994 and nobody had touched since:

*Continuity reconciliation (recall index). Nightly.*

Nightly. Thirty-one years. And on the second screen, when I pulled the thread, the thing itself: a job, no owner, no ticket, that ran at fourteen minutes past two, for nine seconds, and sent forty kilobytes to an address that resolved nowhere.

I sat in a building with a ceiling you could land a plane in and felt my hands go cold for the second time that year.

It wasn't a diagnostic artefact from a migration at my old place. It was *here*. It had been here before my old place existed. And the one difference, the one thing Meerwijk's four centuries of never throwing anything away had given me that my old place couldn't, was a *name.* Recall index. Somebody, in 1994, had been asked what the line was for, and had written it down.

I didn't open a ticket this time. I'd learned that much. I wrote it in the Record, in the kitchen, at one in the morning, with Buddy under the table, and I photographed the screen, and I put the photograph in three places, and I told nobody. Not Noor, not Frank, not Ana.

Especially not Ana. And I knew that was wrong while I was doing it, and I did it anyway, and I filed that too.

---

Frank's tools arrived at Meerwijk in October. Not Frank; the tools. The AI-driven monitoring he'd sent the link about with the skull, two years earlier, in another life. *Enjoy it while it lasts, boys.* The house had bought them, through a consultancy, and the consultancy had done the setup, and I was asked to review the configuration before it went live, because that's what I was for.

I'd like to explain what a default is, because this is where the book turns and I don't want you to miss it because of a word.

When you buy a thing that watches, it comes with opinions. Out of the box, before anyone's touched it, it already knows what's normal and what isn't, what to wake you for and what to let through. Those opinions are called defaults, and nobody reads them, because they're long and they're boring and they've been chosen by experts, and the whole promise of the thing is that you don't have to. You install it. It watches. You sleep.

I read them. I read all of them. Four hundred pages of what the thing had been told was normal.

And on page two hundred and twelve, in a section about scheduled jobs, there was a class of thing the tool had been told *not to report.* Ever. Not to log, not to flag, not to show. A pattern, described in the dry language of patterns: *jobs with no owner, no ticket, and no history, running on a nightly schedule and emitting small payloads to unresolved addresses.* Classified, out of the box, by the experts, as *noise.*

My job. My forty kilobytes. Described in a sentence, in a default, in a product sold to ten thousand companies, as the one kind of thing the watcher should never see.

Nobody had hidden anything. I want to say that as plainly as I can. There was no secret file, no hooded man. Somebody had just been *boring in the right place.* They'd written one sentence into the defaults of a tool that would be installed everywhere, by people who'd never read it, and from then on every company on earth that bought the thing would have a watcher that had been told, before it opened its eyes, that there was one shape it would never have to look at.

The ruleset had a version number. *Rev. 4.* And a review date, at the bottom, the way a policy has a review date, and the date was the end of the quarter.

---

I asked for access to the ledger history. Not the line; the *history* of the line, who'd set it up, what the foundation was, where the post box went. That's the kind of thing a man in my position asks for a dozen times a week, and a dozen times a week somebody says yes within the hour.

On Monday somebody said no.

Not rudely. There was a *process.* The ledger history was in a category of records that required a role I didn't have, and the role required a form, and the form required a sponsor, and Saskia, who'd walked me through a lobby telling me to notice everything, said she'd look into it in a voice that meant she'd been told not to.

On Tuesday I found that my access to the list of scheduled jobs, the ingredients, the thing I'd been hired to read, had been quietly narrowed. I could see everything except one class of job. I'll let you guess which.

I sat at my desk and did the arithmetic. They'd taken away exactly what I'd need to see it again. Not more. Not my job, not my badge, not my login. Just the one shape. It was the most *precise* thing that had ever been done to me, and it had been done with a form.

And on Wednesday, at seven, on the sixth green, Dave said, "You look like a man whose bank's being difficult."

"It's not a bank."

"Whatever it is, it's got the face." He crouched for Buddy. "Hello, foreman. Is he being a misery? He's being a misery." And then, standing, easily, the way he said everything: "If it's an access thing, it's always an access thing, the consultancy that did your tools will have someone. They always have someone. That's what you're paying them for. Ask."

"Ask who?"

"Whoever's name's on the invoice, Jim. That's who you always ask. The person who'd be embarrassed."

I did. That afternoon. I wrote to the consultancy whose name was on the invoice for the tools, a dull two-line email, *could someone help me with a role request that's stalled*, and I got a reply in eleven minutes from a man who signed himself *Julian K.*, who was sorry to hear it, who'd had a word, and who thought it'd be sorted by close of play.

It was. By four. The ledger history was open. The form had never been needed. Saskia came past my desk and said, "Oh, that got through, did it, good," in a voice that meant it hadn't gone through her.

I wrote back to thank him. He wrote back to say it was nothing, that these things were always a form somebody'd forgotten, and that if I ever needed anything else, he was the person who'd be embarrassed, so I should ask.

*The person who'd be embarrassed.* Dave's phrase. In Julian's mouth. On a Wednesday.

I noticed. I'd like you to know that I noticed, and that I filed it under *coincidence*, under *it's a common phrase*, under *everyone in continuity talks like that*, and that the filing system doesn't ask questions, because that's what it's for.

I check paper. I don't check people. It's the thing I'm worst at and it's the thing that nearly killed me.

---

That night I opened the ledger history and read thirty-one years of a payment to a post box, and I found the thing I'd known I'd find, which was that it had been set up in 1994 by a man whose name appeared nowhere else in four centuries of records, and I found the thing I hadn't, which was that the sum had changed. Four times. Not with inflation. In steps. 1996. 2008. 2019. And this year, in July, the week I'd opened a ticket in another building about a job that didn't exist.

Up each time. By a clean multiple. The kind of number a committee agrees.

Somebody had been told, four times in thirty-one years, that the index was up, and had voted the money to match.

And the fifth time, it had been me.
