# Frank's kitchen

Frank asked me round on a Saturday, which should have told me something, because Frank is twenty-nine and single and lives in a flat where the kitchen is also the living room and the bedroom is a mezzanine, and men like that don't ask colleagues round. They ask *friends* round. I'd apparently crossed a line without noticing, the way I cross most lines.

"Beer," he said, at the door. "And I need you to look at something. Properly. With the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely have a face. Pieter calls it the audit face."

There was a television on with the sound down. A hearing, somewhere, men in suits behind a long table, a chyron with the word *phenomena* in it. Frank had it on the way you have the weather on. Neither of us looked at it all evening, and I'm telling you that now because I didn't notice it then, and I should have, and that's the whole point of everything that follows.

---

The first thing was a video.

Somebody had sent it to him, somebody who sends him things, and he wanted it killed and he wanted to watch me do it, and I'll admit that the persona enjoyed that. It was a montage. Dates of earthquakes against phases of the moon, with a voice over it that had learned to sound like a documentary and a graph that went up and to the right with the confidence of a man who has never had to show his working.

"Right," I said. "Pause it. Go back. The graph."

He went back.

"It's not a graph. There's no axis. It's a line somebody drew on a picture of a graph. Second thing: what's the baseline? How many earthquakes happen on days that *aren't* full moons? He's never going to tell you, because the answer is *the same number*, and if you've got no baseline you've got no correlation, you've got a list of nights that something happened, and every night something happens." I drank. "Third thing, and this is the one that matters. Look at the dates he picked. He's got fourteen. There were two hundred quakes above magnitude six in that period. He picked the fourteen that fit. That's not a pattern. That's a man with a drawer full of socks telling you every sock is blue, having thrown the red ones away first."

Frank was grinning. "Go on."

"That's it. That's all it is. It's noise. It's beautiful noise, somebody spent a week on the voice, but it's noise, and you don't need me for it, you need a sock drawer."

"I needed to hear you say it." He killed the video. "Okay. Round two. This one's not a video. This one's got a *book.*"

---

Round two was the one about the missing three hundred years.

You might know it. There's a theory, and it has a book, and the book has footnotes, and the footnotes are what get you, because they look like the real thing. The claim is that a stretch of the early Middle Ages never happened: that a few hundred years were inserted into the calendar by people with reasons, and that's why there's so little from that period, and that's why the dates of a few old buildings don't add up, and that's why a certain emperor seems to have done too much in one lifetime.

"I don't believe it," Frank said. "Obviously. But I can't *kill* it. Every time I go to kill it the footnotes come up."

"Tree rings," I said.

"What?"

"You don't need the footnotes. Trees. There's a continuous record of tree rings going back twelve thousand years, overlapping, logs out of bogs matched to logs out of roofs matched to living oaks. Year by year. No gaps. If somebody'd inserted three hundred years into the calendar, there'd be three hundred years of rings that didn't exist, and there aren't. The trees don't know what year the pope said it was." I drank. "Also the Chinese kept their own calendar and their own eclipses and never got the memo. Also the Mayans. Also, the eclipses. Astronomers can run the sky backwards; you can't fake where the moon was. It dies on tree rings in four minutes. You don't need a book."

Frank looked at me for a while.

"Then why is there a documentary?"

And that's the thing about Frank. He's a skeptic the way I'm a skeptic, which is that the fastest route to the wrong answer bores him, and he doesn't want to be told a thing is stupid. He wants to be told *why someone made a film about it.*

"I don't know," I said. And then, because it was Frank's kitchen and the beer was good and I'd had a bad fortnight: "Here's what I notice about it, for what it's worth. A theory refuted by tree rings doesn't need a documentary. It needs a footnote. Somebody made a documentary anyway. Somebody put the money and the voice and the drone shots into a thing that dies on oak. And when I see an explanation working harder than the mystery, I notice. When I see the *ridicule* working harder than the mystery, I notice that too."

"You think it's a plant."

"I think it's a sock drawer. I think people are shown the blue ones so that when a red one turns up they say *oh, that's one of those.*" I shrugged. "Or I think a man with a book needed a second mortgage. It's usually that."

---

Round three was Frank's. He'd been waiting for it.

He has a list. I should have guessed; he's that kind of mind. On his laptop, a document, years old, headed *Things people believe* and under it two columns: *Still standing* and *Dead.* He's kept it since university. When he hears a claim he puts it in the left column, and when it dies he moves it to the right, with the date and the reason, and he's never once moved anything back.

"This is how I sleep," he said. "Everything goes in. Everything gets a trial. Crashed saucers, dead: the metal was pots and pans, there's a magazine article from 1952. The leaked government documents from the eighties, dead: wrong typewriter, the signature was traced off a letter from 1947, the FBI said so. The man who said he'd worked on the alien craft, dead: he lied about his degrees and had a felony. The water memory thing, dead: a magician was in the room when they ran it blind." He scrolled. It was a long document. "And here's what I've got for your lot. The believers. You know what the single most damaging thing is, in the whole history of it?"

"Tell me."

He turned the laptop round. A scanned page, a declassified thing, 1953, a panel of scientists convened by people who had reasons.

"They *recommended* it," Frank said, with real pleasure. "The debunking. It's policy. They sat down in a room and wrote, I'm quoting, that the subject should be stripped of its special status and its aura of mystery, through a programme of, hang on, *training and debunking*, using mass media, psychologists, and, I love this, Disney. Walt Disney. It's not a conspiracy, James. It's a *memo.* Every time one of your forum people says they're being laughed at, they're right, and it's boring, it's a 1953 memo, it's the most bureaucratic thing I've ever read. That's my trump card. That's the whole thing in one page. It's not sinister, it's a committee."

I read the page. I took my time, because I take my time, and because I'd seen something, and I wanted to make sure it was there before I said anything, and it was.

"Read the next line," I said.

"What?"

"The line under the bit you read. Out loud."

He turned the laptop back round. He read it. He stopped halfway and read it again, and I watched his face do the thing mine does when I put a finger on the right line of page 118.

"'The burden of proof is on the sighter,'" Frank said, slowly, "'not the explainer.'"

"Yeah."

"That's — " He sat back. "That's the opposite of science."

"It's a *rule.* Written down. By scientists. In a government document. It says that whoever sees the thing has to prove it, and whoever explains it away doesn't. That's not a committee being boring, Frank. That's a committee writing a *standard of proof.* And you've been using it. You've been using it for ten years, in that list, and so have I, and so has everyone we've ever met, and I'm not saying the saucers are real, I think the saucers are mostly pots and pans. I'm saying somebody wrote down the rule for how we'd be allowed to check, and then taught it to us, and then we forgot it was taught."

The television was still on. A man in a suit was saying something to another man in a suit. Neither of us looked.

Frank didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "Huh," and moved his cursor to the left column, and I watched him *not* move the 1953 memo to the right.

"It's still dead," he said. "The saucers."

"I know."

"I'm not moving it."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But the memo's — " He looked at it. "The memo's not in either column, is it. The memo's the *table.*"

I didn't say anything. He'd got there on his own, which is the only way anyone ever gets anywhere, and I've learned to shut up when it happens.

---

He walked me down. On the stairs he said, "You've gone quiet lately. At work. Pieter says you've gone quiet."

"Bad month."

"Was it the orphan job?"

I stopped on the stair. He was behind me and couldn't see my face, which is the only reason I let it do what it did.

"What orphan job?"

"I don't know, mate. You said something once, I can't — " He laughed. "Forget it. I'm pissed. Did you know they had to move the definition of hunter-gatherer? Göbekli Tepe. The site's older than farming, so they couldn't have farmers building it, so instead of moving the date they moved the *definition.* Hunter-gatherers can build temples now. That's a did-you-know. That's one of yours. I'm stealing it."

"It's a good one," I said. "Keep it."

I walked home. It wasn't far. The whole way I was thinking about a sock drawer, and a rule somebody had written down, and a man who'd remembered an orphan job for exactly one second on a staircase before the beer took it back.

Not noise, then. Not all of it.

Some of it was a table.
