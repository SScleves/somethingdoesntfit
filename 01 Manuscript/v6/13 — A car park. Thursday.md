# A car park. Thursday.

He took the call in the car, in the long-stay car park behind the station, because the car park had no cameras on the third level and because he had always preferred to be told things while looking at concrete.

"He's accepted," the voice said.

"Yes."

"And the Wednesday?"

"Twice now. He's easy. He wants a friend and doesn't know it, which is the best kind. I asked about the dog." He watched a woman two rows over fail to reverse into a space, and then succeed. "He's filed the job. The one at his old place. He's stopped asking about it."

"He's stopped asking at work."

"He's stopped asking."

A silence with paper in it. He knew the silence. It was the sound of a sentence being weighed, and he had chosen the sentence carefully, and he had chosen it to be light, and he waited to see whether the lightness would hold.

"You said *stopped asking,*" the voice said. "I've got a ticket closed in July, a screenshot we can't account for, a letter to a dealer, a visit to the daughter, and a man who now has the pages. I've got a reading on the index that moved the week he found the notebook and hasn't come back down. That's not a man who's stopped asking, Julian. That's a man who's stopped asking *out loud.*"

"He's not a publisher. He deleted the one post he ever wrote."

"That's worse. You know that's worse." A pen, somewhere, on a desk he'd never seen. "I'm going to put the review at the end of the quarter."

"It was the end of the year."

"It was. And now I've got a reader at level two who's been handed a style guide by a woman who sells curtains, and a handler who tells me the reader's gone quiet. You brought it forward, not me. Every time you make him smaller than he is, I have to make the date nearer than it was. That's the arithmetic. You've always known the arithmetic."

He didn't say anything. The woman two rows over got out of her car and looked at how she'd parked it and got back in.

"How near are you?" the voice said.

"Near enough to know what he loves."

"Which is?"

"The old dog. The wife. Being right about a thing nobody else checked." He considered. "In that order, I think. He'd say the wife. It's the dog."

"Good. Then stay there. And Julian. Don't tell me he's stopped. Tell me what he's doing. I'd rather hear it from you than read it off the index."

"Understood."

The line went. He sat for a while with the phone in his lap and the concrete in front of him and did the arithmetic the voice had said he'd always known, which was that a date had just moved three months because of a verb, and that the verb had been his, and that this was the second time.

He had, in his coat pocket, a flask. He'd bought it on the Tuesday, because he'd said he was a flask man, and he had never in his life owned a flask, and he'd found that he wanted the sentence to have been true when he said it.

He put the phone in the glovebox. He drove to the lock-up near Zwolle and sat in the blue chair for twenty minutes without turning the light on. Then he drove home, and fed a cat that was not his, that belonged to the flat, that came with the flat, the way the flat came with the name on the bell.

*The review was moved to the end of the quarter. The index was read on Monday. It was up.*
