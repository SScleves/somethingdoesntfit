# Leiden. A printer's shop. 1931.

The visitor had a good coat and did not take it off, and he stood in the composing room with the smell of ink and hot metal around him as though it were a smell he'd known all his life, which the printer, whose name was Van der Hoeven and whose father's name had been on the door before his, took to be a courtesy and later understood to be a method.

"One line," the visitor said. "Page one hundred and eighteen. I have it written out."

He had. On a card, in a hand that had been taught to be legible, with the old words on the left and the new words on the right, and a vertical rule between them, drawn with a straightedge.

Van der Hoeven read it twice. He was a careful man. His father had been a careful man and had gone bankrupt being one, and the son had learned that care was a thing you did with your hands and not with your opinions, and so he read the card with his hands, holding it to the lamp, and then he said, "It's not a correction."

"No."

"The old line is not wrong. I don't read English well. But I read enough. The old line is a scholar's sentence. The new line is also a scholar's sentence. They say different things, and neither of them is a mistake."

"That's right."

"Then I don't understand what you're paying me for."

The visitor looked, for a moment, almost pleased. "You're the fourth printer I've asked," he said. "The first three understood immediately. I'd rather it was you."

He explained. Not why; never why. What. The plate for page 118 was to be opened. The one line was to be re-set, exactly, with the spacing adjusted so that the justification would hold and the line count would not change. Nothing else on the plate was to be touched. There were two misprints on that page, and a flaw in the top margin where the metal had pitted, and they were to stay. The copyright page was to say *second impression* and nothing more. No *corrected.* No *revised.* The book was to go out under the same words on the title page, to the same continental trade, at the same price, and the author, who had walked into some hills in Anatolia two years before and had not walked out, was not to be mentioned, because there was no one to mention him to.

"A reader will see it," Van der Hoeven said. "Someone. Eventually. Somebody will have both."

"Somebody will. In fifty years, or a hundred. And when he does, he'll find two typos left in and a flaw in the margin, and he'll think: *they didn't even clean the plate. Who would go to the trouble of changing one line and fix nothing else?* And he'll decide that it must have been the author, before he went. Or a lazy compositor. Or nothing." The visitor put the card down on the stone. "The misprints aren't an oversight, Mr Van der Hoeven. They're the *reason* he'll stop looking. A clean plate is a confession. A dirty one is just a printer."

Van der Hoeven looked at the card on the stone for a long time.

"My father would have refused."

"Your father went bankrupt."

"Yes."

"I'm not threatening you. I want to be clear about that. I don't do that. I'm telling you that there is a foundation, in this city, that is about to take an interest in the Leiden printing trade, and that it will be placing work for forty years, and that the houses it places work with will be the houses that are still here in forty years. That's all. I've told the other three the same thing. I'd rather it was you, because you asked what you were being paid for, and in my experience the man who asks that is the man who'll do it properly and then never think about it again." He buttoned his coat, which had never been unbuttoned. "You have two sons."

"Yes."

"They'll be looked after. Not because of this. Because of the forty years. This is one line." He went to the door. "I'll come back on Thursday for the proof. Leave the flaw."

Van der Hoeven set the line himself. He did it at night, when the apprentices had gone, because he found that he didn't want to be watched doing it, and he noticed himself not wanting that, and did it anyway. He set it with the spacing adjusted so that the justification held. He left the two misprints. He left the pit in the margin, and when he inked the proof and pulled it and held it to the lamp, the page was perfect, and identical, and said something else, and he stood in his father's shop with it in his hands and felt, he would have said, if there had been anyone to say it to, like a man who has been asked to move a chair in a room and has done so and the room is the same room and will never be the same room again.

On the Thursday the visitor came, and read the proof with his hands, and nodded, and paid, in cash, and did not haggle.

"It's very good," he said. "You'll never see me again."

He did not. The foundation placed work with the house for forty-one years. The two sons took it over in 1958 and sold it in 1972 to a firm that became a firm that became a firm, and the plates were melted in a war, and the books went out, every copy, under the same words on the title page, to every library and every dealer and every scan, wearing the first impression's clothes.

*Van der Hoeven kept the card. He never knew why. His grandson found it in a drawer in 1994, with a straightedge rule down the middle, and threw it away, because it was in English, and it was one line.*
