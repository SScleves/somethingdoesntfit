# Leave it

I'm going to tell you about a Tuesday at work, and I need you to stay with me through it, because it's the most boring thing in this story and it turned out to be the whole story.

My job, in one sentence, is to look at screens that look at other things. Ten thousand systems, every night, breathing, and my programs count the breaths and put the counts on a wall. Green is fine. Amber is a meeting. Red is my phone at four in the morning. Most of my working life happens in the green, which sounds restful and is actually a kind of low-grade dread, because green doesn't mean nothing's wrong. Green means nothing's wrong *that we decided to look for.*

Which is why I have the habit. I don't look at the colours. I look at the list under the colours. All the little jobs, the things that run on timers, the housekeeping. Every one has a name, an owner, and a reason, or it's supposed to. In a company our size the list is a few thousand lines long, and nobody reads it, the way nobody reads the ingredients, and I read it the way I read the ingredients, which is to say completely and with a growing sense that I'm the only person who ever has.

The sentence had been in my kitchen for a week by then. Page 118 was in my bag, both copies, in a folder, because I'd stopped trusting the house to keep them. And I'd been sleeping badly, and I don't know whether that's why I saw it or whether I'd have seen it anyway. I've thought about that more than I should. I'd like to think I'd have seen it anyway.

It was called `rcl-agg`.

That's it. That's the name. Seven characters, which is the kind of name you give something when you're in a hurry or when you don't want it to be read. No owner. The owner field, which is mandatory, which you literally cannot save the form without, was blank. No ticket. No description. And it ran, every night, at fourteen minutes past two, for about nine seconds, and sent roughly forty kilobytes of something to an address that, when I looked it up, resolved nowhere. Not to one of ours. Not to a cloud. Nowhere. The name of the address had the shape of a real one, like a sentence has the shape of a real sentence, and when you asked the internet where it was, the internet said it had never heard of it.

I did the obvious things. I checked the history: who created it, when. There was no history. The first entry in the history of `rcl-agg` was `rcl-agg`, already running, like it had been there before the list was. I checked the other offices. Same thing, same minute, same forty kilobytes, in all of them. I checked the backups going back as far as the backups go, which is a long way, because the man who set up the backups was paranoid in the useful way, and it was in all of them too. Every night. Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds. Nowhere.

I sat with that for a while. Then I wrote it down and put it in the stand-up.

---

Stand-up is fifteen minutes in a glass box, standing, so that nobody gets comfortable enough to talk. Pieter runs it. Pieter is my manager and a decent man in the specific way that managers are decent, which is that he will defend you to the end of the earth about anything that doesn't cost him anything.

I went last. I always go last; the persona likes to see the room first.

"One thing," I said. "There's a job on the list with no owner. Runs nightly. Sends data to an address that doesn't exist. I want to find out what it is."

"Which job?"

I told him.

Something happened in Pieter's face. I don't want to make it bigger than it was. It was the size of a man remembering an appointment. "That one's old," he said. "It's a diagnostic artefact. From the migration."

"Which migration?"

"The big one. Before your time." He looked at his screen. "Leave it."

"It sends data somewhere."

"It sends diagnostics somewhere. It's fine. It's been fine for years. Leave it, James, honestly, we've got the payments thing and the Antwerp thing and I'd rather have you on either of those than on a cron job from the Stone Age." He smiled, and it was a real smile, and he meant it, and the meeting moved on, and Frank caught my eye across the box and did a little skull with his hand, which is our thing now, apparently, which I've decided to find charming.

Here's the problem. I was there for the big migration. I did the big migration, a lot of it, two years before Pieter joined. There wasn't a `rcl-agg` in it. I'd have known. I read the ingredients.

I didn't say that. I want to be clear that the reason I didn't say it was not courage or cunning. I didn't say it because Pieter had said *leave it* in the tone you use for something that's already been decided, and I'd spent thirty years learning that when my memory and the paperwork disagree, I'm the error. So I nodded. And I walked back to my desk, and I sat down, and I felt the thing I'd felt in the kitchen with the two books, the old feeling lifting its head, and I understood, for the first time in my life, that I was feeling it *about a person.*

Because Pieter hadn't been wrong. People are wrong all the time; it's the main thing people are. Pieter had been *ready.* He'd had the sentence before I'd finished the question. A diagnostic artefact from the migration. Said the way you say your postcode.

Why lie here?

I'd like to say that's where I started. It isn't. Where I started was: *why would anyone bother?* And that question kept me at my desk until seven, doing nothing, with the list open, reading the ingredients.

---

Ana was in the kitchen with the dogs when I got home, and the dogs were in the kitchen with Ana's dinner, strategically.

"You're late."

"Payments thing."

"Mm." She was reading something on her phone, and she lifted it without looking at me, the way she lifts it when she wants me to see that she's not looking. "You've got a letter. Actual paper. It's on the side."

It was a good envelope, the kind with a lining, and my name was on it in a hand I didn't know, and that was the first time in this whole business that I saw my own name written down by somebody who was part of it. I'd written, two weeks before, to a dealer in the north, an antiquarian, a man with a website from 2004 and a reputation, asking a dull question: whether he'd ever seen a Leiden-printed second impression of a 1926 English survey of flood legends, and whether he happened to know who'd done the printing.

*Dear Mr Klippe,* it began, and I stood in my kitchen and felt oddly caught, as if I'd been spotted in a shop I'd told everyone I didn't go to.

He had seen one. He'd sold one, in 2011, to a university man in Deventer, who'd been very particular about the impression. He didn't know the printer; the Leiden houses of that period were "mostly gone and mostly undocumented, as I expect you know." He wished me luck with "what I assume is a bibliographical question," and then, as a postscript, in a slightly different ink, as though he'd come back to the letter after a cup of tea: *I would gently say that this title attracts a certain kind of enquiry, and that I have found, over forty years, that it is wisest to be plain about one's reasons.*

I read that twice. Then I read it to Buddy, who was under the table in case of a chicken event.

"'Plain about one's reasons,'" I said. "What does that mean?"

"It means he thinks you're a nutter," said Ana, not looking up. "What's the book?"

"The one with the two copies."

"The one you measured."

"The one I measured."

She put the phone down then. "And a man in a shop in — where?"

"Groningen."

"A man in a shop in Groningen has written you a letter on nice paper to tell you to be careful."

"To be *plain.*"

"That's what careful sounds like in a shop." She picked the phone back up. "Did you know Mora ate half a sponge today? Just so we're all plain about our reasons."

I laughed. It was a good line. She has better lines than I do and always has; mine are built and hers just arrive. And I sat down and ate with them, and told her a did-you-know about the Ethiopian calendar being seven years behind everybody else's because they'd counted the start of things differently and never seen a reason to stop, and she said that was a good one, and asked if that meant she was thirty-one in Ethiopia, and I said it did, and she said she'd think about moving.

It was a good evening. I'd like that known. There were a lot of good evenings and I was there for all of them, and I'm not going to pretend I wasn't, to make what came later look cleaner than it was.

---

At one in the morning I was at the kitchen table with the laptop, because of course I was.

I'd like to tell you I was thinking about Tresidder. I wasn't. I was thinking about Pieter's face. About how the sentence had been ready. And about a thing I'd read years ago and filed under interesting-wrong and never thought about again: that the easiest way to hide something isn't to hide it. It's to give it a boring name, and a boring reason, and put it on a list that nobody reads, in the one place where the people who do read lists have already been told to stop.

Two options, I told the dog, who was asleep and did not care.

One: it's exactly what Pieter said. An old diagnostic job that somebody forgot to document, sending its little report to a server that was decommissioned years ago, into the void, every night, like a dog waiting at a door for a man who's not coming home. Sad. Boring. Common as dirt. I've seen fifty of them.

Two: somebody knows exactly what it is. And has told Pieter what to say.

I'm a reasonable man. I gave option one eighty-five per cent, out of habit, and then I sat there and looked at that number and thought about where I'd last used it.

Then I did a small thing. Not a brave thing. A small thing, the kind you can walk back from.

I opened a ticket. Low priority. Plain language. *Unowned scheduled job `rcl-agg` — request owner and purpose for the inventory.* I assigned it to nobody, which is how you assign a thing to the system itself, and I put it in the queue where the dull tickets go to be ignored for a year, and I went to bed.

I'd like to be able to tell you I slept.

Forty kilobytes. Every night. For years. To nowhere.

Nothing waits that long at a door unless it's been told the door will open.
