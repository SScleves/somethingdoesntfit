# Deventer. A Thursday in March.

The old man had written to them on paper, with a stamp, to an address that was a foundation's registered office and nothing else, and he had asked, in four lines, in a hand that was not what it had been, whether somebody might come and talk to him about his notebook.

So somebody came.

He parked two streets away, because he always did, and walked, because the walk told him things: the bin at the gate, not yet out; the curtains, good ones, chosen by a woman; the bicycle with a child's seat on it that belonged to a neighbour. He rang. He waited longer than a younger man would have waited. The door opened on a face that had been expecting him for a long time and had not expected him to look like this.

"You're not what I thought," the professor said.

"People say that." He wiped his feet. "May I?"

The chair was blue and the professor lowered himself into it the way you lower something you're afraid of dropping. The notebook was on the side table, closed, under the man's hand, and the man did not look at it and did not look away from it, and the professor noticed both, and smiled.

"Tea?"

"Let me," the man said. "You sit."

He made tea in a kitchen that had a woman's handwriting on the calendar and a man's on the shopping list. He brought it through. He sat where he was told to sit, which was the chair opposite, the one with the better light on it, and he understood that he had been placed, and he let it happen, because there was no harm in it.

"I'll say what I've got," the professor said, "and you'll say nothing, and then you'll go. That's how it works, isn't it?"

"More or less."

"Forty-one," the professor said. "My grandfather found the first eleven. I found the rest. I've never published. I've never told anyone but my daughter that the book exists, and I told her it was a bibliography, which it is." His hand on the cover. "I'm not asking you to confirm anything. I'm old and I'm not sleeping and I'd like, before I stop, to have asked somebody. That's all. I've written it down. The one word. It's the last thing in there. I wanted to have done it."

The man drank his tea.

"They're all the same edit," the professor said. "Did you know that? I expect you know that. Forty-one books and it's always the same correction, in different clothes. A hand comes out. A giver goes. *Early* becomes *first*. Nobody ever adds anything. You only ever take one thing away." He was looking at the window now. "It's very disciplined. I've admired it, God help me. Thirty years I've admired it."

"You should rest," the man said, gently.

"Is that what you came to say?"

"I came because you asked." He put his cup down. "Nobody comes if you don't. That's the only rule I know for certain."

The professor laughed, which turned into something else, and he waited it out with his hand over his mouth, and then he said, "My daughter's going to be alright. Tell me that, at least. You can say that."

"Your daughter's going to be alright."

"You say it like you mean it."

"I do mean it." He stood. He took the two cups to the kitchen and rinsed them and left them on the rack, the way a guest does. When he came back the professor's eyes were closed and his hand was still on the notebook, and the man stood for a moment in the good light looking at an old man asleep in a blue chair, and then he let himself out, and pulled the door until it clicked, and walked the two streets to the car.

He made one call. "He's asked. Yes. In person. No, nothing. He's tired. The item's on the side table, I'd say weeks." A pause. "I'd rather wait. A body's a fingerprint, you've said so yourself." Another pause, longer. "Understood."

*

Eleven days later the bin was not put out on a Monday and the daughter let herself in with her key.

*

He came back for the furniture in June, with a van and a man he paid in cash, because the daughter had put a card in the window and the card had been reported to him and it was cleaner to be the buyer than to be anything else. He bought the shelves and the blue chair and he did not haggle, and when he asked about the dresser she said no, that one was her mother's, she was keeping that, and he said of course, and the daughter, who had her father's hands, said she was glad the shelves were going to somebody who wanted them.

"And the books?" he said, lightly, as he had planned to say it.

"Oh, those went," she said. "Weeks ago. Man from the market. He takes anything, by the crate. I couldn't look at them."

He stood in the emptied room and let his face do nothing at all.

"Of course," he said. "That's the best thing for them."

He drove the chair and the shelves to a lock-up near Zwolle, and he went through the chair, the lining and the cushions and the frame, properly, and found nothing, and the following Sunday he went to the market in a good coat and stood in the cold at a stall where a man with a beard was talking about a comet, and he looked at the ground, where the crates were, and there were two, and they were full of Reader's Digest.

"Professor's books," he said. "Were there any left?"

"Sold the lot," Beard Man said, not unkindly, mid-sentence. "Chap took twelve. Threw one in. Twelve sounds better."

He thanked him. He bought a condensed Hemingway for fifty cents, because a man who buys nothing is remembered. And he went and sat in the car, and he did not call anybody for eleven minutes, and then he did.

*He was not blamed. It had been a question of weeks, and he had been right to wait. His standing was confirmed at the review. The only thing that changed was that he would now have to find the chap who'd taken twelve, and he had always, in his way, enjoyed that part.*
