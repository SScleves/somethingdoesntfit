# The long table, again

"You've got the week," Bram said. It wasn't a question. He had the folder and he hadn't opened it, and he was looking at the man at the table the way he looked at the index on a Monday: fondly, and for a number.

"Yes."

"When?"

"Thursday. Between one and three. The light over the cooker was on till seven, which it never is; he turns it off when he goes up. He rang the wife at nine instead of her ringing him. He let the dog out and stood at the door for eleven minutes with a coffee, and he didn't check the street." He had his hands flat on the table. "He's not afraid."

"Of us?"

"Of anything. He's terrified of us, still, in the way you'd be terrified of a car. But the other thing's gone. You can see it. He stands differently." A pause. "He said *soul* to the dog."

Bram smiled. "Did he."

"He was embarrassed about it. He apologised. To the dog."

"That's the one I like. That's the one that goes in the file, if we kept that kind of file." Bram turned the folder a quarter turn. "And you watched it."

"I was in the lane. The back. Yes."

"And you'd know."

The man at the table didn't say anything.

"I'm not asking to be cruel, Julian. I'm asking because I've got six handlers who'd have stood in that lane and seen a man on a floor, and one who'd have seen what you saw, and I need to know I'm reading the right one." Bram folded his hands. "You'd know. You're the only one of mine who would."

"Yes."

"How long, for you?"

"Nine years."

"Leiden."

"The month after the transfer. Her kitchen. I'd been in and out with a clipboard for three weeks and on the last day she gave me tea and didn't say anything for twenty minutes and neither did I." He looked at the table. "I didn't go looking for it. It was in the room. It went through me the way it's gone through him, and I drove to Zwolle, and I sat in the car, and I understood what the index was. Before anyone told me."

"And you reported it."

"I reported it."

"Your own."

"I'd been told what the arithmetic was. I knew what a true line did to the date. I filed it, and I watched the review come forward by a quarter, and I understood that I had become the only instrument you had that could see the thing it was measuring, and that there was a seat in that." He lifted his hands off the table and put them back. "So I took the seat."

Bram was quiet for a long moment.

"I've always thought that was the bravest thing anyone's done in this building," he said, eventually, and he meant it, and that was the problem with him too. "And the saddest. You know that."

"Yes."

"And now there's another one. In a kitchen. With a dog. And he's not going to take a seat, is he, because he's past wanting one, and the only thing that stops him being a reading we can't act on is that you're the one who knows how to read him." Bram opened the folder, finally, and took out the sheet, and turned it to the light, and the right-hand column had a line on it that wasn't a number. It was a name. "I need the line, Julian. Hands, yes or no. In your words. It's the only week that's ever mattered. You said so yourself, in a car park, when you still thought it was about books."

The man at the table looked at the sheet for a long time.

"James has hands," he said.

The first name. He heard himself use it and so did Bram, and neither of them remarked on it, and it sat on the table between them like a cup.

"Thank you." Bram wrote. One word, in the right-hand column, next to the name. "The review's in three weeks. I'm going to recommend two. I'd like you to have a conversation with him before then. Not on a course. Somewhere with the wife. I'd like him to be offered what you were offered, properly, with the arithmetic, and I'd like you not to be there, because I think you'd be a problem for him and I think he'd be a problem for you." He closed the folder. "You'll be at the depot. There's a transfer. Your name's on it. That's not a punishment."

"I know what it is."

"Good." Bram stood. "The old dog. Was it you?"

"No."

"I'd believe you either way. I want you to know that." He went to the door, and stopped, with his hand on it, and said, not turning round: "Nine years, and you've never once asked me whether I've got them. Hands."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you'd have told me," the man said. "You tell everyone everything. It's how you seat them."

Bram laughed, once, surprised, and went out.

*The review was brought forward to two weeks. The index was read on Monday. It had gone up by one, and the line beside the one had a name on it, and for the first time in nine years the man with the flask did not drive to Zwolle, because the chair was no longer there. It had been transferred.*
