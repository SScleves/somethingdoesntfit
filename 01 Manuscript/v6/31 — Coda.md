# Coda

Buddy died on a Thursday in the spring, four years later, at home, on the blankets by the radiator, at a quarter past two in the morning, and I was on the floor with him, because that's where he was.

I'm not going to describe it. Not because it's private. Because it was *ordinary*, and that's the thing nobody tells you, and it's the thing I'd spent four years getting ready to find unbearable and found, in the end, to be the only thing about it that I could bear. He was old. He'd been slowing for a long time. One day the slowing finished. His head was on my knee. Ana was on the other side of him with her hand on his chest, flat, the way she does, and Mora was in the doorway not letting anyone in or out, and at some point his breathing changed, and then it stopped, and we both knew, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

Then Ana said, "Well. That's that, old man," in a perfectly steady voice, and started to cry, the real way, the way with no shape to it, and I held her, and I didn't say anything either.

Here's what I want to tell you. It's the only reason I've written any of this down.

Nobody said *he's in a better place.*

Nobody said *he's at peace now.* Nobody said *you'll see him again.* Not Ana, not me, not Ana's mother on the phone in the morning, not Frank, who came round with beer and sat on the kitchen floor where the blankets had been and said, "Fuck," and then, "He was a good foreman," and then nothing, which was exactly right. Nobody passed the words around. Nobody reached for the wall of comfort, because there isn't one for dogs, there's never been one, and it turned out that was the gift. We'd had four years of not needing it, and when the day came we found we didn't want it, and we buried him without it.

In the garden. By the back gate, where the bowl was. Ana's brother came with a spade and didn't say anything either. I'd checked the rules; you can, here, if the garden's yours and the hole's deep enough, and I made it deep enough, and I put him in it on his blanket, and I stood there in the grey light with the spade and I said the only thing I had, which was the truth.

"I don't know where you are. I never did. I know where you've been, which is here, and I know it was enough, because you told me so every single day for seventeen years, and I was listening, in the end. That's all I've got, mate. I'm not going to make anything up."

Ana put her hand on my back.

"That was a good one," she said.

"It wasn't a did-you-know."

"I know. That's why it was good."

---

The young dog came in the summer. Not a replacement; Ana was very clear about that, and so was Mora, who took one look at the puppy and left the room for a week. A brown thing, all feet, from a woman near Deventer who'd had a litter and a card in a window. We called her Ida. There was no argument about the name, which was a first.

And one evening in the autumn, when she was six months old and had never once in her life seen Buddy, I went up the stairs to bed, and I got to the top, and I turned round, the way you do, and she was sitting at the bottom step.

Not following. Sitting. Watching me go. With a patience that broke something in me every single time.

She'd never seen him do it. Nobody had taught her. It's not a thing dogs do; I've asked, I've read, I've checked, and dogs follow you up or they don't, and she could, she was six months old and made of springs, and she sat at the bottom of the stairs and watched me go up like a foreman.

I stood at the top for a long time.

Then I filed it. Under coincidence. Where it belongs, because there are a thousand things a dog can do and one of them has to be that one.

But I'd like you to hold it. That's all. Not believe it. Hold it, the way I held a clock that said 02:14. Something gets transmitted. I don't know what. I don't know how. I know it doesn't go without someone to catch it from, and I know I was standing at the top of the stairs, and I know she caught it.

---

Ana said a strange thing in the spring after that.

We were in bed, early, light coming in, and she woke up and looked at me for a second, longer than a second, with a face I'd never seen on her, the face you'd have if you'd been told a stranger's name and found you already knew it.

"I had the strangest — " she said. And stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing." She lay back. "I thought you were somebody else. For a second. Somebody I — " She shook her head. "It's gone. It was a dream. Your hands were different."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. Cracked. At the knuckles." She laughed, half asleep. "I knew them, though. That's the thing. I knew them from somewhere."

I didn't say anything. I lay there in the light and looked at the ceiling with one arm dead under my wife and I didn't say anything at all, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't have been a story, and I'd promised a dog I wouldn't make anything up.

Possible. Not promised. That's where I've left it. That's where I'm leaving it now.

The job still runs. Fourteen minutes past two. I don't check it every night any more. Most nights.

Frank sent me a link last month, with the skull. The new revision of the defaults for the tools, the ones that watch. *Rev. 6.* He'd read them; he reads them now, which is my fault, and he'd noticed that page 212 was gone. The class of thing the watcher was told never to see. Removed. No note, no reason, no *corrected.* One section, untouched everywhere else. He didn't know why. He thought it was funny. *Enjoy it while it lasts, boys.*

I don't know why either. I've stopped filing things I don't know. I think somebody, somewhere, who reads the queue where things go to die, read it, and did the only thing they had, which was one line, and left the typos in.

That's all. That's the book. A man who works in IT noticed a thing that didn't fit, and pulled it, and something on the other end pulled back, and it was a person, and the person turned out to have hands, and I'll never know what he did with them, and I've decided that's his. My wife doesn't believe a word of it and believes me. There's a brown dog at the bottom of the stairs. There's an old one by the back gate, under the bowl, where I can see him from the kitchen while I make the coffee.

Ana came down this morning while I was standing there looking at the gate, and put her chin on top of my head, which she does, and looked out at the garden with me.

"He's in the garden," she said.

Not *a better place.* Not *at peace.* The garden. The one claim about where the dead are that anybody in this house has ever been able to verify.

"Check," I said.
