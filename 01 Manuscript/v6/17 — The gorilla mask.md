# The gorilla mask

Frank told me the gorilla story at the barbecue, and I'm going to tell it the way he told it, as a story, because that's all it is, and because I've never found the paper.

"Forty-three," he said. He had tongs. Ana had given him tongs to stop him talking with his hands and it hadn't worked. "California. Desert. They're testing the first American jet, properly secret, nobody's supposed to know jets exist. And the test pilots, who are bored, because test pilots are always bored, they get fed up with the other pilots at the base radioing in going *what the hell was that, no propeller, going like the clappers*, right, and the brass going *you saw nothing.* So one of them puts on a gorilla mask. And a bowler hat. And a cigar. And he flies the jet, in formation, right up alongside some poor sod in a propeller plane, and *waves.*"

"That's not true," said Ana.

"Every aviation book I've ever read says it's true, which is almost the same thing." Frank turned a sausage. "Point is, the poor sod lands and goes *I saw a plane with no propeller flown by a gorilla in a hat*, and what happens?"

"They laugh at him."

"They laugh at him *forever.* He's gorilla guy. He was *right*, James. He saw a jet. He was the first man in his squadron to see the future, and he spent the rest of his life being the man who saw a gorilla. And nobody had to deny anything. They just stuck something stupid to the truth and let him carry it." He pointed the tongs at me. "That's your lot. That's the whole technique. You don't hide the plane. You put a gorilla in it."

"Handle with humour," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something I read."

Dave, who'd been quiet, who'd been very good at being quiet all afternoon, in the corner by the heater with a beer, said, "That's a good story, Frank," in the tone of a man who collected them, and Frank beamed, and the sausages burned, and I went inside to find more plates, and that's when I found out about the persona.

---

I hadn't looked at it in weeks. Twelve years, a few hundred followers, the light did-you-knows; I'd stopped posting in the summer, after the correction, and I'd stopped reading it because every time I opened it I saw the third page of a style guide. I opened it in the kitchen with plates in my other hand because somebody'd sent me a link with *is this you??* on it, and the link was to my own account, and the account was *fine.*

That's the thing. It was fine. Twelve years of me, intact, every steam engine, every calendar. And then, under the one about the door ten metres up the wall, three sentences I hadn't written. In my voice. *Almost.* The same rhythm, the same dryness, and then a turn at the end, a little flourish about *who built it and why they'd want us to forget*, a wrong date, a word I'd never use. Under the calendar one, a line about bloodlines. Under the steam engine, a paragraph, quite good, really, about suppressed technology, with a link.

And in the drafts, the thing I'd deleted in the spring. *Has anyone here had an NDE.* Not deleted. Posted. Dated the week I'd deleted it. With four hundred replies.

I stood in my kitchen with the plates and understood that somebody had been in my account for months and had never once removed anything. They'd *added.* Two per cent. Three sentences in a hundred. Enough that anyone who went looking for the man who'd posted about page 118 would find a man who also, it turned out, believed in bloodlines and suppressed engines and had been asking strangers about dying since April, and would file him, courteously, fully, under the usual thing.

I tried to correct it. Right there, plates on the counter. I deleted the three sentences under the door, and I posted, under them, a short dull note: *the previous additions to this account were not written by me.*

Eleven people saw it. The account has a little number. Eleven. The bloodlines line had been seen by six thousand.

That's the arithmetic. I'd spent my life believing that the truth, said plainly, in the right place, would do its own work. Here was the right place and here was the truth and here was the number eleven, and outside my own window a man who'd stuck a gorilla to me was drinking my beer and being lovely to my wife.

I didn't know it was him. I need to say that. I thought it was *them*, a them, the one in five hundred, some pleasant man at a desk with a template. I put the phone away. I took the plates out. Dave looked up and said, "You alright, Jim? You look like a man whose bank's being difficult," and I laughed, because it was our bit now, and said, "It's not a bank."

---

Ana had wanted the barbecue. That matters. She'd said, in September, *you've been nicer since Wednesday golf man, bring him round*, and then October had happened and I'd gone back into the submarine and she'd said it again, less nicely, *bring him round, James, I want to see the person who makes you normal.* So I had. And Frank, because Frank. And the neighbours, who are fine. And Henk, the ringleader of the four retirees, who turned out to be a retired surveyor with opinions about my grip. And a man from the forum, somehow, a lonely one, who'd worked out where I lived from a photograph and turned up with a bottle of wine and theories and whom Ana, to her enormous credit, fed.

And Dave, with a six-pack of something craft and a flask, because he was a flask man, and who was, I'm going to say it plainly, *wonderful.*

He was good with Henk. He was good with the neighbours. He let the forum man talk at him for twenty minutes about the Sphinx and said "huh" in all the right places and never once let his face do what mine was doing. And he was good with Ana. Better than good. He asked her about her grandmother. Not *sorry for your loss*; he asked what she'd been *like*, and Ana told him about the knitting, the undoing and redoing, and he listened the way almost nobody listens, with his whole body turned to her and his beer forgotten, and when she'd finished he said, "She sounds like somebody who knew that the doing was the point," and Ana's eyes went bright and she went inside for a minute.

She found me in the kitchen later. "I like him."

"I know."

"You never bring anyone home. Eleven years. You've never once brought a person home that you liked." She put her hand on my chest, flat, the way she does. "He's kind. He's actually kind. Keep him."

---

Here's the thing I filed.

I'd started a did-you-know. The steam engine, the Roman one, the oldest in the folder, the safe one, because the forum man was getting onto bloodlines and I needed to steer the table. "The Romans had a working steam engine," I said, "first century, a ball on a pipe, it spun, it worked, and they shelved it, as a toy, because — "

"— because slaves were cheaper," Dave said. "And the explanation was working harder than the mystery. Like a lab coat two sizes too big."

Everybody laughed. It was a good line. It was a *very* good line.

It was mine. Not the slaves; everyone knows the slaves. The lab coat. The coat two sizes too big. I've thought it a thousand times and I've written it in the Record maybe a dozen and I have never, as far as I know, said it out loud to a human being, because it's the kind of thing you say to a dog.

"That's a good line," I said.

"It's a very good line, Jim," said Dave, and raised his beer to me.

And I filed it. I want to be exact about where. Under *I must have posted it once.* Twelve years, a few hundred followers; I'd probably used it. Under *great minds.* Under *he reads the same things I do, that's why I like him.* The filing system doesn't ask questions. That's what it's for. And it was a good afternoon and my wife had her hand on my chest and I wasn't going to be the man who ruined it by checking.

Buddy didn't go near him.

All afternoon. Buddy goes near everyone, eventually, slowly, paying for every step; it's his whole policy. He went near Henk. He went near the forum man, which was a kindness. He lay by the heater for four hours with Dave three feet away and never once looked at him, and Dave, who'd asked about him first, who'd crouched on a green, never once tried.

Mora went over once. Sniffed his shoe. Came back. Didn't go again.

Old dog, I thought. Tired. Too many people.

I filed that too.

---

That night, after everyone had gone and the forum man had been put in a taxi with his theories and his empty bottle, Ana fell asleep on my chest at ten and I lay there with one arm dead looking at the ceiling, and I didn't think about the lab coat. I thought about the number eleven.

Because here's what the gorilla does, in the end, and it took me a barbecue to see it. It doesn't just make you laughable. It makes *correcting it* laughable. The man who says *I never said that about bloodlines* is a man talking about bloodlines. The man who says *that's not my draft* is a man with a draft. Every true thing I could say now had a gorilla standing next to it, waving, and the only way not to be the man with the gorilla was to stop saying true things where anyone could hear.

So I stopped. Not that night. But that's where it started: the decision not to be believed. To do what Noor did. To be a neighbour instead of a story.

I thought it was defeat. It was the most dangerous thing I ever decided.

Ana shifted. "You're thinking," she said, asleep.

"No."

"You're always thinking. It's like sleeping on a fridge." She settled. "He's nice, though. Golf man."

"Yeah," I said. "He's nice."

Three feet from the heater, for four hours, and the dog never looked at him once.
