# A corridor. He is sixteen.

The corridor has a machine at the end of it that sells chocolate and crisps and one kind of sandwich, and the boy has been looking at the machine for forty minutes, because it is the only thing in the corridor that isn't a door.

He has a coin. His mother gave it to him, from her purse, without looking, the way she has given him everything for the last three days, and said *get something*, and he has not got something, because getting something would mean walking to the end of the corridor and the end of the corridor is further from the room, and he has decided, without deciding, that the distance matters.

He doesn't know what the machines in the room are for. There are four of them and they each make a different sound and nobody has told him which sound is which, and he is sixteen, and he reads everything, and he has read the little cards on the front of two of the machines and they are in a language that is almost English and isn't, and he is ashamed, in the corridor, that he wants to know what they do more than he wants to be in the room.

A nurse comes out. She's nice. They've all been nice. She crouches, which is wrong, because he's taller than her standing, and she says, "He's comfortable," and the boy nods, because he has learned in three days that *comfortable* is the word they use when the other words have run out, and that you nod.

"Is there anything you want to ask?"

He has eleven things. He has written them in his head in order. What the sounds are. What the number on the second machine means and why it went from 94 to 88. Whether the thing in the bag is the same thing it was yesterday, because the bag is a different colour. Whether *comfortable* is a measurement. Whether anybody here knows, actually knows, not guesses, what happens in the room on the last night, because he has a feeling, from the way the nurses have started to look at his mother, that it is going to be tonight, and that nobody is going to say so, and that the not-saying is a kindness that he is expected to accept the way he is expected to accept the coin.

"No," he says. "Thank you."

She squeezes his arm and goes.

Later, in the room, his father's hand is on the sheet, and the boy looks at it, and he knows the hand better than he knows his own, the knuckles, the one nail that never grew right after the door, and he does not take it, because taking it would mean something and he does not know what, and he is afraid of doing the wrong thing in a room where everything is being done right.

A doctor speaks to his mother in the doorway. He is a kind doctor, and tired, and he has a clipboard, and he says a sentence that the boy will not remember and then another that he will. "There's some paperwork. Not now. Leave it for now."

*Leave it for now.* His mother nods. The boy watches her nod and watches the paperwork go away under a kind man's arm and understands, at sixteen, in a corridor, that there is a category of thing that is not his, that is handled, that goes away under an arm, and that the correct response is to nod.

It's tonight. Nobody says so. At four in the morning the sounds change and then there are no sounds and a nurse he hasn't seen before puts a hand on his mother's shoulder and says, very gently, "He's at peace now. He's in a better place."

The boy hears it. He hears it as a sentence. He turns it over, once, in the corridor, standing by the chocolate machine with a coin in his fist, and he thinks, clearly, in the voice he will have for the rest of his life: *based on what?* And then he thinks: *don't.* And he doesn't. He puts it away. He has nowhere to put it, so he puts it where there's nowhere, and he will find out, twenty years later, on a kitchen floor, that it's still there, exactly as he left it, with the coin.

His mother is crying. He holds her coat. He stands half a step behind and to the left. He is good at it. He has no idea what he's doing.

At six they let him go back in for a minute, alone, and the hand is on the sheet, and he does take it now, when it can't mean anything, and it's cool, and the knuckles are the knuckles, and he stands there holding his father's hand for the first time since he was small and thinks nothing at all, which is the only time in his life he will manage it, until the floor.

Afterwards, on the way out, he puts the coin in the machine. It takes the coin and gives him nothing. He looks at it. He doesn't press anything. He doesn't ask anyone. He leaves it.

*The paperwork was done in the spring. He never saw it. He went back to school on the Monday and was fine, and was told he was fine by a series of professionals over the next twenty years, and was graduated by every one of them, and never once mentioned the machine.*
