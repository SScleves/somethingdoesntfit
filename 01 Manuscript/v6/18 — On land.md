# On land

The pub by the course has a back terrace with heaters, and on a Saturday in November you can sit out till nine if you keep your coat on and lie to yourself about the temperature. Ana had come to meet us. Her idea. She'd said on the phone, *I'll come for the last hour, I want to see you two being idiots*, and here we were, and we'd been idiots for eighteen holes and she'd arrived in time for the beer.

She was on her second glass and telling him about the toaster.

"Three weekends," she said. "*Three.* Every wire in the house. I'm standing in my own kitchen holding a toaster like a woman in a hostage video."

"Did he find it?" Dave said.

"He found a *label.* Somebody'd labelled a fuse wrong in nineteen ninety-something." She squeezed my arm. "He was so happy. I've never seen him happier. Not on our wedding day."

"Fair," I said.

"That's a good day," Dave said, and meant it, and smiled at me over his glass. "Careful man. There's nothing wrong with careful. Old houses go up. You read about it, that professor's place near Deventer, was it, last spring."

I'm going to tell you what happened in my body, because I'd read about it a hundred times and it was nothing like the descriptions.

My heart didn't race. It did one thing: a single hard knock, like somebody testing a door. Then it went very slow, and my hands got cold, and everything on the terrace got extremely clear and extremely far away. The heaters. The coasters. My wife's hand on my sleeve.

I'd never said Deventer to him. I've never *written* Deventer anywhere a person could read it. There's no fire, no professor's place, no last spring; Hendrik Maasen died in a blue chair with the bin not out and his daughter sells curtains four streets from the station and the only three people alive who know that I know it are her, and a woman in Leiden, and me.

Ana was laughing at something else already.

I took a sip. A long one that I made look short.

"Deventer?" I said. "I thought that was up near Zwolle."

The heaters ticked. Somebody inside dropped a tray.

"Zwolle," said Dave. "You might be right." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the coaster. "I'm bad with those towns."

That was it. That was the whole thing. Nothing happened to his face. I've thought about it for a long time and the honest description is that nothing *left* it. The warmth was still there, the crinkle, the man who'd shanked one into the trees on the second and laughed so hard I'd liked him again before we got to the ball. All of it stayed exactly where it was. It just stopped being attached to anything.

He knew I'd caught it. Ninety-nine per cent. And he was looking at me the way I look at a number that's just done something a number shouldn't, and I understood he was at ninety-nine as well, and that neither of us was going to get the last one, and that this was the arrangement now.

"Who's the professor?" Ana said. "Is this a book thing?"

"It's a book thing," I said.

"Shame, is all I meant," Dave said to her, easily, and put his hand flat on the table between us. "Some people spend so long looking at old wreckage they forget how quickly a new house goes up. Especially the nice ones. Good street. Dogs."

Ana made a face. "God, Dave. That's a bit morbid for a Saturday."

"Home insurance," Dave said. "Boring subject. I'll stop." He looked at his watch, and then at me, and there was nothing in it, nothing, no regret, no *shame it had to be you*, and he stood and put a note under his glass.

"Good game today, Jim." He put his coat on. "Let's not play another."

He kissed Ana on the cheek. He'd done it at the barbecue too. She let him.

---

When he'd gone she said, "Well, that got weird," and finished her wine, and I said "yeah," and sat on a terrace under a heater next to the person I love most in the world and felt, and this is the part I didn't expect, *sad.*

Not frightened. That came in the car. Sad, the way you're sad when you find out a thing you enjoyed wasn't real, because I had enjoyed it. I hadn't had a friend in eleven years and I'd had one for nine weeks and he'd been very, very good at it. He'd asked about the dog first. He'd chipped in from the fringe and said nobody would ever believe him. He'd listened to my wife talk about her grandmother with his whole body, and I'd watched him do it, and I'd thought, *keep him.*

And there was no him. There was a man whose job was to be near enough to know what I loved, and he'd done the job, and now he'd told me so, in front of her, with a joke about insurance, and gone.

I don't know what he felt. I've had years to decide and I think the honest answer is: nothing. Not cruelty. A man who's finished a task. He'd paid for the drinks.

---

In the car Ana said, "What *was* that?"

I drove.

"James. What was that. The professor thing. The house thing. He went weird, you went weird, you did the face, you did the thing with the town, I know the thing with the town, you do it to me when you think I've got a date wrong. What was that?"

And I had the whole shape. I had all of it; I'd had it since Leiden. I could have pulled over on the ring road and told her, and I'd have had to tell her all of it, the thin book and the ledger and the three pages and the forty kilobytes and the number eleven and the dog that wouldn't go near him, and she'd have believed me or she wouldn't, and either way she'd have been *in it.* Knowing is being in. I'd read that on a style guide and I'd done the math on it and one in five hundred of the people who'd said *she's at peace now* had been in, and the one person on earth I was certain wasn't, I was not going to put there on a ring road because a man had said the name of a town.

"He's a golf friend," I said. "I don't know. He had a bad day."

"Don't."

"He's in insurance. Insurance people say things like that. It's how they flirt."

"Don't *do* the voice at me."

I did the next thing. God help me. I reached behind me, into the back seat, where the tinfoil hat had lived since last Christmas because she'd made me wear it to the course once for a photo, and I held it up, and I said, "Honestly, Scully, I think he was just trying to get me to wear this," and I waited for the laugh.

It didn't come.

We both heard it not come. That's the only way I can put it. For eleven years the hat had been the thing that worked, the joke that let her be worried and let me be weird and let both of us stay in the same car, and I held it up on the ring road in the dark and it was just a hat. A piece of foil shaped like a hat by my wife's hands, two Decembers ago, with love.

She took it out of my hand, gently, and put it on her lap, and looked out of the window.

"One evening," she said, after a while, "you're going to tell me. And it's going to be too late to be the evening I wanted."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything.

That was the evening the jokes stopped. I'd promised you I'd get to it, and I've got to it, and I'd like to tell you it was dramatic, but it was a wife in a car with a tinfoil hat on her knees, not crying, looking at the lights.

We got home. The dogs did the thing. Mora, two states. Buddy, two thumps and the slow walk across. Ana went down on her knees in the hall in her coat and put her face in the black neck, and I stood in the doorway with the keys in my hand and watched, and I understood that there'd been a day, in April, when I'd watched exactly this and thought *the dogs are doing more for her than I did all day*, and that I'd had seven months to learn something from it, and hadn't.

The hat went back on its hook by the door. She hung it there herself. It's where it stayed.
