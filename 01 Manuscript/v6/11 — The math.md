# The math

Els Maasen sold curtains four streets from the station, and she had her father's hands, and I knew that because I'd never met her father and still knew it the second she put them on the counter.

I'd gone in on a Saturday with a story. The story was that I'd bought some of her father's books at the market and found a letter in one and wanted to return it. The letter was real, a dull one, a note from a librarian about a renewal, and I'd put it in an envelope and carried it across town and I'm not proud of any of that. The persona handles the small stuff. This wasn't small, and it handled it anyway, which is the first time I noticed that the persona didn't have a line it wouldn't cross, only lines it hadn't been asked to.

She looked at the letter. She looked at me.

"You're the one who took twelve."

"The man at the market told you."

"He tells everyone everything." She almost smiled. "He said you were the only one who went through them spine by spine. Like Dad." She put the letter down. "You didn't come about a letter."

"No."

"Then what?"

I've thought a lot about why I told her the truth. It wasn't a decision. I think it was her hands. "He kept a notebook," I said. "It was in the crate. I don't think he meant it to be. I've been reading it and I don't understand it and I thought you might know what he was doing."

She was quiet for long enough that a woman came in, looked at some fabric, and went out again.

"He was doing that for thirty years," Els said. "I thought it was a bibliography. He said it was a bibliography." She came round the counter. "Wait here."

She was gone ten minutes. When she came back she had a folder, cardboard, the kind with an elastic band, and she held it the way you hold something you've decided to be rid of.

"The furniture went to a man with a van. I kept the dresser. I don't know why. Mum's." She put the folder on the counter. "This was in the bottom drawer, under the lining paper. Dad must have put it there. I found it in May. I've looked at it maybe four times. I don't know what it is and I don't want it in the house and I don't want to throw it away, because he hid it, and he never hid anything." She pushed it an inch towards me. "You seem like someone who checks things."

"I am."

"Then check it. And don't tell me."

Three pages. Photocopies of photocopies; you could see the grey ghost of a staple in the corner, copied along with the text, three generations deep. The top of the first page said, in a plain typeface that had never been chosen by anyone who cared about typefaces:

*PREFERRED FORMULATIONS. Fourth revision. For internal circulation to readers at level 2 and above.*

Below that, two columns. *Deprecated* on the left. *Preferred* on the right.

I read it standing at my own kitchen table with my coat still on and the dogs waiting to be let out.

I'll give you a few. I won't give you all of it; I don't think the whole thing should be in a book, and I've thought about that more than I've thought about most things. But you need a few, or you won't believe me.

*Deprecated:* was given to. *Preferred:* arose among.
*Deprecated:* taught by. *Preferred:* developed by.
*Deprecated:* the earlier peoples. *Preferred:* the first peoples.
*Deprecated:* brought. *Preferred:* discovered.
*Deprecated:* a visitor. *Preferred:* a tradition.
*Deprecated:* remembered. *Preferred:* imagined.

That was the first page. It went on. Thirty-odd pairs, and every pair did the same thing, the thing I'd found on a kitchen floor with a wet rope on my chest: it took the hand out. Nothing was ever added. Nothing was ever *denied*. If you read the right-hand column on its own, it was perfectly respectable; it was how every careful scholar I'd ever read already wrote. That was the horror of it. It wasn't a lie. It was a *style.* It was the house style of the people who decided what careful sounded like.

The second page was about dates. *Where a date is contested, prefer the later.* *Where a layer is contested, prefer the shallower.* *Where a sequence is contested, prefer the one in which the simpler precedes the more complex.* Each with a line or two of justification that sounded exactly like every methods section I'd ever skimmed, because, I understood, standing there with my coat on, it *was* every methods section I'd ever skimmed. Somebody had written the rules for looking, and then the rules had been taught, and then the people who'd learned them had taught them, and three generations later the rules were just what looking *was.*

The third page had a heading I've never been able to forget.

*WHEN A READER NOTICES.*

Four paragraphs. The first said that a reader who notices a change between printings is to be treated as a bibliographical enquiry and answered as such: courteously, fully, with reference to the ordinary practice of the trade. The second said that a reader who *persists* is to be offered the opportunity to publish, and that publication should be facilitated, in an appropriate venue, with an appropriate framing. The third said that a reader who persists after publication is to be *reviewed.*

The fourth paragraph was two lines long.

*In no case is a reader to be contradicted. Contradiction confirms. The preferred response to a correct reader is agreement, followed by reclassification.*

I read that one four times. Then I let the dogs out, because they'd been very patient, and I stood in the garden in my coat and watched Mora try to eat a leaf and thought: *Dear Mr Klippe. I would gently say that it is wisest to be plain about one's reasons.*

Courteously. Fully. With reference to the ordinary practice of the trade.

---

I'd like to say the math came to me in a flash. It came the way everything comes to me, which is slowly, at a kitchen table, while I'm trying to do something else.

Page one said *level 2 and above.* That means there's a level one. That means there are levels. And a document doesn't get a fourth revision unless it's been *used*, by enough people over enough years that the first three wore out, and I know what a fourth revision looks like, I've written them, they're what happens when the thing is boring and necessary and has been for a decade.

So: how many people?

I'm not a statistician. I'm a man who counts breaths. But here's how I did it, and you can do it too, and I'd rather you did.

Say the thing is real. Say there's a group of people who maintain what careful sounds like, and a job that runs at two in the morning, and a man in a van who doesn't haggle, and a manager who says *leave it*, and a dealer who says *be plain.* Say none of them know each other. How many would you need?

Not a cabal. A cabal couldn't do it; a cabal can't keep a secret in a town, let alone a century. You'd need something duller. You'd need, in a company of five thousand people, somebody who could close a ticket and somebody who could touch the backups and somebody who could tell a manager what to say about a job. That's three. Call it ten, to be safe, for every five thousand. One in five hundred.

One in five hundred. I sat with that.

That's the man who delivers our post. Not *is*. Could be. One in five hundred means that in a town this size there are a hundred and sixty of them, and they don't know each other, and most of them have been asked to do one small thing once and never asked again, and they did it, because it was small, and because the man who asked was pleasant, and because they'd been told a reason that was true enough.

One in five hundred means that at the funeral, in that slow line of people saying *she's in a better place*, statistically, somebody was in.

I stopped there. I got up and I put the kettle on and I didn't make anything with it.

Because here's what one in five hundred does to you, if you let it, and I let it. You stop calling people. Not dramatically. You just notice, on a Tuesday, that you haven't rung your mother in three weeks, and that the reason you haven't is that you'd have to tell her about your week, and you can't, and so you'd have to tell her about Ana's week, and you'd hear yourself doing it, performing the normal, and you'd rather not. You stop going for the beer after work. You stop answering Beard Man's questions about what you're reading. You develop, over about a month, the social life of a man in a submarine, and the submarine is your own kitchen, and the people on the surface are all lovely and any one of them could be the man in the van.

I knew that was the wrong lesson. I knew it while I was learning it. It didn't help.

---

The thing with Ana happened over breakfast and it was my fault and I'm going to tell it straight.

"You were up at four," she said. Not a question.

"The payments thing."

She buttered toast. She's a careful butterer, corner to corner, and she did the whole slice before she said anything else.

"The payments thing finished in June. You told me. You said *good catch* in a voice and did the thing with your hands."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything.

"I don't mind that you're up at four," Ana said. "I mind that you've started having a thing you tell me and a thing you don't. I can see the join. I've always been able to see the join, that's the entire reason you married me, you said so, on the third date, you said *you're the only person who's ever caught me.*" She put the knife down. "So catch you. What's the four o'clock thing?"

And I had the shape by then. I had it. I could have put the three pages on the table and the thin book next to them and said: *I think somebody's maintaining the world, and I think they've noticed me.* And she'd have said *okay, Mulder* and asked a hundred questions and we'd have had the day, and whatever came after would've come after with two of us in it.

"Reading," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her toast and ate it, corner to corner, and went to work, and didn't kiss the top of my head, and that was the whole thing. That was the cost. She was quiet for a day. One day. She was perfectly nice the entire time, and I'd have paid anything by the evening to be shouted at instead.

One in five hundred. And the only person I was certain wasn't in, I'd just put on the outside.

---

The email came that Friday and I laughed out loud at my desk, which I don't do.

A recruiter. I get them; everyone gets them; they're weather. This one was good, though, that was the thing. Somebody had done their homework. It was from a consultancy I'd never heard of, on behalf of a finance house I had, a big one, old money, the kind of place that has a building with a name. They wanted somebody to look at their systems *from the outside*, it said, somebody with "a reputation for noticing what others have been told to leave." And then, in the last line, the thing that made me laugh: *We're told you play off the seventh. We have a member there who'd be delighted to show you the back nine.*

Play off the seventh. That's not a golf term. It's a *joke* about me, the trees on the seventh, and I'd made it exactly once, out loud, on the course, to the four retirees, who'd laughed, and it had turned up on the club's website in a members' photo caption six months ago, under a picture of me and an old dog in a cart, because the secretary thinks she's funny.

So they'd looked at the club's website. Fine. Recruiters do. And they'd found the one line that would make me laugh, and they'd put it at the end where the laugh would land, and I laughed, and then I stopped, because I'd just spent a month learning that the preferred response to a correct reader is *agreement.*

I looked up the consultancy. Registered two years ago. One director. A name I didn't know and wrote down anyway, because I write everything down.

*Kalder.*

And under the photo of me and Buddy, in the members' gallery, the caption, the joke, and below it the course's own line, which I'd never read before, because who reads those: *Pictured: J. Klippe and companion, every Wednesday at seven.*

Every Wednesday at seven. Anyone who wanted to know what I loved had only to look.
