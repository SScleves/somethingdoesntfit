# Twenty minutes

Ana's mother lives in a house where every surface has a cloth on it, and I put the objects on the cloth, one at a time, in the order I'd decided on in the car, and Ana sat opposite with her arms folded and her mother upstairs with the television up loud on purpose, and Mora under the table with her chin on my foot because it had been nineteen days and in Mora's world that's a resurrection.

"Twenty minutes," I said. "You can stop me. You can throw me out. I'm going to put things on the table and tell you what they are, and I'm not going to tell you what I *think* until the end, and then you can decide."

"Go."

The two books. "This is a sentence that was changed in 1931, two years after the man who wrote it walked into some hills. They changed one line and left the typos so nobody'd check. I found it on our kitchen floor. That's the night you came down for water and I said I hadn't found anything weird."

"I remember."

The thin book. "This is a list of forty more. Kept by a dead man and his grandfather. I was handed it in a crate. I don't know whether that was an accident."

The three pages. "This is the style guide the edits were made from. Fourth revision. His daughter found it in a dresser. It's got a section on what to do when someone like me notices, and the answer is: agree with him, and reclassify."

The screenshot, printed. "This is a job that ran every night at my old place and sent forty kilobytes to nowhere. I asked what it was. Within a week it was gone and so was my asking. It's at the new place too. It's been there for thirty-one years. It's called a recall index. The sum they pay for it goes up in steps, and the last step was the week I asked."

The schedule. She looked at it and didn't touch it. "You've seen that."

"Our address."

"Our address. The word in the box is the golf club's word for Buddy. The visit was the night he got sick. Nobody came to the door. I don't know what happened and I'm never going to."

The slip from Noor. "This is a number a woman in Leiden gave me because I asked instead of argued. It's an item in a depot. She's gone quiet since. Not dead. Quiet. There's a difference and I've learned it."

The ruleset, the one page, 212. "This is the tool my old mate Frank sent us a link about with a skull. It's been told, out of the box, never to see the job. Somebody wrote one boring sentence into the defaults and it's installed everywhere. It's got a review date. The date's in two weeks."

And then the last thing, which wasn't an object, so I put my hand flat on the cloth where it would have gone.

"And this is me. On the floor. Four nights. I can't put it on the table. I can tell you that I was afraid of dying from the age of sixteen, in a corridor, and that I'm not any more, and that it happened on a kitchen floor with the dog watching and the oven clock on, and I'm not going to tell you it was anything but that, because it wasn't. I'm not glowing. I'm not going to make you do anything. I just don't have the thing any more, and I think that's the only thing they've ever been counting, and I think they've counted me."

I took my hand off the cloth. "That's it. Nineteen minutes. What I think is on the table. You can throw it out."

---

Ana didn't say anything for a long time. Her mother's television laughed upstairs at something.

Then she reached across and picked up the thin book, and opened it at the back, and looked at the word, and closed it, and put it down exactly where it had been, square to the cloth, the way her grandmother would have.

"I don't believe it," she said.

"Okay."

"I want to say that first, so you've heard it. I don't believe there's a *them*. I don't believe a bank in a wig has been paying for a machine that counts souls. I don't believe you've got a soul, or I do, but not like that, not like a thing that gets *counted*, that's insane, James, that's the most insane thing anyone's ever put on my mother's tablecloth." She wasn't crying. She was doing the other thing, the clear thing. "And I've been sitting here for nineteen minutes watching you put it there, and you did it the way you do the toaster. In order. Boring. With the bits you couldn't prove *labelled.* You said *I don't know* six times. I counted. You've never said I don't know six times in a *year.*"

"I've stopped pretending."

"I can see that. That's what I can't get past." She unfolded her arms. "You look different. You've looked different since the phone call. You stand like somebody who's put something heavy down, and I've been married to you for eleven years and I've never once seen you without it, and I didn't know it was there until it wasn't."

"So what do you believe?"

"*You.*" She said it like a slap and a kiss at once, the way she says most things that matter. "I believe you. I don't believe it and I believe you and I don't know what to do with that, so I'm going to sit here until I do."

---

What she did, in the end, was ask for the plan. Not the memoir. That's her. Twenty minutes of what, and then: *so what are you going to do about it, Mulder.*

"I don't have a plan. I've got a problem. Here's the problem." I moved the schedule next to the ruleset. "Everything I've done to find out what it is has made it *worse.* Not for me. For the date. I asked about the job and the sum went up. I found the notebook and the sum went up. I sat on the floor and I'm fairly sure something happened to the date that night, because that's what it's *for.* It's a thermostat. The more people stop being afraid, the sooner it does whatever a review is, and I don't know what a review is, and I've got two weeks."

"Then stop."

"I did stop. That's the thing. I stopped in October. I stopped publishing, I stopped telling Frank, I stopped telling you, I went quiet like the woman in Leiden, and I think that's exactly what they *want*, and I think it's also exactly what they're afraid of, and I can't make both of those be true and I've been trying for a month."

Ana looked at the table. "What did the pub man want?"

"To know what I loved. He said so, I think. Not to me."

"And what do they want?"

I thought about Bram, whom I hadn't met. About a man on a phone in 1993 saying *it's a budgeting thing, Wim.* About a column headed *Visit.* "To house me. I think. There's a word for it; I've seen it in the ledger. People who've worked it out and are offered a seat. Somewhere. With their families. On a list."

"A seat where?"

"I don't know. Wherever you go when the review happens."

Ana was quiet. Then she said, "They'd offer you *me*."

"Yes."

"And the dogs."

"I think so. I think that's what *N seats* means."

"And you'd say — "

"I'd say no." I heard how it sounded and I said it again, slower, so she could check it. "I'd say no. Not because I'm brave. Because I don't need it. That's the thing that changed on the floor, Ana, that's the *only* thing. A seat is a rescue. I don't need rescuing. And I don't think they've got a thing to do with a man who doesn't."

"Except visit the dog."

"Except that."

She stood up. She went to the kettle, which is what her family does instead of deciding, and she filled it, and she didn't turn it on, and she stood with her back to me, and Mora's chin stayed on my foot.

"You're staying," she said. "Here. Tonight. With him. Mum's got the back room and he can't do the stairs so he'll have the kitchen, which he'll prefer."

"Ana, if they've got our address they've got — "

"Then they've got this one too, and you've just told me the list has got two weeks on it, and I'm not spending two weeks in my mother's spare room on my own waiting for whoever they send to turn up at *our* house and offer you a bunk while I'm here. If they want to offer you a bunk they can offer it with me in the room." She turned round. "That's the plan. That's the whole plan. I don't believe it and I believe you and the four of us are going to sit in my mother's kitchen, and if somebody comes to the door, I'll open it. Because I'm not in. Whatever that means. I'm not in, and I'm the only one of us who can say that, and I'd like to see what happens to whoever they send when the person he's threatening answers the door and offers him tea."

I looked at my wife, standing by a cold kettle in her mother's kitchen with her arms folded, and understood that she'd just done the thing I do, the thing I'm supposedly good at, which is to look at a system from outside and work out what it can't handle, and that she'd done it in four minutes, and that it was *her.*

"Okay," I said.

"Good. Bring the hat in. It's in the car, I saw you not bring it in."

I brought the hat in. I hung it on her mother's hook, by the door, because there wasn't anywhere else, and it looked ridiculous there, and Ana looked at it, and for the first time since a terrace in November, she almost laughed.

Almost. We're not there yet. But I saw it go across her face, and I'd have signed for forty more years of almost.
