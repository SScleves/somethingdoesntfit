# The week it vanished

Beard Man knew how the professor died, because Beard Man knows everything in the way a river knows everything: it's all gone through him and none of it's stayed in order.

"Chair," he said. He was pricing a box of Reader's Digest condensed novels that nobody on earth would buy, and he priced them anyway, lovingly, at fifty cents each. "Blue one. Daughter found him. Monday. Bin hadn't been put out, that's how she knew, he always put the bin out. Nice man. Didn't haggle."

"You met him?"

"Sold him things. Years. Particular." He held a condensed Hemingway up to the light as if checking it for the parts they'd left out. "Wanted first printings. Not first editions, mind. Printings. Most people don't know the difference. I told him the difference once and he said he knew, thank you, and I liked him for it."

"And the daughter sold you the books."

"Curtains," said Beard Man. "She does curtains. Four streets from the station. Didn't want the books, wanted the shelves. Said he'd stopped sleeping at the end. Said he'd be up all night with them." He put the Hemingway down. "Said the last few months he kept asking her the same thing."

I waited. He let me wait. It's the only power he has and he enjoys it.

"What thing?"

"Whether she'd be alright." Fifty cents. Fifty cents. "Which is a normal thing for a dying man to ask, and I told her so, and she said no, not like that. She said he asked it like a man checking a *list.*"

Mid-sentence, as always. The comet. The prophet. I stood in the cold with my hands in my pockets and let it run over me and didn't hear a word, because I was thinking about a dying man and a list and the word *Ask*, in a hand that shook.

"Who bought his furniture?" I said, when there was a gap.

"Man in a van." Beard Man shrugged. "Fifties. Good coat. Polite. Didn't haggle either. Everyone's very polite about that house."

I need to tell you about the week, and I need to do it in order, because the order is the evidence.

Monday, I checked the ticket. I'd put it in the queue where tickets go to die and I expected it to be where I'd left it, dying. It had been closed. Not by a person. By the system, the way the system closes things when somebody tells it to, with the reason filled in: *Resolved — duplicate of #41822.*

I looked up 41822. It didn't exist. Not deleted; deleted tickets leave a stone. It had never been raised. My ticket had been closed as a duplicate of a ticket that had never been born, and the person who'd done that had either not cared whether I'd check, or had known that in a queue that size nobody ever checks, and I couldn't decide which was worse.

Tuesday, I checked the list. I'd got into the habit, the way you get into the habit of touching your pocket for your keys. Fourteen minutes past two. Nine seconds. Forty kilobytes.

It wasn't there.

Not a failure, nothing in red. The line was gone. `rcl-agg` had been in the list on Monday, and it wasn't on Tuesday, and there was nothing between the two states, no entry that said *removed by*, nothing in the history, which was correct, because there had never been a history. Something that had never been born had stopped existing. I checked the other offices. Gone in all of them. I checked the backups.

That's the part. I want to go slowly here.

The backups are snapshots. That's what they're for. They're paper. You can't reach into last Thursday's backup and change what it says; if you could, there'd be no point having it. The whole reason I've been able to sleep for eleven years is that somewhere there's a copy of last Thursday that nobody can touch.

Last Thursday's backup didn't have `rcl-agg` in it.

Neither did the one before. Neither did any of them, going back as far as they went. I sat at my desk at ten in the morning with my coffee going cold and paged backwards through two years of snapshots, each one a sealed room, and in every room the thing I'd seen with my own eyes four weeks ago had never been there.

I know what that is. I'd been looking at it for a month on my kitchen table. Somebody had reopened the plates, changed one line, and left everything else untouched so it'd look, to anyone who checked the way people check, like it had always been that way.

Only this time I'd *watched* them do it. I had my own notes. I had a screenshot, from the first night, because I screenshot things, because the Record. I looked at the screenshot and then at the list and they disagreed, and for the first time in my life I was in the one position I'd never been allowed: the paper said one thing and I said the other, and *I was right.*

It felt like nothing. That's the honest report. It didn't feel like vindication. It felt like a floor going.

Wednesday, I asked Frank.

I did it casually, by the coffee machine, because I have a persona for a reason. "That job I brought up in stand-up. The orphan one. Did Pieter ever say what it was?"

Frank looked at me with the perfect, open, blank, friendly face of a young man who has no idea what you're talking about and likes you anyway.

"Which stand-up?"

"Couple of weeks back. No owner. Sent stuff to nowhere. You did the skull."

"I do the skull a lot, mate." He laughed. "Sorry. Genuinely. I don't remember. Was it the Antwerp thing?"

"No. Never mind. It was nothing."

"Based on what?" he said, grinning, because it had become a bit, and I laughed, and it was a good bit, and I went back to my desk and sat down and felt my hands go cold.

I believe Frank. I want that understood before anything else that happens. Frank wasn't lying. Frank just didn't remember a fifteen-second item at the end of a fifteen-minute meeting from two weeks before, which is the most normal thing in the world, and which meant that the only witness to the fact that I had raised `rcl-agg` in a room with six people in it was me.

Thursday, I went to Pieter.

Not in stand-up. His office, door open, the way you ask about holiday. "Quick one. That orphan job. It's gone from the list. Did someone clean it up?"

He looked at me for exactly as long as it takes a decent man to decide to be kind.

"James. Which job?"

"The diagnostic one. From the migration. You said leave it."

"I say leave it about a lot of things." He smiled. He wasn't being cruel. Nothing in his face was ready this time; nothing had been rehearsed. That's what undid me. He'd had the sentence ready the first time and he didn't have anything ready now, because now there was nothing to have a sentence about. "If it's gone, it's gone. That's a good thing, isn't it? One fewer thing on the list."

"Yeah," I said. "One fewer thing."

Friday, I didn't go in.

I told Ana I had a cold. I did have a cold, in the way that you can have a cold if you need one. I sat in the kitchen all day with the dogs and the thin book and my screenshot and I did the thing I'm paid to do, which is to work out what a system is from the outside by what it does, without ever being allowed to see in.

Here's what I had. Something ran every night at the same minute and sent a small report to nowhere. I'd asked what it was. Within a week the thing had been removed, the asking had been removed, the record of the asking had been removed, the paper trail had been removed back to the beginning of time, and the two people I'd asked had been left with nothing in their heads but the natural blankness of men who have better things to remember.

Nobody had threatened me. Nobody had so much as frowned. It was the gentlest thing that had ever happened to me and I sat in my kitchen with a cold I didn't have and shook.

Because here's the thing. I'd asked about a book, and nobody cared. I'd written to a dealer, and got a kind postscript. I'd measured and ruled and read nine books page against page and the world had let me, the way it lets a man do a jigsaw. It was only when I'd asked about the *job* that something had moved. Not the books. Not the dead man. The forty kilobytes.

Why lie here?

Two options, I told the dog, because by then it was a liturgy.

One. It really was a diagnostic artefact, and some conscientious soul in another office had seen my ticket, cleaned up the job, closed the ticket with a typo in the duplicate number, and purged the backups to save space, and Pieter had simply forgotten, and Frank had simply forgotten, and I was a man with a cold reading a conspiracy into housekeeping. It happens. It's always the most likely thing.

Two. Somebody reads the queue where tickets go to die. Somebody has the keys to the backups, which is a list of about four people, and I'm one of them, and I didn't do it. Somebody wanted it to be as though I'd never asked. And whoever it was had done it the same way somebody in Leiden in 1931 had done it: one line, nothing else touched, and the honest certainty that nobody would ever check the way I check.

I gave option one sixty per cent. It was the lowest I'd ever gone. Buddy watched me do it from the blanket and offered nothing, which is a form of support.

Mora brought me the toy at some point, wet, and put it in my hand, and I threw it down the hall without thinking, and she brought it back, and I thought: nothing gets transmitted without someone to catch it. Something had been catching those forty kilobytes every night for years. And the night I'd noticed, it had stopped. Not because of the books. Because of me.

That's when I understood that the thing I'd been hunting could hunt.

I'd like to say that's when I stopped. I'd like to say a lot of things. What I actually did, at eleven that night, with my wife asleep upstairs and a cold I didn't have, was open the thin book to the last page, and look at two numbers and a date and a word, and type the coordinates into a map.

A hill. East of Urfa. Nothing on it. A road that stopped a kilometre short.

And I sat there and looked at a hill in Turkey on a laptop in a Dutch kitchen and felt, for the first time since the funeral, not curious, not afraid, but something I didn't have a word for yet and would, eventually, have to say out loud to a dog.

*Ask,* the professor had written.

Fine. But ask *who*?
