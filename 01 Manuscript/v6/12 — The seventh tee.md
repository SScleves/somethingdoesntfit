# The seventh tee

Wednesday, seven, dew on everything, the four retirees up ahead on the third where they've been since 1998, and a man on his own on the sixth green who waved me through.

That's unusual. People don't wave you through at seven in the morning; there's nobody to wave. He was standing by the flag with a putter in one hand and his other hand up, patient, the way you'd hold a door, and when I came up with the cart and Buddy's ears in the breeze he called, "Go on, I'm hopeless, I'll be here all day," and laughed at himself, and I liked him in the time it took to say thanks.

Fifties. A good coat, the kind that's been good for years. Mid-range clubs, not new, kept clean. He'd been three-putting, by the look of the green, and he stood aside while I missed from four feet, and said, "Oh, that's a relief," with such obvious pleasure that I laughed out loud.

"How old?" he said. Not to me. To the cart.

"Twelve."

"Twelve." He crouched. He didn't put his hand out, which is the thing people do, the thing that dogs hate; he just crouched where Buddy could see him and let Buddy decide. Buddy decided, after a while, that he could be looked at. "Hello, foreman," the man said, and I don't know how he knew that word, except that it's the only word, if you've ever seen Buddy in a cart. "What's he supervising?"

"Me. It's a full-time job."

"I bet." He stood up, with the small noise men our age make. "Dave."

"James."

"Jim," he said, as if trying it, and shook my hand, and nobody has ever called me Jim in my life, and I let it go, because I liked him, and because it was seven in the morning and the course was wet and gold and I'd spent a month in a submarine.

---

We played the seventh together, which is to say that I put my drive into the trees as per the club's website and he put his somewhere I'd describe as *theoretical*, and we found both, eventually, and he was funny about it the whole way. He had the trick that good company has, which is that he laughed at himself before you had to, so that you never had to. He asked about the course. He asked about the retirees. He asked where the dog slept, and whether the young one was a Doberman, and said he'd had a Doberman once and she'd eaten a sofa, and that it had been the best sofa he'd ever had and he hadn't missed it for a second.

Then, on the seventh green, from the fringe, from a lie I wouldn't have given a man money to try, he chipped it in.

It went up, and it landed, and it rolled, and it went in, with the little sound, and he stood there with the club still up and his mouth open like a man who'd been handed a baby, and then he looked at me and said, "I'm never going to be able to explain that to anyone," and I laughed so hard that Buddy barked.

That's the moment. If you want to know when I liked him, it was then. Not the chip. The sentence after. *I'm never going to be able to explain that to anyone.* He'd done the one impossible thing of his year and his first thought was that nobody would believe him.

I knew that feeling. I'd been living in it since April.

---

We had coffee in the clubhouse. It's a shed with a machine, but they call it the clubhouse, and at half past eight on a Wednesday it's empty, and we sat by the window with Buddy under the table and the retirees still out there, visible, on the fifth now, having made two holes of progress in an hour and a half.

"What do you do, Jim?"

"IT."

"Ah." He nodded, the way everyone nods. Then, instead of the next thing everyone says, he said: "What kind? I ask because my nephew says IT and means he fixes printers, and my brother-in-law says IT and means he's a millionaire. I never know which."

"Neither. I watch things. Software that watches other software. When something's dying in the night I'm the one they call."

"The night shift of the night shift."

"That's actually good. Can I have that?"

"It's yours." He drank. "I'm in continuity, for my sins. Insurance, basically, for things that can't be insured. If your building burns down, who does your payroll on Monday. If your records go, what's the copy. Very dull. Nobody at a party has ever asked a second question."

"What's the copy," I said.

"What?"

"You said: if your records go, what's the copy. That's the whole job, isn't it. That's mine too. Everybody thinks the job's the thing. The job's the copy."

He looked at me over his cup for a second longer than a man looks at you over a cup, and then he smiled, properly, like a man who's found something at a market.

"Did you know," he said, "that the Pentagon's own office for this stuff, the lights-in-the-sky stuff, the one on the news, has the word *phenomena* in its name? Because the committee that set it up sat for three weeks and could not agree on a noun. Objects, craft, vehicles, they all meant something. So: *phenomena.* The one word that means nothing. I think that's the most honest thing a government's ever done."

I laughed. It was a good one, it was a *real* one, it was exactly the kind I collect, and I hadn't had one handed to me across a table in years, because I'm the one who hands them. "That's good. That's going in the folder."

"You've got a folder?"

"Everyone's got a folder."

"I'll want to see this folder."

The retirees had reached the sixth. Buddy had gone to sleep on my foot. The coffee was bad and the window was fogged and the whole morning had the quality of a thing I'd have signed for forty more years of, and I noticed myself thinking that, and I noticed that I hadn't thought it since the Saturday after the funeral.

"They've got you on anything interesting?" Dave said. "At work. Or is it all printers."

"Mostly things that run at two in the morning and nobody owns."

"The worst hour," he said, with feeling. "Nothing good ever ran at two in the morning." And then, easily, standing, collecting his cup: "Same time next week? I'll bring better coffee. I'll bring a flask. I'm a flask man. You'll find that out about me."

"I'm here every Wednesday."

"I know," he said. "It's on the website. You and the foreman. It's the only reason I joined." And he laughed, and I laughed, because it was a joke, and he put his coat on, and patted Buddy, and went.

---

I'd had the interview the Monday before. I haven't mentioned it because there's not much to say about it. A glass room in a building with a name on it, a woman who asked good questions, a man who asked none, and a sense, the whole hour, that the decision had been made by people who weren't in the room and that the room was a courtesy. They wanted me to look at their systems from the outside. They used the phrase *a reputation for noticing what others have been told to leave*, again, in the room, which meant it was in a document somewhere, which meant somebody had written it down about me.

I'd gone because of the laugh. I'd like to say I'd gone because money leaves a trail, and a finance house is where the trail would be, and a man who wanted to know what `rcl-agg` had been sending to nowhere might do worse than stand where the money was. I thought all that *later*, in the car, as a reason. I went because somebody had made me laugh and I hadn't been for a month.

They offered on the Thursday. More money than I'd have asked for, which was the second thing that should have bothered me. I told Ana at dinner. She said, "A bank? You hate banks," and I said it wasn't a bank, it was older than a bank, and she said that was worse, and then she said, "You've been nicer this week," and I said had I, and she said, "Yeah. Since Wednesday. Did something happen on Wednesday?"

"I played golf with a man who chipped in from the fringe."

"That's it?"

"He said he'd never be able to explain it to anyone."

She looked at me for a bit. "Okay," she said. "I'll take it. I'll take Wednesday golf man. Bring him round."

I drove home from the course the next Wednesday with the window down and the flask coffee in me and Buddy asleep in the back, and I noticed that I was in a good mood, which I hadn't been for a year, and I noticed myself noticing.

And I didn't ask why. That's the honest report. For once in my life there was a thing that had changed and I didn't go looking for the cause. I just drove.

He'd asked about the dog first. I keep coming back to that. Everybody asks about the dog eventually. He'd asked *first.*
