# Chapter Thirty-Three

Some-when later.

---

It is a long afternoon at the end of summer and there are about forty people on the grass, which is not a park and not a garden and has no name that anybody uses.

Nobody is in a hurry. That is the first thing you would notice, coming from where we are. Not that they are idle — two of them have been arguing about a roof for an hour and a half and are enjoying it enormously — but that nothing in the afternoon is being *got through*. There is no next thing. There is a great deal of next, and none of it is at anybody's back.

Somewhere out past the trees the ugly work is being done, and it makes almost no noise, and nobody watches it, and nobody has ever thanked it.

---

The child is six or seven and has been talking about the harbour for two days.

There isn't a harbour. There is no water within a hundred kilometres and the child has never seen the sea, and knows this, and goes on describing the harbour anyway: that the rope was stiff in the cold and you had to warm it in your hands first, and that there was a dog on the wall who was not anybody's.

The grandmother listens to all of it. She is doing something with her hands and she does not stop doing it while she listens, which is a particular skill and takes about fifty years.

"How stiff?"

"Like it didn't want to."

"Mm," the grandmother says. "That's tar. They used to tar it. That's exactly what it does."

The child looks up.

"Did you have one?"

"Not me." She turns her work over. "Somebody did."

Nobody in this whole afternoon has told the child that this is imagination, or a phase, or a story. Nobody has looked at anybody else over the child's head. Two people have asked follow-up questions and one of them wrote something down, and the writing-down was done openly, at the table, the way you would note a bird.

The child will lose most of it by twelve. Almost everybody does. There is a word for that here and it is not a sad word; it is closer to the word for *milk teeth*.

---

The dog is young, brindle, about eighteen months, and belongs to the house rather than to a person.

It has spent the afternoon doing the ordinary things — the treeline, the ducks, an unpleasant interest in a bag — and at some point it comes and lies down on the grass near the man who is lying on his back with his eyes shut.

He isn't asleep. He is doing the thing you do at the end of an afternoon, which is nothing.

After a while, half in a doze, he lifts his palm off the grass. Just that. Fingers up, an inch, nothing in it, a tic.

And the dog gets up.

Front legs, then the back. Stands over him and looks down at him — *you called?* — with the particular readiness of an animal responding to a signal that nobody in this world has taught it, because nobody in this world uses that signal, because it was invented once by a tired man on a kitchen floor at four in the morning who wanted to make a dog stand up and had a word for it that he'd got from a film.

The man laughs, and puts his hand down, and says something to it, and the dog lies back down, offended.

Nothing happens. Nobody sees.

---

Later, when the light has gone orange and people are starting to think about the inside, a woman comes across the grass with two cups.

She's about fifty. She hands one down to the man, who has sat up by now, and she says something ordinary about the cold coming and how it is always the same week every year.

And then she stops.

It's very brief. She is looking at his hands around the cup — at his hands, not at his face — and there is a fraction of a second in which she does not say the next thing, and her own hand goes about two centimetres toward his and does not arrive.

"Sorry," she says. "I thought — no."

"What?"

"Nothing." She sits down on the grass beside him, which takes her a moment, because fifty. "I do that."

"Everyone does that."

"Do they?"

"Constantly," he says. "It's the commonest thing there is. Nobody ever mentions it."

They drink their tea.

She does not ask. He does not ask. It is not a rule, and nobody has forbidden anything, and there is no bittersweet music.

It is simply that they have all afternoon and all evening and, as far as either of them knows, a great deal after that, and there is no longer any reason on earth to force a thing like that in the first ninety seconds.

---

The child comes back at some point, having exhausted the harbour, and stands in front of the two of them with a book.

It's a real book — paper, sewn — and the child has got it from the house, and it has been read enough times to have gone soft at the corners.

"This is wrong," the child says.

"Which bit?"

"This bit says it one way and the other one says it different." The child holds it out, thumb jammed in the page, entirely certain, entirely unbothered, in the way of somebody who has never once been told that their certainty is inadmissible. "It's the same book. It's got the same name on it. But it doesn't say the same."

The woman takes it, and looks, and turns it to the light.

And then she gives it back, and says the thing that everybody here says, and that took about four hundred years and a great deal of unglamorous work by people whose names are not written anywhere:

"Well spotted. Go on, then."

"Go on what?"

"Check."
