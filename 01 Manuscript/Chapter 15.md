# Chapter Fifteen

Winter. Three more entries verified, which took four months, and I'll do them in one paragraph because by then the procedure was boring and boring is the point.

Entry fourteen, a 1934 survey of calendar systems, first edition against 1949: a figure about the long slow wobble of the earth's axis, attributed in 1934 to *knowledge evidently older than the Greek astronomers who recorded it*, and in 1949 to a named Greek astronomer who is now described as having first correctly described it. Entry thirty-four, a school textbook, 1972 against 1988, where the phrase *the first civilisations* appears zero times in the earlier and fourteen times in the later, and where — this is the part that took me a week to accept — nothing false has been added. Not one sentence in that book is untrue. A phrase has been installed. Entry nine, a regional archaeology monograph, where *a tradition of instruction* becomes, simply, *a tradition.*

Nine verified. The grammar was now unmistakable, and I wrote it out on one card and pinned it above the shelf:

*Givers deleted. Dates pulled down. Instruction becomes tradition. Early becomes first.*

Four moves. Ninety years. Publishers who never met, in four languages, on three subjects.

Not an idea. A **habit.**

---

And then entry twenty-two killed itself in a bookshop in Ghent and I have never been so glad about anything.

Twenty-two is the one that had excited me most, which should have been the warning. A conference proceedings volume from the nineties containing a paper — a genuinely wild paper, the kind I'd have chased at nineteen — matching rock art from four continents to shapes produced in plasma discharge experiments. Present in the first print run. Absent from the library-bound second run, with the pagination closed up as though it had never existed.

A whole paper. Not a clause. Gone, with the seam sewn shut.

I found a first run in Ghent, in a shop up a staircase, from a man called Devos who deals in scientific proceedings and has, I would estimate, four customers.

He got both volumes out on the counter, first run and library binding, because he had both, because of course he did.

"You're the man with the printings," he said. "You emailed."

"That's me."

"Show me what you think you've got."

So I showed him. Pages 148 to 161 in one, and in the other, 147 goes to 148 goes to 149 and the paper isn't there and the numbers don't skip.

He looked at it for maybe eight seconds.

Then he opened the front of the library-bound volume, put two fingers into the gutter, and pulled out a loose slip of paper about the size of a bus ticket that had been sitting in there since about 1997.

*ERRATUM.* And four lines explaining that owing to a production error one paper had been omitted from part of the print run, and that offprints were available from the secretariat on request.

I sat down on a stack of periodicals.

"You wanted it to be sinister," Devos said. Not unkindly.

"I did."

"Everybody does." He put the slip back where it lived. "You know how many of these I find? They fall out. Nobody reads them, nobody keeps them, and in twenty years somebody like you finds a hole in a run and gets very excited. It's the commonest thing in the trade."

"Have you got a lot of people like me?"

"I've got four customers," said Devos, "and you're the third-maddest."

I want to describe what I did next, because it's the only reason anything after this is worth reading.

I drove home. I opened the list. I found entry twenty-two, in the professor's hand, in blue ballpoint, and I wrote next to it in my own:

*FALSE. Production error. Erratum slip located, Ghent, 14 Feb. Struck.*

I told Noor before I told the forum, because by then I told Noor most things.

> **ME:** 22 is dead. erratum slip, tipped into a copy in ghent, 1997. production error. it was never anything.
>
> **HER:** good
>
> **ME:** good?
>
> **HER:** you have been carrying that one around like it was the best thing you had. now you are carrying eight things that are all better than it. that is a promotion.

Then, about a minute later, because she is who she is:

> **HER:** are you going to publish it
>
> **ME:** yes
>
> **HER:** ok. then I will stop worrying about you for a bit.

Then — and this took me two days to make myself do — I went to the forum where I'd posted the dull true thing, under the same account, and I posted a correction. Publicly. Saying that a claim of this shape, which I had been pursuing, had turned out to be an ordinary printing accident, and here was the erratum slip, and here was the shop, and if anyone had been persuaded by me they should un-persuade themselves.

Nine views.

You want to know why I bothered? Two reasons, and only one of them is noble.

The noble one: if I don't publish the ones that die, I'm not doing this, I'm just decorating.

The other one is that the professor never struck anything out. Forty-one entries and not one correction. Which either means the man was right forty-one times in a row, or it means he stopped writing down the ones that failed — and if it's the second one, then his list is not a record, it's a case, and a case is a thing you can dismiss.

I needed mine to be a record. Even at nine entries. Even at eight.

I crossed one out so that the other eight would be worth something, and I have never regretted a single thing in this business more or less than that.


---

Now the thing that came out of the failure, which I would never have found if twenty-two had held.

Because of Ghent I had spent a fortnight in the ugly bottom drawer of this subject — conference proceedings, regional monographs, offprints, series with print runs of four hundred, the material that no library buys and no scholar cites and no publisher reprints. Nobody's second impressions. Nobody's anything.

And it occurred to me, sitting on a stack of periodicals in a cold shop in Ghent, that I had never once looked *there* for edits.

So I went looking. Three months. About sixty titles, wherever I could get two states of the same text, in the least important corners of the least important subjects I could find.

I found nothing. Not one edit. Not a clause, not a date, not a giver removed.

And that, finally, was the thing I'd been waiting a year and a half for.

---

Let me put it plainly, because it took me a long time to be able to.

If some process is quietly rewriting our record of the deep past, and that process is *impersonal* — a drift, a fashion, a habit of language, the slow tidying of a discipline that prefers tidy — then it should be everywhere. Fashions don't care who's reading. Drift doesn't check the circulation figures. If it's weather, it rains on the regional monographs too.

It doesn't.

The edits are dense in exactly one kind of place: the books that get quoted. Textbooks. Standard surveys. Popular titles with long lives. The ones that get into the next generation's footnotes and the ones that end up in the bibliography of everything else. That's where they are. And in the material nobody would ever check, there is nothing at all, because nobody would ever check it.

Which means the edits are **aimed.**

Somebody is deciding where the effort goes. Somebody has a model of who reads what. The gaps in the pattern are not random — the gaps are shaped like an audience, and you can see the shape of the audience by looking at the holes.

I wrote it in the file at two in the morning and I've never had to change it:

*The absence is the fingerprint. Weather doesn't skip the cheap seats.*

The other track. I've been putting this off for two chapters and I'd rather do it in daylight.

The window closed. That's the short version.

I did the chemical thing three more times across that winter, always alone, always measured, always logged, because if I'm going to do something that people would use against me I'm at least going to do it with a notebook. And the third and fourth times told me nothing the first hadn't. Same aperture. Same enormous sense of *there is a great deal on the other side of this*. Same complete inability to get through.

A plateau. In my world a plateau is information: the instrument has a range and you have reached the end of it. You don't get more by turning the dial harder; that's how people hurt themselves, in every field I've ever worked in.

So I stopped, and looked around for the other instrument, and found the one that has been sitting there in every tradition on earth for three thousand years being extremely boring.

I started sitting.

I was terrible at it. I want to be accurate about the register here, because I know what the word *meditation* does to people, and it did the same thing to me: I pictured a man in linen trousers being calm about his breathing, and I have never in my life wanted to be that man.

What it actually was, for the first four months, was: a kitchen chair, twenty minutes, a timer, and an old dog watching me with visible concern. Nothing happened. My legs hurt. My head produced, in order: the shopping, a work thing, a golf swing, an argument I lost in 2011, the shopping again.

Ana walked in on me at week six.

"What are you doing?"

"Sitting."

"You're sitting in the dark, in a chair, facing a wall, at half past five in the morning."

"Yes."

She thought about it. Then she said, "Is this instead of a midlife crisis, or is it the midlife crisis?" and left, and I heard her tell the dog in the hallway, "He's meditating," in the tone you'd use for *he's joined a band.*

---

Here's what I got, eventually, and it wasn't peace.

Somewhere in the fifth month, in that dull grey twenty minutes, things started coming up that had no business being there.

Not visions. I want to be extremely careful. Nothing announced itself, nothing spoke, nothing glowed.

What happened was that I would be sitting there, bored, and there would be a *face*. For less than a second. Perfectly specific — an actual face, with a jaw and an expression and a weather to it — belonging to nobody I have ever met, and gone before I could look at it.

And hands. More often hands than faces. Working hands, mostly. A hand doing something with rope. A hand on a doorframe.

I did what I do. I logged them. Date, duration, description, and a column I labelled *familiarity* on a scale of one to five, because I am who I am.

And what the log said, after four months, was the thing that started the last part of this story.

They weren't new.

I have been getting these my whole life. In the shower. On aeroplanes. In the four seconds before sleep, which everyone gets and which is why I'd never thought about it for one instant in forty-four years. A face, a hand, a room I don't know, cold mud, a grey sky — filed instantly, automatically, under *tired*, or *stress*, or *that's just what heads do*, and gone.

I have a folder for anomalous printings and I have kept it for ten years.

I have never once, in my entire life, kept a record of the anomalies in my own head.

The one system I never instrumented was me.

I want to be honest that at the time I did not think this had anything whatsoever to do with page 118, a list of forty-one entries, or a man who walked east out of Urfa in 1929. I thought it was a separate hobby that I was ashamed of in a different way.

I logged it in a different file.

I even called it something else.
