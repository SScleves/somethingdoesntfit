# Chapter Twenty-One

Entry thirty-one isn't a book, which is why I'd left it for last.

It's three sheets of paper in an archive that has been open to the public since the seventies, and the only reason nobody has made anything of it is that there is nothing to make of it. I want to be honest about that before I start. This is the weakest thing in my file and I nearly cut it, and the only reason it's here is what it did to me personally.

Sheet one is a page from a flight log, October 1943. Handwritten, in pencil, by a man whose name is on it and whose service record you can look up. He describes an object he could not identify, keeping station off his wing for a little over two minutes, and he uses a phrase that I would defend as good technical writing in any decade:

> *without visible means of propulsion.*

That's what a pilot writes when he is being careful. Not *no engine.* Not *impossible.* He wrote down the limit of what he could see and stopped there, and I would hire that man.

Sheet two is the typed transcript, filed the following spring. Same words. Same order. One addition:

> *without, reportedly, visible means of propulsion.*

Seven letters and two commas. The pilot's careful sentence becomes a claim somebody has made. The observation becomes a story about an observation. Nobody said the man was lying; nobody had to; the sentence now stands a step back from itself and points at him.

Sheet three is not about that incident at all. It's a memo, a general one, unremarkable, of the kind that must have gone out in their thousands, and it concerns how the reporting of such sightings should be managed within the unit. It is two paragraphs long and it is entirely reasonable and it ends by advising that further reports of this kind be

> *handled with humour.*

---

I read that phrase in a library at half past two on a Wednesday afternoon and something happened in my chest that I have not been able to describe to anybody since.

Because I know that phrase. It's mine.

I have a line I have used at tables for twenty years. It's one of my best. It comes out when a subject is getting heavy and I want to bring it down, and it always gets a laugh, and I have never once in two decades wondered where I got it or whether it was true:

*The best way to kill an invention isn't to ban it. It's to file it under entertainment.*

Nobody argues with a toy.

I said that at a funeral. I have said it at four barbecues. I said it to my wife in a car. I have been walking around for twenty years with the method in my pocket, deploying it as *charm*, for laughs, to make myself likeable and to make heavy subjects light — and there it is on a piece of paper from 1944, in a two-paragraph memo about administration, in the driest possible language, as an instruction.

That's the whole trick. That's all of it.

You don't suppress a thing. Suppression is expensive and it makes martyrs and it tells everybody where to look. You make it *funny*. You put it next to the horoscopes. You let it be talked about all day long, at length, by anybody, in a register that guarantees nobody serious will ever put their name near it — and then you can leave the actual evidence lying around in an open archive for fifty years, because the mask does the work for free, and the mask is worn voluntarily by the very people who are looking.

I sat in that library and thought about being ten years old and being told I'd misremembered.

And then I thought about a man at a market with a beard who is right in interesting ways, and about all the years I spent deciding he was ninety per cent nonsense, and about how comfortable that decision had been.

---

Two weeks later they did it to me, and it took about ninety minutes, and I never found out who.

I should explain the persona, and I have put it off for three years because it is the most embarrassing thing I own.

I have an account. I've had it since 2013. It doesn't have my name on it and it never has, and I use it on maybe five forums, and it does exactly one thing: it posts small, true, checkable, slightly strange facts. That's it. That's the whole persona. Twelve years of it, a few hundred posts, *did you know that Ethiopia is running seven years behind us and it isn't an error*, *did you know they found a working steam engine in the first century and shelved it as a party trick*, *did you know there is a broadsheet in a Zurich library where a printer in 1561 describes something over Nuremberg and nobody has ever satisfactorily explained it.*

Light. Never the crank version. Never a theory attached. I have a rule, and I've kept it for twelve years: **if I can't hand you the source and let you go and disagree with me, I don't post it.**

Ana knows about it, in the way she knows I have a folder. She thinks it's sweet. She thinks it's the trivia habit with a keyboard. She has never asked to see it and I have never offered, and if you want to know why, it's because it's the only place in my life where I get to be interested in something out loud without watching a face do the thing faces do.

That account is where the dull true post went, in December, the one about page 44 of a paperback with a ziggurat on the cover. That's the one that got the polite correction. That's the one that put my name on a form somewhere.

So it wasn't hard to find. It has twelve years of posts under it and any competent person could reconstruct the whole shape of me from it in an afternoon, which is roughly what I'd been counting on nobody bothering to do.

---

Here's what I found, on a Tuesday night in April, because somebody had linked to it in a thread about something else.

My post about the paperback was still there. Same title, same date, same account.

It was about four times as long as I'd written it.

Everything I'd actually said was still in it, word for word, in the right order. The page number, the two printings, the photographs, the flat statement. Untouched.

And around it, before and after, in *my* register — this is the part that keeps me up — three paragraphs I did not write. Not raving. Not capital letters; whoever did this was far too good for capital letters. Three paragraphs of *slightly too much.* A date that is wrong by fourteen years, stated with confidence. A sentence connecting the paperback to a thing it has nothing to do with. And, at the end, one clause about which families have known this for a long time.

That's it. That's the whole operation. Ninety-eight per cent of it is mine and true and checkable, and about two per cent has been added, and the two per cent is chosen so precisely that no serious person will ever read past it.

I know it's good because I did the test. I sent it — the doctored version, with no explanation — to Noor, who knows me, who has argued with me for four months, who is the single most exacting person I have ever dealt with.

She wrote back: *is this you? the middle bit is fine but who are the families*

That's a woman on my side, reading carefully, in about the time it takes to read it.

---

I did the sensible thing, which is not always true of me.

I went to the site. I could not get into the account — the password had gone, and the recovery email had gone, and the address it now pointed at was one character different from mine in a way you would not see if you weren't looking for it. I wrote to the moderators. Three of the five forums never replied; two did, and both were kind, and both explained, correctly, that they had no way of knowing which version was the original, because their edit history only goes back ninety days and this had been done to posts from 2014.

So I did the other thing. I made a new account and I posted, under my own initials, a correction: that the account had been compromised, that specific paragraphs in specific posts were not mine, that here is what the original said, and here is the photograph, and here is the page.

It got nine views.

Nine. Over a fortnight.

The doctored post has been up since 2019 in a form I didn't write, and by then it had been quoted four times, once by somebody who was being funny about it.

And that's the arithmetic, and it is the only thing I would put on a wall. **A claim and its correction do not travel at the same speed, and they never have, and the gap between those two speeds is where all of this lives.** You don't have to censor a man. You add two per cent, and the man spends the rest of his life at nine views.

---

I stopped, after that. Not the thing you would assume.

I did not stop looking. I have never once stopped looking.

I stopped trying to be *believed.* That was the last week of April, at a kitchen table, with an old dog under it, and I remember the exact shape of the thought: *there is no version of this where I convince anybody, and there never was, and I have been spending about a third of my energy on an outcome that was never available.*

It's the closest thing to peace I'd had in a year. It's also, if you're keeping score, precisely the moment I became dangerous, because a man who has given up on persuading you has only got one thing left to do.

---

The barbecue was Ana's idea and I would like the record to show that I was against it.

May, first proper weekend of it, the garden, about fourteen people. Her friends from work. The neighbours with the twins. Frank, who came in a shirt so aggressively floral that three separate people photographed it. A man from the forums who I had made the mistake of telling about the market, who found out about the barbecue by a route I have never established and who turned up at four with a bottle of wine and a printout, and who was, I want to say, harmless and lonely and about ten per cent right about one thing, and Beard Man would have hated him.

And Dave, who arrived with a six-pack and an enormous bag of ice, because he is the only man I have ever met who thinks about ice.

He was magnificent. I don't know how else to put it. Within forty minutes he had been recruited to the barbecue itself by Ana's friend Saskia, who does not delegate; he had the twins convinced he could take his thumb off; and he had got Frank talking about his father's boat, which I have worked next to Frank for six years and did not know existed.

At one point I came out with a tray and Ana was sitting on the step with him, and she was talking about her grandmother.

Not the version. The real one — the knitting she'd undo and start again, the blanket, the lame TV shows. My wife does not do this. She does not do it with people she has known for four hours and a good number of people she has known for thirty years.

And Dave was doing the thing that almost nobody does, which is that he wasn't waiting. He wasn't queueing up a story about his own grandmother. He was just sitting on a step with a beer, saying *and what was the programme?* and *did she ever finish any of them?*

Ana said, "Never. Not one. She'd get to the end of a row and pull the whole thing out."

"Why?"

"I never asked her. I was a kid, it was just what she did." And then, and this is my wife all over: "I've been thinking lately she was probably just checking it. Whether it held. And I've decided I don't want to know, because the other version is funnier and she'd have preferred it."

Dave laughed. And then he said, "She sounds like she was good value," which is exactly the right size of thing to say, and Ana said, "She was the best of us," and drank her wine.

I stood there holding a tray of sausages and felt something I could not name for about two hours and eventually identified, late that night, doing the washing up, as *gratitude.*

---

Buddy did not go near him. All afternoon.

I noticed at the time and thought nothing whatsoever of it. He was on the cool flagstones by the back door where he always is in May, and fourteen people came past him and every one of them got the tail, twice, the short version of a dance he used to do.

Dave crouched down to him once, early on, offering the back of his hand the way you do, the way he'd done at the golf course, and Buddy looked at it and then looked away, out at the garden, not moving, not growling, just — declining.

"He's twelve," I said. "He's got opinions now."

"Fair enough," said Dave, and stood up easily, and went to get more ice.

Mora went to him twice. The first time with the whole undignified black hurtle, the resurrection greeting she gives everybody. And then about ten minutes later she came back, sniffed him properly, and went and lay down under the table by Buddy, and stayed there, which for that dog is roughly the equivalent of a man leaving a party without saying goodbye.

---

The click came at about half seven and it lasted four seconds and I laughed.

The light was going. Most people were down the garden. I was at the table with Dave and Frank and the forum man, and the forum man had been talking for a while about something, and it was getting heavy in the specific way a garden gets heavy when one person is talking too long and everybody can feel it.

So I did the thing. Twenty years of reflex, out it came, no more thought in it than putting your hand up when something's thrown at you.

"Did you know the Romans built a working steam engine? First century. Hero of Alexandria. Spun on a boiler, actually worked. And they shelved it as a toy, because slaves were cheaper, so there was nothing for it to solve —"

"— and the best way to kill an invention isn't to ban it," Dave said. "It's to file it under entertainment."

He said it into his beer, half-turned away, looking down the garden at the twins doing something to a football.

Then he said, "Nobody argues with a toy," and drank.

Frank said, "That's *good*," and the forum man wanted to know who said it originally, and I said I had no idea and that I'd been stealing it for years, and Dave said, "Everybody steals it, it's that kind of line," and Ana called from the kitchen door to ask where the big tongs were.

And that was it. That was the whole thing. Four seconds, at a barbecue, between the tongs and the football.

I laughed. I remember laughing. I remember being *pleased* — the small specific pleasure of somebody knowing the same obscure thing you know, which for a man like me is about as close to intimacy as it gets in a garden.

I want to put down here, as flatly as I know how, everything that was true in that moment and that I did not assemble for another nine weeks:

That I had been reading a 1944 memo containing that instruction, in a library, six weeks earlier, and had never told a living soul.

That the line is not from anywhere. I have looked. Three years I've looked now. It is not in any book, it is not attributed to anybody, and the only place in the world it exists in that exact form, word for word, in that order, is in the mouth of a man who has been saying it at tables for twenty years to make himself likeable.

And that the previous winter, an account with twelve years of small true facts on it had posted one dull correct thing about page 44 of a paperback, and somebody, somewhere, had read every single word of it.

I laughed, and I went and found the tongs.
