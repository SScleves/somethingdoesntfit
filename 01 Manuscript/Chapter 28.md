# Chapter Twenty-Eight

I did not go and look at the building. I did not go, and it was the hardest thing I did all year.

There is a version of this where a man flies to Turkey with a hire car and sits on a hillside with binoculars for three days, and I thought about it for about a fortnight, and the reason I didn't is that I finally worked out what I'd be doing: I'd be **collecting permission slips.** Same as Antwerp, three years earlier, standing in front of a case of printing plates learning that a thing which had obviously happened could happen. It feels exactly like progress and it is exactly not.

There is nothing on a hillside that a satellite hasn't already told me, and there is a great deal in the paperwork that nobody has ever read.

So I did four months at a kitchen table.

---

Here is the actual method, and it's dull, and dull is the whole point.

**Planning and construction.** Any building of that size in that country generates a file: the application, the geotechnical report, the structural drawings, the environmental screening. Some of it is online, some of it is available on request from the provincial authority, and the request is a form, and the form is in Turkish, and there is a woman in Rotterdam who does technical translation for nine cents a word and who has never once asked me why.

**Utilities.** A water licence, an electrical connection agreement, and — the good one — a fire-suppression certification, because a building with an inert-gas suppression system has to declare the protected volume.

Their declared protected volume is about forty per cent larger than the building.

I sat with that for a week and got it wrong twice before I got it right, and the right answer is not exciting: the figure includes a subsurface volume, it's declared honestly, and the certificate is a public document because that is what a fire certificate is for. Nobody is hiding it. It's simply that nobody has ever put the fire certificate next to the geotechnical annex, because why would you.

**Procurement.** This is the seam, and it is the seam in every organisation I have ever worked in. An institution can keep almost anything quiet except **what it buys**. Foundation-funded bodies publish tenders and awards. I have twenty years of awarded contracts: a climate-plant maintenance contract, a security systems contract, a pest management contract, and — the one I read four times — a rolling contract for the supply of **archival boxboard and polyester sleeving** at a volume that is not consistent with a regional finds depot.

You do not sleeve pot sherds.

**Staffing.** Job advertisements are public and they are honest, because they have to be, because otherwise the wrong people apply. Over twelve years that place has advertised for: two paper conservators, a photographic technician, a book conservator (twice), a cataloguer with Ottoman and Latin, and a facilities engineer with experience of Class 1 archival environments.

Not one archaeologist. In twelve years.

---

Then I got it wrong, and I want to do this properly because it cost me seven weeks and about nine hundred euros.

In among the procurement I found a contractor: a small Turkish firm that had held the climate-plant maintenance contract from 2013 to 2019. Four employees. A website with a photograph of a van.

And I built a whole thing on it. Weeks of it. The maintenance contractor has physical access on a schedule, the maintenance contractor's engineers see the inside of the building routinely, the maintenance contractor is a small firm and small firms are not secure, and I speak fluent facilities engineer — I have spent twenty years in rooms with those people, they are my favourite people in any building, and I know exactly how to be one of them on a phone.

I got as far as an actual conversation, in careful English, with a man called Erhan who was perfectly nice.

The contract ended in 2019.

It ended in 2019 because it was competitively retendered and they lost, which is public, which was in the award notice I had already read and had skimmed, because I had found the thing I wanted and had stopped looking at the thing in front of me — which is precisely and exactly the failure I have been lecturing everybody about for three years, committed by me, at the last possible moment, on the most important document I had.

*First few words, last few words, length about right, done.*

Seven weeks. I told Ana and she said, "Well, at least you're consistent," which is the most useful thing anybody said to me that winter.

---

The way in was in the collections policy, on page four, in a section headed *Access*, and I had read that document a dozen times.

> *4.2 Supervised access to collection items may be arranged for researchers and other bona fide enquirers. Requests should be made in writing, quoting the accession number where known, with an indication of the purpose of the enquiry. Requests are considered by the Keeper of Collections.*

Requests should be made in writing.

That's it. That's the door. It's on page four of a PDF that has been on a public website since 2019 and I would put money on the number of people who have read it being under fifty.

Now — I want to lay out why this works, because when I explained it to Ana she said "that can't be it," and she was voicing the correct objection, which is that it is *too easy* and nothing in three years had been easy.

It works for exactly the reason everything else failed.

A place like that has two defences and they are both excellent. It is defended against **theft** — climate control, accession numbers, a security contract, a good specification, and the plain fact that stealing from an archive is a specialist crime that almost nobody can monetise. And it is defended against **exposure**, superbly, by a century of practice and a style guide in its fourth revision with a whole section on what to do when a reader notices.

Everyone who has ever worked out what that building is has wanted to do one of those two things.

Nobody has ever written a letter.

And they cannot refuse one. That's the beautiful part and it took me a month to be certain of it. If a bona fide enquirer writes, on paper, with his real name, citing an accession number out of their own published transfer schedule, in the ordinary language of the trade — then **saying no is the only response that means anything.** A yes means nothing; a yes is what every depot in the world says on a Tuesday. A no, to a specific published accession number, from a facility whose entire existence is predicated on being an ordinary conservation depot, is a data point that can be published, and they know that better than I do, because they wrote § 6.

Their whole hundred-year method rests on never confirming anything.

I was going to ask them a question that could only be answered by confirming something.

---

*Ask.*

One word, in different ink, shakier, at the bottom of a page, under a set of coordinates and a date circled three weeks after the man died.

For three years I read that as a clue. A pointer. Ask *who*, ask *what*, an instruction with a missing object.

It isn't. There's no missing object.

Hendrik Maasen spent forty years doing what his father-in-law's generation had done before him: verifying, filing, copying out in a fair hand, never publishing, never knocking on a door, never once in four decades putting his name on a piece of paper and posting it to the people concerned. He had a rule and he kept it and the rule was *paper only, never people*, and I believe he made that rule after something he did in the nineties that cost somebody else, and I will never know what it was.

And then at sixty-nine, with a bad heart, having stopped sleeping, he wrote down one word at the bottom of his own life's work, and it is not addressed to me.

It's the thing he'd finally understood he should have done at thirty.

He circled a date three weeks after he died. I've never found out what it was for. But he'd got as far as a date.

---

I wrote the letter on the fourth of March and it took nine days and about forty drafts and it is four sentences long.

I'm not going to give you the accession number. It's from the 2017 transfer schedule, annex C, and it is one item, and I chose it because it is dull and because it is *theirs* — it is in their own published list, in their own numbering, on their own paper.

The letter says who I am, and what I do for a living, and that I have an interest in variant states in interwar academic printing. It says that I understand from the published transfer schedule that item number so-and-so was received by them in 2017. It asks whether supervised access might be arranged, on any date convenient to them, for one day. It gives my address, my telephone number, and my email.

It does not mention Tresidder. It does not mention page 118, or the list, or a foundation, or a man in a station café, or forty years of a dead man's handwriting.

It is, in every particular, the letter that a slightly obsessive amateur with a dull hobby writes to an archive, and every single word of it is true, and that is the only reason it works.

I signed it with my own name.

Ana read it before I posted it and said, "It's very boring."

"That's the idea."

"No, I mean it's *boring*, James. It's the most boring thing you've ever written and you write configuration files."

"Good."

She handed it back. "You know what's mad? Of everything you've done in three years — the ruler, and the man at the station, and the thing in the kitchen — this is the first time I've been properly frightened."

"Why this?"

"Because it's got our address on it," Ana said.

---

I posted it on the fifteenth of March and then I did the other things.

I spoke to Frank, and took the leave, and told him it was to do with Ana's mother's flat, and he said "take longer, you look terrible," which is Frank.

I made the arrangements for Buddy, which were more complicated than the arrangements for me. He was on four things twice a day by then and the new mats were down all through the ground floor, and Ana knows all of it and did not need telling, and I told her anyway, twice, and she let me.

And I did the small thing in the bathroom with the tap running, one last time, and made two changes to it — one because of something the man in the station café had said, and one because of a date.

Then I put the phone back in the drawer under the spare toothbrushes.

---

The reply came on the nineteenth of April, by email, from an address at the foundation, in perfectly good English, and it was five lines long.

They thanked me for my enquiry. They confirmed that the item was in their care. They noted that the reading room was available on Tuesdays and Wednesdays and asked me to propose two possible dates. They asked me to bring photographic identification. They asked me not to bring a bag into the reading room and advised that pencils only were permitted.

*Pencils only.*

I sat in my kitchen with an old dog on his mats and read that sentence about nine times, because it is the single most ordinary sentence in this entire book, and because after three years and forty entries and two thousand kilometres of paper, the thing that finally opened was a door that had never been locked.

I wrote back and proposed two dates in May.

They took the first one.
