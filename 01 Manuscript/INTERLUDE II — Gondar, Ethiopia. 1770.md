# Gondar, Ethiopia
### 1770

The Scotsman is enormous and he has been ill for most of a year.

He is six foot four in a century when that is a spectacle, red-haired, thirty-nine years old, a former wine merchant and a former consul at Algiers, and he has walked and ridden into the highlands of Abyssinia because he intends to find where the Nile comes from and he does not intend to be talked out of it.

He arrives at Gondar in February. It is a real city with a real castle and a court, and it is in the middle of a civil war, and the man who actually holds the power is a warlord from Tigray who is about seventy and is said, credibly, to have had people executed in the street outside the palace that week.

Bruce is useful to them because he can do medicine. Smallpox is in the city. He treats the royal children and he treats the ras's household, and this buys him the thing that no European in that century has been able to buy, which is time, and access, and the right to be a curiosity at court for three years rather than a prisoner.

He learns the language. He copies things down. He is arrogant, and vain, and an extremely good observer, and both halves of that are going to matter enormously.

---

Now the thing about the church there.

The Ethiopian church is older than most of the churches arguing about it. It has been Christian since the fourth century, which is before England, before Ireland, before most of Europe, and — this is the part that matters — it was cut off. Not persecuted. **Isolated**, geographically, by the rise of Islam across North Africa in the seventh century, so that for the better part of a thousand years the Ethiopian church went on being a church in the mountains while Europe went on being Europe somewhere else.

And an isolated system does not get the updates.

Their canon is bigger than everybody else's. Eighty-one books, depending on how you count, and among them there are texts that the rest of Christendom argued about for two hundred years and then let go of — quietly, without a decree, without a bonfire, in the ordinary way that things stop being copied because they have stopped being read.

Their calendar is different too. Seven or eight years behind the Gregorian, because of a different calculation of the date of the Annunciation, and thirteen months, and a new year in September. It is not an error and it has never been corrected. There are people alive right now with two entirely legitimate answers to what year it is.

They simply never ran the patch.

---

Among the books in that canon is one called Enoch.

It is a very strange book and it is very old. The oldest fragments of it that anybody has ever found are in Aramaic, and they were found in a cave near the Dead Sea in the twentieth century, and they are older than most of the New Testament.

What it is about is instruction.

Two hundred of them come down. In the story they are called the Watchers, and their crime is not violence and it is not lust, or not only; the crime that gets itemised, name by name, in a list, is **teaching.**

One of them teaches the making of blades and breastplates, and the working of metal out of the ground.
One teaches the cutting of roots, and one the loosing of spells.
One teaches the signs of the sun. One the signs of the moon. One the signs of the clouds and the weather.
One teaches them cosmetics — how to darken the eyelid, how to make jewellery from stone.

And for this they are bound in the valleys of the earth, in darkness, until the judgement.

That's the book. That is what is in it: an itemised list of the useful arts, presented as an indictment.

It is quoted in the New Testament — in the Epistle of Jude, by name, as prophecy. Tertullian defended it in the second century and said plainly that he thought it was scripture. And then, over the following two hundred years, the Greek and Latin churches let it go, and by the time Athanasius circulates his list of the approved books in 367 it isn't on it, and it goes out of use, and then out of copying, and then out of existence.

For a thousand years, in Europe, the Book of Enoch does not exist. It is known only as a name — a thing Jude quotes, which nobody can produce.

Scholars assume it's lost. Several assume it never existed as a whole book at all.

It has been on a shelf in Ethiopia the entire time, in Ge'ez, complete, in the liturgy, being read out loud, every year, by people who were never asked and would have been surprised by the question.

---

Bruce leaves Gondar at the end of 1771 and it takes him two more years to get home, most of it appalling, some of it nearly fatal.

He brings out three manuscripts of Enoch.

One he presents to the Bodleian at Oxford. One goes to the royal library in Paris. One he keeps.

He hands the most sought-after lost book in European scholarship across a desk to a librarian, and the reaction of the learned world is — nothing. Nothing at all. It sits in the Bodleian, in a language almost nobody in Britain can read, for the better part of fifty years, until in 1821 an Oxford professor called Richard Laurence finally translates it into English and Europe reads Enoch for the first time since the fourth century.

Fifty years. On a shelf, catalogued, in a public library, available to anyone who asked.

Nobody asked.

---

And Bruce.

He publishes his account in 1790, five volumes, and London decides he is a liar.

Not on any particular point at first — simply as a posture. He is too big, too pleased with himself, too Scottish, and his book contains things that men in coffee houses know to be impossible. He says the Abyssinians cut steaks from living cattle and eat the meat raw. He describes a court, and a queen, and a civil war, and a Christianity older than their own, in a country that the polite opinion of Europe has decided is a fable.

He is mocked in print for years. Dr Johnson, who had himself translated a book about Abyssinia without ever going near the place, did not believe him. It becomes a genre: the Bruce anecdote, the traveller's tale, the man who went to the moon and came back with a story.

He spends the rest of his life defending a book that is substantially, boringly, verifiably true.

Almost every single thing in it was confirmed by later travellers. The raw beef too.

He died in 1794, at home, by falling down his own staircase while helping a lady to her carriage.

---

So: a man walks a very long way into a landscape and comes back out of it carrying a book about the ones who taught us things and were punished for it — and the response is not suppression, and it is not violence, and nobody edits a plate.

The response is that he is laughed at for twenty years, and the book sits in a library for fifty, and both of those cost precisely nothing and required no one to do anything at all.

*Bruce's manuscripts are in the Bodleian and the Bibliothèque nationale. Laurence's 1821 translation is out of copyright and free. The Ethiopian church still reads Enoch, and it is still September when their year turns over.*
