# Chapter Twenty-Nine

The taxi from Şanlıurfa took fifty minutes and cost about what a taxi costs, and the driver talked to me for most of it about a football transfer.

I want to start with that because it sets the register for everything that follows. Nothing in this chapter is dramatic. It is the least dramatic day of my life and it is the day everything actually happened.

The turning off the provincial road is signposted. There is a barrier at the top of the spur, and it was up, and there was nobody in the hut beside it, because it was a Tuesday morning in May and the barrier is for lorries.

The car park has thirty marked bays and there were nine cars in it. The trees along the eastern side, which somebody has been watering for a decade, are now about six metres tall and give shade to the bays nearest the building, and there is a bicycle rack, and there was one bicycle in it.

I was seven minutes early. I sat on a low wall for six of them.

---

The reception desk has a visitor book.

An actual book. Hardbound, ruled columns: date, name, organisation, person visiting, time in, time out. The woman behind the desk turned it round and gave me a pen and photocopied my passport and gave me a lanyard with a clip.

While she was doing the photocopy I read the page.

Fourteen entries since the beginning of March. A plumbing contractor twice. Somebody from the provincial directorate. A courier. Two people from a university in Ankara on the same day in April — a joint entry, an hour and forty minutes, *person visiting: A. Pekcan.*

Two months. Fourteen lines. On a hardbound page with columns, in biro, in a building that holds — I now know — about eleven kilometres of shelving.

That's the whole book, right there, on the desk, in the most boring object in the building. Nobody comes. Nobody has ever come. There is nothing to defend against, because the defence is that there is no reason for anybody to be interested, and that defence has held for twelve years and it held against me too until the day I wrote a four-sentence letter about a dull item.

I signed my name and wrote *research* in the purpose column and got a lanyard, and that is how you get into the most important room in this book.

---

Dr Aylin Pekcan is the Keeper of Collections and she came out to get me herself, and I liked her within about eleven seconds, which I have had to make my peace with.

Mid-forties. Enormously competent, in the particular way of somebody who runs a technical facility and has no patience left over. She apologised twice on the walk down the corridor: once because the retrieval would take twenty minutes, and once because the reading room was cold, and she said the cold one like a woman who has apologised for it four hundred times and thinks the visitors are wrong.

"It is eighteen degrees," she said. "Eighteen is correct. People want twenty-two. Twenty-two is for people, this building is not for people."

The reading room is one table, four chairs, a foam cradle, a snake weight, a lamp on an arm, and a window with a blind at half-mast. Pencils in a jar.

She took my bag. She left me the notebook after checking it, which is standard, and she gave me a pencil.

"Twenty minutes," she said. "There is water."

---

The item is a 1931 offprint of a paper on flood traditions in the upper Euphrates, forty-one pages, in a plain wrapper, and it is exactly what the transfer schedule says it is, and it is of no interest whatsoever.

I chose it because it was boring. I have never been so pleased with a decision.

I looked at it for about forty minutes, properly, with the lamp laid down flat the way a woman in Leiden taught me, and it is a perfectly ordinary offprint that has been well kept, and there is a stamp on the wrapper from a university library that no longer exists in that form, and somebody in 1953 wrote a shelfmark on it in pencil and somebody in 2017 wrote a new accession number on a slip that is not attached to it but laid in beside it, which is correct practice.

Then Dr Pekcan came back to see whether I needed anything, and I did the thing I had come two thousand kilometres to do, and it took one sentence.

I said: "May I ask you something about the transfer schedule?"

---

She sat down.

That's it. That's the entire operation. She sat down, across the table, in a cold reading room, on a Tuesday morning in May, because a man had asked her a question about her own paperwork and nobody had ever done that.

I want to be honest about the next hour: it was the best professional conversation of my life and I was there under something that was not quite false pretences and not quite honest ones.

I asked how the 2017 material had come in. She told me — nine consignments, eleven months, one of them a disaster because of a customs delay and a van without proper conditioning, and she is still angry about it. I asked how she catalogues mixed material with three previous numbering systems on it. She got a piece of paper and drew me the concordance structure and it is genuinely elegant. I asked what she does with the old shelfmarks and she said, with feeling, "We keep them. *We keep them.* You would be amazed."

And then I asked the question I had written on the inside of my wrist in biro on the plane, in the specific dull register of a man asking about workflow:

"When you take in a deaccessioned collection — do you ever get the duplicates as well? Or only the items with provenance?"

And Dr Aylin Pekcan said:

"We take everything. That is the point of us."

---

She showed me the stacks.

I did not ask to see the stacks. I want that clearly stated, because I have gone over the ethics of that day more than I have gone over anything else in my life: **I did not ask.** She offered, in the way that any professional offers to show you the good part when you have demonstrated that you can understand it, and I said yes.

We went through two sets of doors and down a ramp and it got colder and quieter, and the second set of doors is where the building stops being a building, and past that it is cut rock with a concrete floor and a very good lighting scheme, and it goes back a long way.

Rolling stacks. Powered, on rails, twelve of them in the first chamber and I did not count the chambers. The air is dry and moving and it smells of almost nothing, which is the smell of an enormous amount of money spent correctly.

She ran a stack open for me with a handle and let me look down the aisle.

Books.

Boxes of paper, sleeved, in archival board, labelled in three systems, in perfect order, at eighteen degrees and forty-five per cent, in a rock chamber in Anatolia, going back into the dark.

---

I need to tell you what I felt, because it was not what I had spent three years expecting and it undid me completely.

I had come to find a **hoard.** I'd been imagining it for a year: a vault, a trove, stolen things in the dark, the loot of a century.

That is not what that place is.

It is the best-run archive I have ever stood in. Every single object in it has been *saved*. Not hidden — saved, in the technical sense: cleaned, deacidified where necessary, boxed, sleeved, catalogued, given a stable environment and a concordance to its old numbers, and kept by people who are proud of it and who apologise about the temperature.

Half the material on those shelves would be pulp. Genuinely. That is not their propaganda, it's the actual counterfactual — the deaccession waves of the 2000s were real and most of what got deaccessioned did get destroyed, everywhere, by everybody, for the honest reason that storage costs money and a scan is cheaper. And a foundation with a good specification came along and said: *we'll take it.*

They are not the people who destroyed the record.

They are the people who **have** it.

And I stood in that aisle with my hand on a rolling stack and understood the thing that took the floor out from under three years:

*It was never destruction disguised as help. It is help. It's real help, done properly, by good people, at great expense.*

*The crime is the list of who's allowed in.*

Fourteen names in a visitor book in two months.

---

The reference section is at the end of the second chamber, near the plant room, on ordinary open shelving, spine out, at eye level. Standards, conservation manuals, a run of a journal, three ring binders of environmental logs, the fire procedures in a red folder, and — this is not hidden, it is the fourth item from the left — a bound A4 volume about four centimetres thick with a printed spine label.

Dr Pekcan was six metres away at the plant-room door talking to a man about a chiller. She had left me looking at a stack, which is what you do with a visitor who has demonstrated they can be left, and I was left for about nine minutes.

I read what I could in nine minutes, standing up, and I want to say clearly what I did and didn't do. I did not take it. I did not photograph it; there is no phone in that building, they hold your bag. I read it, standing at a shelf, at eye level, and I have a memory that has been professionally trained for twenty years to hold configuration parameters, and I wrote the whole thing down four hours later in a hotel room in Şanlıurfa in a notebook I still have.

It is not a secret document. That is the thing I need you to understand before I tell you what's in it. It is on an open shelf next to the fire procedures because it is **the operations manual for the facility**, and the people who work there need it, and there is no version of running a building like that where the manual is kept somewhere the staff can't reach.

The spine label says: **OPERATING FRAMEWORK — CONSOLIDATED. REV. 11.**

---

I'm going to give you the structure, because the structure is the argument.

It opens the way every operations document I have read in twenty years opens: scope, definitions, responsibilities, a revision history on page two with eleven entries going back to a first issue that is dated before I was born.

**§ 1–3** are the building. Environmental parameters. Plant maintenance. Accession and retrieval procedure. It's good. I'd hire whoever wrote it.

**§ 4 — Indicator Set.**

And here is where I stopped breathing properly, because I know this document. I have written this document. Everybody in my trade has written this document.

It's a list of indicators with acceptable ranges. Not temperature — the indicators aren't about the building. They're about the world outside it. About thirty of them, in categories, each with a definition, a source, a reporting interval and a **normal band**.

I'm not going to reproduce the list because I only had nine minutes and I will not invent the parts I didn't read. What I can tell you is the *shape* of the ones I did read, and the shape is unmistakable: they are measures of how much spare attention a population has. Not wealth. Not stability. **Slack.** Discretionary time. Discretionary money. The proportion of people whose next twelve months are not accounted for.

Each one has a band. Each band has an upper limit.

**§ 5 — Escalation.**

Thresholds. What happens when an indicator sits above its band for *n* reporting periods. Level one, level two, level three. Each level names a category of response and refers you to another section, the way a real manual does. *For sustained level two, see § 7.*

**§ 6 — Classification Defaults.**

Four pages. A schedule of *classes of observation not requiring escalation*, with a review date and a maintainer's initials.

I have read that document before. I read it at my own kitchen table in May, out of a config bundle Frank sent me because he was excited about it, in a folder called `defaults`, in a hundred and forty kilobytes of extremely dull.

*Monotonic drift, below threshold. Slow-onset accumulation. Seasonal re-baselining.*

Not the same words. The same three ideas, in the same order, as a **deliverable**, with a version number and a review date, in a manual on a shelf in Anatolia — and shipped, four years later, as the factory settings of the tools that watch a large fraction of everything.

Nobody hacked anything. Somebody was in the room on the day the defaults were agreed, and was no cleverer than anybody else in it, and sounded entirely reasonable.

**§ 7 — Population Phase.**

Two pages, and this is the one that made me put my hand on the shelf.

It is written in the flattest language in the entire volume. There is nothing cruel in it. There is no contempt in it anywhere — and I have read a great deal of contempt in my career and I know what it looks like on a page.

What § 7 describes is the maintenance of conditions.

Not repression. Not propaganda. **Load.** It talks about the maintenance of sufficient ordinary pressure — economic, temporal, attentional — such that the indicators in § 4 remain within band. It notes, in a sub-clause, with a reference to an annex I did not get to, that the objective is not deprivation; that deprivation is unstable and expensive and historically produces the opposite of the desired effect.

The objective is that people should be **occupied.**

Busy. Worried about something real and immediate and solvable. Frightened of things that are near. Wanting things that are purchasable. Tired at the correct times.

There is a sentence in there — I wrote it down within four hours and I would swear to the sense of it and not to every word — to the effect that *a population with unallocated attention will allocate it*, and that the historical record indicates it allocates it upward.

---

I want to stop the archive for a minute and tell you about my mother, because I understood § 7 standing at a shelf and I understood it through something that happened to me at twenty-three.

I was living abroad and I'd finished studying and my parents were stopping the money. I'd known the date for two years. It was September. And that summer I stopped sleeping and I rang my mother and told her, in the careful way you tell your mother, that I thought I might be depressed.

And she said two things that made me furious for about eleven years.

She said: *You're too poor to be depressed.*

And she said: *What you've got isn't depression. It's fear of life. You're scared.*

I have re-run that conversation more times than any other conversation in my life, and here is what I have arrived at, and it isn't that she was being cruel, because she wasn't; she was raised in a way that made both of those sentences simply descriptive.

She was right, and she was right in a way neither of us understood.

Not because there's no such thing. Because of what she was actually saying, which is: **you are about to be too busy for this.** And I was. By November I had a job I hated and rent I could barely make and a landlord and a bus timetable, and I did not have one single unallocated hour in which to ask myself what any of it was for, and I was — this is the horrible part — *fine.* Better than fine. I was fine for eleven years.

That's not a cure. Nothing was solved. Nothing was even looked at. What happened is that a large and frightening question got crowded out by a small and urgent one, and a small urgent problem is *survivable* in a way that a large open one is not, and human beings will take that trade every single time, and I took it for over a decade without ever knowing I'd been offered it.

You do not have to stop a man asking what happens when we die.

You have to make sure that on the evening he might have asked it, the boiler is broken.

That is § 7. That's all it is. Two pages of flat language about maintaining ordinary pressure so that the indicator in § 4 stays inside its band, and I stood in a rock chamber in Anatolia at eighteen degrees and forty-five per cent and thought about being twenty-three and being too poor to be depressed, and it is the only time in three years that I have wanted to sit down on the floor.

---

**§ 8 — Continuity of Function.**

Site provisioning, dependency schedules, and a subsection headed *authorisation dependency* that describes, in the same flat register, an arrangement in which the operation of certain systems — access, stores, environmental — is contingent on the presence and the continued biological function of an authorised principal.

I read it twice in the nine minutes because I did not believe I was reading it.

It's a dead man's switch. Not a metaphor, not an insurance clause. A hard dependency, so that the people who hold the doors and the guns cannot outlive their usefulness to the people who hold the keys, because the doors and the air and the stores are keyed to a pulse.

They have written down, in an operations manual, in a revision history that goes back before I was born, that they do not trust their own staff, and they have engineered around it, and the engineering is on the same shelf as the fire procedures.

**§ 9 — Baseline Restoration.**

Half a page and a table. Post-event conditions, and a projection in three columns.

I'm going to give you the only figure I am certain of, because I read it twice and because it is a number and numbers are what I keep.

The projection for **full baseline stability** — the state in which nobody alive remembers the previous configuration well enough to describe it — is given as **two generations.**

Fifty years. That's the number. That's the whole business model of the thing I had been chasing for three years: they do not have to convince anybody of anything, ever. They have to hold a position for fifty years, and then everybody who could contradict it is dead, and the record says what the record says, and the children of the children have no vocabulary for the question.

---

And the last thing, which is not a section, and which resolved a question I had left open for two years in a spreadsheet with the word *honest* next to it.

Slipped into the front of the volume, inside the cover — not bound in, just tucked there, the way anybody tucks a note into a manual — was a single sheet. A review note. Somebody's comments on Rev. 10, ahead of Rev. 11, in the ordinary language of a person doing a document review.

Four comments. Three of them are about numbering.

The fourth says — and this is the one sentence from that entire building that I would put my hand on a Bible about, because I read it four times in nine minutes and it is why I stopped being able to say *I don't know*:

> *§4 and §7 should not be presented as separate functions. We do not forecast the band and then maintain it; maintaining it is how it is forecast. Suggest merging at next revision.*

For two years I had written, in a file, that my data could not distinguish between a group that **predicts** how this goes and a group that **causes** it, and that anybody who claimed otherwise was selling something.

And there it is, in a reviewer's comment about document structure, in the mildest voice imaginable, from somebody who is not making an argument and does not think they are saying anything at all:

**A forecast you maintain is a cause.**

They stopped distinguishing years ago. It wasn't a decision. It was a formatting suggestion.

---

Dr Pekcan came back from the plant room and said, "The chiller is fine, it is always fine, he likes to tell me," and I said something, and we walked back up the ramp.

I signed out at ten past three. Time in, time out, in biro, in a hardbound book with ruled columns. She shook my hand and said I was welcome to come again and that next time I should propose a Wednesday, because Tuesdays she has the plant meeting.

I said thank you. I meant it.

Then I sat on a low wall next to a bicycle rack in the sun for about twenty minutes, and then I called a taxi, and in a hotel room in Şanlıurfa that evening I wrote for four hours by hand, and about halfway through I stopped and did the other thing — the small thing, the eleven-second thing, the one I have never described.

I did it differently that night, and it took about an hour, and what I put inside it was not a claim and not an accusation and not a story.

It was a document written from memory, and a set of diffs, and one other thing that I did not have yet and was going to have to go back for.
