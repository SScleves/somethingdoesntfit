# Nuremberg
### Dawn, 14 April 1561

The thing about a woodcut is that you cannot hedge.

Ink on paper from a carved block: every line is a decision that cannot be taken back, cut in reverse, in the dark of the wood, by a man holding a knife. You cannot write *approximately*. You cannot write *a sort of*. You cut a shape or you do not cut it, and whatever you cut is what four hundred years will see.

Hans Glaser is a printmaker in Nuremberg, which in 1561 is one of the great cities of Europe — free, rich, Lutheran, full of metalworkers and instrument-makers and mathematicians, the city of Dürer forty years dead and of the men who make the best clocks and the best globes on the continent.

Glaser makes broadsheets. Single large sheets, a picture and a block of text, printed cheap and sold in the street: a comet, a monstrous birth, a battle, a flood. It is the news, and it is the tabloid, and it is the only mass medium there is.

On the morning of the fourteenth of April he has a story that everybody in the city already has, because everybody in the city was outside looking at it.

---

Between four and five in the morning, at sunrise, a great many people in Nuremberg saw something in the sky and it went on for about an hour.

That is the part that isn't in dispute. Whatever happened, it was public, it was at dawn, it was seen by a large number of ordinary people simultaneously, and it lasted long enough for them to fetch each other.

What they described is where the difficulty starts, and the difficulty is not that the witnesses are lying. The difficulty is that they have no words.

They describe **globes**. A great many of them, blood-red and blue-black, in groups, three by three and four by four, sometimes alone.

They describe **rods** or **tubes** — long, thick, dark — and out of the ends of these tubes, more globes coming.

They describe **crosses**, blood-coloured.

They describe two great **pipes** in which the rods and globes went to and fro, and the whole business moving one against another as though it were a battle, and going on until it exhausted itself, and the shapes then falling from the sky toward the earth as if burning, and consuming themselves with great smoke.

And they describe, at the end, a thing like a **great black spear**, over the city, pointing east to west.

---

Now look at that vocabulary, because the vocabulary is the entire reason this survives.

*Globes. Rods. Tubes. Crosses. Pipes. A spear.*

Those are the words of people who work with their hands in a city full of workshops. They are not the words of a vision. Nobody says *angel*. Nobody says *demon*. Nobody in the text says *the Devil* — the interpretation, when Glaser gives it at the end, is a warning from God to a sinful people to repent, and it is the standard sign-off of every broadsheet ever printed and it is manifestly bolted on after the description.

The description itself is people reaching for the nearest object on the bench.

*It was shaped like the thing I use.*

That is what a witness does when they have no category. They do not invent a new word — inventing a word is difficult and looks mad. They point at the closest familiar shape, and the closest familiar shape in Nuremberg in 1561 is a globe, a rod, a tube, a pipe and a spear, because those are the things in the room.

---

Glaser cuts the block.

He has to make a great many decisions that nobody asked him to make. How many globes. How large. Where the crosses go. Whether the smoke rises or falls. He has interviewed people; he says so; the text is written as report — *many men and women saw*, and then what they saw.

And then, because he is a tradesman and not a philosopher, he does the two things a tradesman does. He puts the city at the bottom of the picture so you know where you are. And he sells it.

---

Five years later, in August 1566, over Basel, in Switzerland, another printer produces another broadsheet, and it describes black spheres in the sky at sunrise, in numbers, moving against one another, some becoming red and fiery and consuming themselves.

Different city. Different printer. Different confession, even — the politics of those two towns were not the same.

Same shapes. Same hour. Same vocabulary of ordinary objects.

---

There are explanations and they are respectable ones and this account is not going to argue with them.

The likeliest, by a distance, is atmospheric optics. Ice crystals high in the air do remarkable things at sunrise — sun dogs, halos, pillars, arcs, mock suns in rows. A cold April dawn over Central Europe is exactly the condition for it, and a person who has never been told what a parhelion is will describe one as a globe, and will describe a light pillar as a rod, and will describe a cross-halo as a cross, because those are the shapes they *are*.

That is very probably what happened, and it does not make the broadsheet less interesting. It makes it more.

Because either way — either way, both ways, whichever way — the same thing is true about it, and it is the only claim being made here:

**A large number of people saw something they had no category for, and they reported it in the vocabulary of the objects on their workbenches, and a tradesman cut it into a block of wood because it was worth a penny, and that is the only reason we have it.**

Nobody in that city had the word *ice crystal* or the word *refraction*, and nobody had the other words either. They had globes and rods and pipes, and they used them, and they did not stop to be embarrassed about it, and they did not agree amongst themselves to say nothing.

That is the whole of what a witness can ever do. Point at the nearest shape and say *like that*, and be wrong about what it was, and be right about what they saw.

The broadsheet is a piece of paper about the size of a tea towel. It has been sitting in a collection in Zürich for four hundred and sixty years. It cost about as much as a loaf.

*The sheet is in the Wickiana collection at the Zentralbibliothek Zürich. The Basel one of 1566 is catalogued beside it. Both have been photographed and both are online, and you can read the German if you are patient, and you should look at the globes.*
