# Chapter Twenty

The three pages came from Els Maasen in an envelope with a curtain-shop compliments slip in it, and a note in biro that said: *Found this behind the drawer lining. Not sending anything else. Please don't write again.*

She kept that. She has kept it since.

They're photocopies of photocopies — the kind that have been through the machine so many times the type has gone furry and the edges have gone black. Somebody photographed them, badly, at an angle, years ago. In the top corner of the first one you can see the shadow of a thumb.

There's no letterhead. No organisation, no address, no date except a line at the foot of each page in the small dead typeface of a document that expects to be superseded:

> *Preferred Formulations — fourth revision. For internal guidance. Not for circulation.*

Two columns. On the left, *deprecated*. On the right, *preferred*.

I'm going to give you six of them, because six is enough and because I have looked at these three pages more than I have looked at any other object in my life.

| deprecated | preferred |
|---|---|
| was brought / was given / was taught | emerged / developed / appeared |
| received from | held in common with |
| a tradition of instruction | a tradition |
| earlier than previously supposed | of uncertain antiquity |
| the origin of the practice is not known | the practice is of considerable age |
| there is no local antecedent | local antecedents are not preserved |

Read the right-hand column on its own. Not one word of it is false. There isn't a lie on that page. Every preferred formulation is a sentence a careful scholar could write in perfect conscience on a Tuesday afternoon, and several of them are *better English* than the thing they replace.

And the whole column does one job. Every entry takes a sentence with a hand in it and gives you back a sentence with weather in it.

---

But that's not the part that put me on the floor of my own kitchen.

The third page has a heading. It says:

> **§ 6. When a reader notices.**

And underneath it, seven numbered paragraphs of the calmest prose I have ever read, of which the first is:

> *6.1 The great majority of enquiries are satisfied by a courteous and prompt reply. Reply promptly. Confirm the difference. Supply the explanation before it is sought. Where an explanation is supplied by the enquirer, prefer theirs.*

*Prefer theirs.*

That's the one. That's the sentence that made me get up and walk around the room. Because I had had that done to me — four days after a dull, true, correctly-cited post on a bibliography forum, by an expert with a real name and a fifteen-year history, who agreed with me, and then improved my wording.

He hadn't been lying to me. He'd been on page three.

And here's the thing I need you to understand about this document, because it took me a fortnight to get there and it changed what I thought I was dealing with.

I had spent two years imagining a room. A small number of people in a room, deciding things. Everybody does; it's the only shape we've got for this. And then I sat on my kitchen floor with three furry photocopies and understood that there is no room. There's no meeting. There's no cabal, no oath, no candle.

There's a **house style**.

A style guide is not a plan. A plan is a thing you do once. A style guide is what you produce when the thing is being done *so often, by so many different people, most of whom have never met each other*, that you cannot afford to have them each decide individually how to phrase it. Nobody writes a style guide for one book. You write one for a *volume of work*.

It was in its fourth revision.

---

The coffee happened nine days later.

An email, in Dutch, then in English, from an address at a foundation I'd never heard of. Warm, brief, professional. He had seen that I had been in correspondence with a number of institutions about early twentieth-century printing variants. He was in the country the following Thursday. He wondered whether I would be free for a coffee near the station.

I said yes in about ninety seconds, and then spent nine days working out why I'd said yes so fast.

Two reasons. The honest one is that after two years of paper I would have walked into a burning building for a human being who knew what I was talking about.

The second is that I knew exactly what was happening. I want that stated plainly, because when this is over I don't want anybody thinking I was fooled. A man does not fly in to have coffee with a hobbyist. I knew I was being handled. I knew the friendliness was a technique before I'd finished reading the second paragraph, and I went anyway, because being handled is a two-way channel and I wanted my turn.

I got there twenty minutes early and sat where I could see the door, which is a thing I'd read about doing and had never once done.

---

He said his name was Bram. I'll call him that, because I have to call him something.

Sixty, maybe. Grey, neat, a raincoat he folded rather than hung. Ordinary shoes. He had the specific quality that certain older men in this country have of being absolutely unhurried in a railway station, and he shook my hand and said my name.

Both of it. First and last, unhurried, and correctly — including the spelling, which you cannot hear, except that I heard it, because he said the second syllable a fraction long the way you do when you have read a name more often than you have said it.

"James Klippe. Thank you for coming. Coffee? They do a terrible coffee here, I'm afraid, but the alternative is the place with the piano."

---

I'm going to give you most of this conversation, because it is the most important one I have ever had, and because I have thought about every sentence in it for a very long time.

He began by asking me about myself. Not work, not family. Something about me.

I heard it, and I laughed out loud, and it was the first genuinely enjoyable moment of the whole afternoon.

"That's my line," I said.

"Is it? It's a good line." He stirred his coffee. "It's very hard to be disliked afterwards."

"Where'd you get it?"

"Where does anybody get anything." He put the spoon down. "How is your list coming along?"

---

Nothing. He gave me nothing. I want that understood before I quote a word of it, because when you write down a conversation on a page it looks like an exchange of information and this was not that. In fifty minutes that man did not confirm one single fact. He asked me questions. When I put something to him he asked me what I thought of it. When I pushed he told me, pleasantly, that I was better placed to say than he was.

Here's the shape of it.

"Ten," I said. "Verified properly. Nine, if you're strict, and I'm strict."

"That's a great deal of work."

"It's about three years."

"And what do you think it is?" He said it as though he'd like to know. He may have. "Genuinely. Not what you'd write down. What do you think it is?"

"I think somebody has a preference about how the deep past gets described, and has had it for a long time, and acts on it in the places that get quoted and not in the places that don't."

"Mm," he said. "And why would anybody do that?"

"That's the part I can't do."

"Try."

So I tried, in a café, to a stranger. I said: because a population that thinks it was given something goes looking for who took it away. He listened to that the way a good doctor listens, and when I'd finished he turned his cup around on the saucer twice and said:

"That's better than most."

Then:

"May I put something to you? Not as an argument. As arithmetic."

---

"There was a period," he said, "and I'm not going to tell you when or where, when a group of people decided to do the opposite. To simply say everything, plainly, at once, because it was true, and because there is a certain kind of person who believes that true and good are the same word."

"And?"

"And you'd have to know how it went to have a view on it, and you don't, and I'm not going to tell you." He smiled slightly. "Which is unsatisfying, and you're entitled to disbelieve the whole story."

"You're aware that an argument I'm not allowed to check isn't an argument."

"I am entirely aware of that. I'd think less of you if you'd let it past." He turned the cup again. "I'll put it a different way, then, in a way you *can* check, because you've spent three years checking things and I'd rather not insult you.

"You work with systems. When a system fails, you'll agree there are two kinds of failure. There's the kind where a component wears out, and there's the kind where somebody did something.

"Now — the damage is identical. The building burns down either way, the money's gone either way, the people are just as dead. But the response is not identical. Not remotely. When a thing simply broke, people fix it. They work through the night, they help each other, and in a year they've rebuilt it better. When a thing was *done to them* —" he opened one hand, "— they stop fixing it, and they go and find who did it. And they are usually wrong about who did it. And that part takes a generation, and it costs more than the fire did."

He drank some of his terrible coffee.

"That's all. That's the entire arithmetic. Nobody has ever had to hide a catastrophe. You only have to be careful about the sentence that says somebody *chose* it."

And I sat in a station café and could not, on the spot, think of a single thing wrong with that.

I've thought of things since. I'll get to them. It took me two years and a room under a hill.

---

Then it turned, and it turned so gently I nearly missed the moment.

"You've built something," he said. "Haven't you."

I didn't say anything.

"That's not a question I need answered. I'd assume it of anyone competent." He looked at me over the cup, mild as a librarian. "The heartbeat. The thing where the silence does the work." A small motion of his hand, dismissive and almost affectionate. "Turn it off. It's a little embarrassing, and to be candid with you it's the only reason you and I are having coffee."

There it is. That's the sentence I'd trade a year of my life to have recorded.

He said it like a man mentioning that your fly is undone.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," he agreed. "Of course."

---

The offer took four minutes and it was not what I'd braced for.

"You're wasted," he said. "I mean that flatly, it isn't flattery, you're forty-four and you're very good at one specific thing and you're doing it for a company that will replace you with a licence fee in six years. You know that better than I do; you've read the configuration files."

"That's an unusual opening."

"There's work. It's dull and it's careful and it pays about double, and most of it is exactly what you did on your kitchen table at midnight, except with access, and a budget, and somebody who says thank you." He said it without any pressure at all. "I need a shadow. Somebody who reads the way you read. There is a shortage of people who look in the boring corner, Mr Klippe, and it is getting worse every year, and it turns out that we cannot generate them internally."

"You want the man who found you."

"I want the man who found us *and published the one that turned out to be a printing accident.*" And that, for one second, was the only time he was unguarded, because his voice changed a quarter of a tone. "Do you know how rare that is? You crossed out an entry in your own list. In public. Nobody does that. Nobody on either side does that."

"Then you'd have to let me go on looking."

"You'd have far more to look at than you have now."

"But not the books."

He put his cup down.

"No," he said kindly. "Not the books. Not that little collection of yours, the — Record, is it? Not that. You'd hand that over and, frankly, it would be a weight off you. No more of that business with the old printings." He smiled, and it was a real smile, warm, a man doing you a favour. "We know what's in most of them."

---

I want to describe what that did, because it wasn't fear.

It was the specific sensation of somebody reaching into your house and picking up the one thing on the shelf you'd have gone back into the fire for, and turning it over, and being *nice about it.*

Not the list. The Record. My own. Ten years of it, four hundred entries, nine-tenths of it about nothing, kept in a folder with a ten-year-old password, and nobody in the world has ever cared about it except me, and he knew what it was called.

---

The last part was about Ana and it was the ugliest thing that has ever been said to me in a pleasant voice.

"Talk to your wife about it," he said, putting his coat over his arm. "Genuinely. Take the weekend. She'll have a view."

"She doesn't know what I do."

"She knows what you're *like*," he said. "That's the part that matters and it's the part you keep underestimating. And you should understand what the alternative looks like from where she's sitting, because I think you've only ever imagined it from where you're sitting." He was doing his scarf. Ordinary movements. "There's nothing dramatic in it. Nobody is going to come to the house. What happens is that the work gets slower, and then it gets expensive, and then it stops being possible, and about four years into that a good woman is living with a man who has become quite difficult to be married to, and she will not be able to say exactly when it happened.

"We don't disappear people, Mr Klippe. That's an old-fashioned idea and it was always more trouble than it was worth. We hire them." He put out his hand. "Ask her which she'd prefer. That isn't a threat, it's genuinely the better question."

---

I said no. It took me a few seconds and it was not a good no. There was no speech. I've replayed it and what I actually said was:

"I don't think so. Thanks for the coffee."

He didn't seem surprised or disappointed. He seemed like a man whose train was in nine minutes.

"Well," he said. "Think about it for longer than that."

And then, at the door, in the tone of somebody making conversation on the way out, doing up a button:

"You know, it's a shame about Hendrik. Everyone found him difficult, but he had the best eye of his generation." He glanced back. "We've retired fourteen this year, and none of the others could have taught you anything."

Then he went to get his train.

---

I was sick in the station toilets. Not immediately. I stood on the concourse for about four minutes first, reading the departures board without reading it, and then I went downstairs and paid seventy cents and was sick, and washed my face, and looked at myself in a mirror that was scratched all over with initials, and thought, with total clarity:

*Retired.*

Not *lost*. Not *we lost fourteen*. He'd used a word with a person's whole life in it and he had not noticed himself do it, and it is the only unguarded thing anybody in this business has ever given me for free.

Fourteen in one year. Routine enough to have a word for. And the criterion, when I laid it against everything I had — Tresidder, the neurophysiologist, a Recanter who signed her own correction and lived to be ninety, the Quiet who noticed and stopped and were never touched at all —

The criterion isn't what a person believes.

It's whether they've written it down in a form somebody else could use.

Hendrik Maasen didn't die for a theory. He didn't have a theory; that's the whole point of him, that's why there's no thesis in that book. He died with forty ruled lines in his neatest handwriting, made checkable on purpose, left where a stranger would find them.

---

I drove home and Ana was making something and the radio was on and Mora came skidding into the hall.

She said, "How was your day?"

And I stood in the doorway of my own kitchen with my coat still on and understood that I had not done a did-you-know in nine weeks.

Not one. Not to her, not to Frank, not to the four men on the third tee. For twenty years I had been a machine that produced them, three or four a week, to fill silence and to be liked and to get out of conversations in under three minutes; it was the single most reliable thing about me; my wife bought me a hat about it every Christmas.

"Fine," I said. "Long."

She looked at me for a second too long, the way she does.

"You've gone quiet," she said. "Do you know that? Not tonight. Generally. You used to come home with something."

"Nothing's happening."

"Yeah," Ana said, and turned back to the pan. "That's what I mean."

---

I went up at half past ten. Buddy was already on the landing where he sleeps now, at the top, because he can still get up but the down is worse.

I did the thing I do before bed, which takes eleven seconds and which I have never once described. I did it standing in the bathroom with the door shut and the tap running, and then I put the phone back in the drawer under the spare toothbrushes and closed the drawer and got into bed next to my wife.

*Turn it off,* he'd said. *It's the only reason we're having coffee.*

I have never in my life been so glad to have wasted forty minutes.
