# Chapter Twelve

The market is on Sundays. That is the only reason the next thing that happened was a library and not a man with a beard, and I spent six days walking around with *he wanted to know if anyone ever came asking about him* going round in me like a stone in a washing machine.

The library thing was already booked. I'd requested the book in October.

---

Entry seventeen. *Highlands of Anatolia*, 1963, page 212. *Eleven thousand years* → *a much earlier period.*

Three known copies of the 1963 impression anywhere. One in a private collection that doesn't answer letters. One in a university in the United States that will photograph a page for you for sixty dollars and did, beautifully, promptly — and it turned out to be the 1971 reprint miscatalogued, which cost me two months and sixty dollars and taught me something I'll come back to.

And one in a national library, forty minutes away by train.

I'd filled in the form. I'd invented a research interest, which I felt bad about for approximately nine seconds. I had a reader's card with my name on it, spelled the way that form spells it, which is going to matter later and which I want you to notice now.

They wrote back and said the item was unavailable. So I went anyway, because you can't argue with an email.

---

The woman at the special collections desk was called Marijke and she was, I want to say this clearly, entirely on my side. That's the thing nobody tells you about closed doors. There's almost never a person behind them. There's a person in *front* of them who would love to help you and can't.

"Nineteen sixty-three," she said, reading the screen. "Yes. I'm sorry — that one's withdrawn."

"Withdrawn."

"For conservation." She turned the monitor a little so I could see it, which is a small kindness a lot of people wouldn't do. There it was, in a database field, in that flat institutional font: *NIET UITLEENBAAR — CONSERVERING 1994.*

"1994," I said.

"That's when it was flagged, yes."

"And it's been in conservation for thirty-one years?"

She laughed. She had a nice laugh; she'd clearly had this conversation before. "No. No, it means it was assessed as too fragile to serve, at some point, and then — honestly? Then nothing. Things sit. There's a queue. The queue is longer than my career."

"Can I see the queue?"

"You cannot see the queue," she said, "because the queue is a spreadsheet and it would break your heart."

---

I want to be fair about what happened next, because when I've told this story since I've noticed how easy it is to make her sound sinister, and she wasn't. She was good at her job and she gave me twenty minutes she didn't have.

"There's a scan," she said.

"There's a scan?"

"Everything in that class was scanned. Big programme, about eight years ago. Grant-funded — we could never have paid for it." She was typing. "Which is honestly the best thing that's happened to this collection in my lifetime. Two hundred thousand items. Anyone in the world, for free."

"Who funded it?"

"A foundation." She said it the way you'd say *the council*. "There's a plaque downstairs, I've walked past it four thousand times, I couldn't tell you the name."

I got the name later. I'm not going to give it to you yet, because I didn't have it yet, and because when I did get it I sat in a car park for a while.

"There," she said. "It's up. Do you want a terminal?"

---

The scan was of the 1971 reprint.

I need to slow down here because this is the only thing I got out of that day, and it took me forty minutes at a terminal to be sure of it, and then another two months to stop being sure of it, and then a year to be sure again.

The catalogue record says 1963. First impression, 1963, and the record is detailed — collation, binding, provenance, a note about the bookplate. The record is a good record made by someone who had the object in their hands.

The scan attached to that record is the 1971 text.

I could prove it from the screen without leaving my chair, which is the only reason I trust it. The 1971 reprint reset four pages of the front matter and moved the map; it has a publisher's device that the 1963 doesn't; and page 212 in the scan carries a running head that the 1963 collation in their own catalogue record says shouldn't be there.

And page 212 in the scan says *a much earlier period.*

So: the world's only publicly accessible copy of the 1963 first impression is a photograph of a different book, filed under the first impression's record, freely available to anyone on earth, for ever.

---

I went and found Marijke and I tried very hard to ask it in a way that wasn't mad.

"The scan attached to this record. Is there any way to check which physical copy it was made from?"

"It'll be in the digitisation log."

"Could I —"

"It's not public." She saw my face. "No, no — it's not secret, it's just not *public*, it's an internal workflow thing, it lives in a system I hate. I can look."

She looked. It took her four minutes and she made a face at the screen that I've thought about many times since.

"It says the source item is the shelfmark you asked for."

"But it isn't."

"You've clearly looked at it more closely than I have," she said, carefully. "Are you telling me it's the wrong edition?"

"I think the scan is 1971."

Marijke was quiet for a second. Then she said the most honest thing anybody said to me that entire year.

"Then somebody scanned the wrong copy. It happens. It happens more than we like. There were three of us running a project that size and there were student assistants and there were —" she stopped. "I'll flag it. I mean it, I'll flag it. It'll take about two years."

"Can I see the physical one? Just to compare. Ten minutes, gloves, in front of you, I don't care."

"It's withdrawn," she said, and she looked genuinely sorry about it. "I can't."

---

Then, at the door, walking me out, being kind, she said the thing that I did not hear properly for another six months, and that I would now give a year of my life to have heard on the day.

"The good news is the physical ones don't matter as much now. Once something's scanned it's safe. We've started deaccessioning some of the duplicate material — space is the thing that kills us, the stacks are full, and if it's digitised and it's not the only copy, there's an argument." She shrugged. "It upsets some people. I understand why. But it means we can accept new collections."

*Once something's scanned, it's safe.*

Everybody in that building believed that. She believed it and she was right, on every axis anybody had ever measured. Scanning genuinely is preservation. Access genuinely is a good. Every part of that programme was funded generously, done publicly, praised in the press, and celebrated by exactly the people who care most.

And at the end of it, the object with the ink on it goes in a skip, and what survives is a photograph that somebody chose.

I got that far in the tram and had to get off two stops early and stand in the cold for a bit.

---

Here's where the chapter turns and where my life turned with it, and it happened on a platform at half past four on a grey Tuesday afternoon.

I had been going about this the way you go about anything: gather more evidence.

And standing there I finally admitted the arithmetic, which is:

**There is no more evidence.**

Not somewhere else. Not later. Not with more effort. Entry seventeen has three copies and I cannot reach any of them; there are twenty-nine entries left and every one of them will have its own version of a locked door with a nice woman in front of it. I am a man with a job and a mortgage and no institution behind me, and doors do not open for people like me, and they are not supposed to, and honestly they *shouldn't* — the whole reason those doors exist is to keep out people exactly as plausible-sounding as me.

So if you can't get more evidence, and you can't get through the door, what do you actually have?

And the answer arrived with the tram, and it arrived in my own professional vocabulary, which is how I know it was mine:

*You don't have more evidence. But you can make the system respond.*

That's my whole job. That's the only thing I have ever been good at. You cannot see inside most systems — they don't let you, they're not built for it, the logs are lies and the documentation is a work of fiction. So you don't ask what it *is*. You give it something and watch what it does. You learn what a thing is by what it reacts to.

I stood on that platform and thought: *nobody in any of this has ever tried that. Not the professor, not the man with the older ink. They collected. For fifty years, between them, they collected.*

I was going to poke it.

---

I built it that night and I built it properly, and it is the single cleverest thing I did in the whole business, and it is also the thing that ended my life as I knew it, and both of those are true at once.

Three posts. Three different forums, three different accounts, three different registers, all within nine days of each other.

**Post one — true.** Entry three. The ancient-engineering paperback, first printing against fourth, *techniques we have not yet reconstructed* → *doubtless simpler than they appear.* Stated flatly, with page numbers and printing statements and two photographs, no theory attached, no adjectives. A dull man reporting a dull fact. Posted in a bibliography forum where dull is the house style.

**Post two — plausible and false.** An edit I invented. Same grammar, same shape, same direction, in a real 1958 book I own two copies of, describing a change that does not exist and never has. Same tone, same photographs — except the photographs are of a page where nothing happened, and nobody who hasn't got both books can tell. This one is the control. This one is the steel table.

**Post three — true, in crank costume.** Entry three again. Identical facts. Capital letters, three exclamation marks, a sentence about *what they don't want you to know*, one deliberately wrong date, and a closing line about how the truth always comes out. Posted where that lives.

And then I did the thing that makes it an experiment instead of a stunt, which is that I wrote down what each outcome would mean **before** any of them happened. I've still got the file. I'll give you the four lines:

*If nothing is touched: I have a hobby and a good imagination.*
*If the crank post gets attention and the others don't: the world is normal, and loudness attracts noise.*
*If the false post gets a correction: my method is worthless, because they are correcting things that aren't there.*
*If the TRUE post — the dull one — is touched, and the other two are not: then something is reading for content, not for volume.*

Then I sat there for a while with the cursor blinking, because I am not, whatever else I have made myself sound like by now, a brave man.

I thought about Ana asleep upstairs. I thought about the professor sitting on an upturned crate in the rain, asking a bookseller whether anyone had ever come asking about him.

And I thought: *these are anonymous accounts on three websites. What's the worst that happens.*

I want that sentence preserved exactly. I'd like it on the stone.

Then I clicked post three times and went to bed, and slept extremely well, feeling clever.
