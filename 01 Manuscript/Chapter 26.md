# Chapter Twenty-Six

I want to do the strategy first, because it's four paragraphs and then we can get to the kitchen.

**Exposure does not work and I have the receipts.** Not as an opinion — as a tested result. I published one dull true thing correctly and got a polite correction inside four days. I published a *retraction* of my own worst mistake, honestly, with the erratum photographed, and got eleven views. And when they finally wanted me gone they did not delete a word of mine; they added three paragraphs to twelve years of small true facts and let the mask do the rest. The mask is not something they put on me. It's something I'd been wearing for two decades, at tables, for laughs, and they simply stopped taking it off.

So: no newspaper. No documentary. No thread. Anyone who tells you the answer here is *get it out there* has not run the experiment, and I have, twice, and it cost me a persona and a friend.

**What's left is arithmetic.** I cannot beat a hundred people, or a thousand, or however many there are; I don't even know the order of magnitude and I never have. But I have spent twenty years inside organisations and there is one thing I know about all of them, without exception, and it is this: **an organisation is not a mind. It's a very large number of people, each of whom is doing a job, most of whom have never met, and almost none of whom have read the whole document.**

Two tiers. That's what everything in three years says. There are people who know what this is, and there are a great many more people who maintain it — who file, who scan, who guard, who catalogue, who write the transfer schedules and drive the vans — and who have inherited a *role*, not a secret, and who are, by every indication I have, decent.

You cannot compromise an organisation. You can compromise **one person in it**, and if the one you compromise is the right one, the other ninety-nine have to sit in a room and listen to him.

**And there is exactly one thing they have never once defended against.**

They're excellent against theft — climate control, accession numbers, a good specification. They are *superb* against exposure; they've had a century's practice and a style guide in its fourth revision. They have a whole section, § 6, on what to do when a reader notices, and it is the best-written page in the document.

There is no section on what to do when somebody writes them a letter, on paper, with his real name on it, citing their own published catalogue, and asks politely to be shown one object.

Because nobody ever has. Why would they? Everybody who finds out what that place is either wants to steal from it or tell the world about it, and both of those are handled.

*Ask.* One word, in shaking ink, at the bottom of a page, added later by a man who spent forty years being right in private and died before he did it.

It isn't a clue. It was never a clue. It's a note-to-self from a man who had finally understood, at sixty-nine, with a bad heart, what he had failed to do for four decades.

---

Ana came over on the Saturday. Her mother's, an hour and ten in the car, and she came at eleven in the morning which meant she'd decided something in advance.

Mora came in like a cannonball and did nine laps of the ground floor and then found Buddy on his mats and stood over him and licked his entire face for about forty seconds while he sat there enduring it with his eyes shut like a man at the barber.

Ana watched that from the doorway with her coat still on.

"He's thinner," she said.

"He's better than he was. There's a whole regime now. There's a *stand* for the water bowl."

"You bought a stand."

"I built a stand," I said, "badly," and she laughed, and something in the house came off the wall a bit.

---

We did it at the kitchen table and it took four hours and I want to give you the shape of it rather than all of it, because four hours is four hours.

I started at the beginning. Not the funeral — the *actual* beginning, which is a boy of ten reading something in a book and being told by every adult in the room that he'd invented it.

Then the tabs. Then the folder called Reading. Then the crate at the market and the twelfth book, free, because twelve sounds better. Then page 118 and the blemish and two identical typos. Then the handbook I'd bought for thirty-one euros to test myself on, and the fact that I'd found two out of four and hallucinated a fifth, and what I'd done about it.

She stopped me there. First stop, an hour in.

"Wait. Go back. You did an experiment to check whether you were any good at this."

"Yes."

"And you were bad at it."

"I was terrible at it."

"And then what?"

"Then I wrote a procedure and did it again until I was good at it."

Ana sat back and looked at me for a second and said, "Okay," and I said "what," and she said, "Nothing. Keep going. That's just very you and I hadn't heard it out loud before."

---

She stopped me eleven or twelve times. I've never in my life been listened to like that, and I've been married to her for fifteen years, which tells you something about me and not about her.

She did not ask *is it true* once. Not once, in four hours.

What she asked was: who else knows. What did the daughter's face do when you said your father's name. Why did you not tell me in March. What exactly did the man at the station say about me, word for word, say it again. Does Frank know. Is Frank in it. Does Noor have children.

And, twice, about the dog.

At the point where I got to the barbecue she got up and walked to the window and stood there with her back to me for a while, and I stopped talking, and after a bit she said:

"He asked me about my grandmother."

"I know."

"He sat on that step and asked me what programmes she watched, and I told him about the blanket." Her voice was perfectly level. "I've told about four people in my life about the blanket."

"Ana —"

"Was that the job? Is that *work*, for him? Is that something they're taught?"

"I don't know."

"I want you to know that I liked him more than I like most of my friends," she said, still at the window. "I want that said out loud in this kitchen. Because otherwise in about six months I'm going to start pretending I always thought there was something off about him, and I didn't. I thought he was great."

---

Then the flashes, and the kitchen floor in August, and the corridor.

That took the longest and it was the worst of it, and she did not touch me while I did it, which was exactly right, and she made two cups of tea in the middle of it that neither of us drank.

At the end she said:

"Do you believe it?"

Which is the correct question and the only one anybody has ever asked me that I couldn't dodge.

"I believe I experienced it," I said. "I don't know what it was. There are two explanations and one of them is that I'm a man who sat in the dark for five hours after six weeks alone, and I can't get rid of that one, and I've stopped trying to."

"But you know what you think."

"I know what I think," I said. "I'd just never write it down."

Ana nodded slowly.

And then she said the thing that I have thought about more than any other sentence in this book, including all the ones on page 118.

"Okay. So — I don't believe it." She said it gently. "I want to be honest with you because we've had about two years of not being. I don't believe there's a — a wheel, or lives, or that you saw anything real in this kitchen. I think you had an experience and I think you're the most honest person I know about what you don't know, and I still don't believe it."

"Right."

"But I believe *you*," she said. "Those are different and I need you to hear the difference, because I think you've spent your whole life not knowing there was one." She put her hands flat on the table. "I don't have to think you're right. I've never had to think you're right. I have to think you're *not lying and not unwell*, and I've never once had to work at that, not in fifteen years, not now."

I didn't say anything for a while.

"That's the whole thing, isn't it," she said. "That's what you've been doing since you were ten. Trying to get somebody to say the first one, when what you actually needed was the second one."

---

At about four she asked what I was going to do, and I told her, and this is where it stops being a marriage conversation and becomes the plan.

"There's a building," I said. "It's a conservation depot in southeastern Turkey. It's four years old — well, it was four years old in 2017 — it has a very good climate-control specification and no publication record at all, and forty crates of a broken-up special collection went there in 2017 along with material from at least eight other institutions I've now traced."

"And that's where the books are."

"That's where I think the *originals* are. First impressions. Withdrawn papers. Every physical copy that got deaccessioned in fifty years of generous digitisation programmes, all of it beautifully looked after by people who love records, in a room with a good specification, on public money, with a car park."

Ana thought about that.

"That's not a hiding place," she said. "That's a library."

"That's the entire point. It's the best hiding place there is, because it isn't one. Nothing's stolen. Nothing's destroyed. Everything is preserved, properly, by professionals, and the only thing that ever happened is that it stopped being where anyone would look for it."

"So you're going to break in."

"No."

"James —"

"No, listen. That's what I'd have said a year ago and it's wrong, and it's wrong for the same reason everything else I tried was wrong." I turned the transfer schedule round on the table so she could see the annex, six-point type, page two hundred and eleven of a public annual report. "They are defended against theft and they are defended, brilliantly, against publicity. Neither has ever been tried on them successfully and both have been tried.

"Nobody has ever asked."

"Asked what?"

"To come and see something. Politely. With my name on it, on paper, citing an accession number out of their own published catalogue, the way any researcher in the world writes to any depot in the world on a Tuesday." I sat back. "And they'll say yes. That's the part I'm sure of. They'll say yes because saying no is the only answer that would confirm anything, and they have spent a hundred years never confirming anything."

Ana looked at the annex for a long moment.

"And when you're in there?"

"Then I'm one man in a room with the people who maintain it," I said. "Not the ones who decide. The ones who maintain it. And I'm going to tell them something about themselves that I'm fairly sure nobody has ever told them."

---

She asked the right question then, of course, because she always does.

"Why?" Ana said. "Not why the plan. Why any of it. You're forty-four, you've lost a year and a half of your life, you've lost your — whatever that was, with Noor. You've lost me for five weeks and you nearly lost me properly. So what is it *for*, James? Because if the answer is that somebody has to know the truth about the flood, I'm going to be honest with you, I don't care, and I don't think you do either."

And I gave her the true answer, which I had not planned and had never said out loud.

"Because everybody at your grandmother's funeral told you that you'd see her again," I said, "and I said yes, and I hadn't checked. And it turns out I've spent three years finding out that a great deal of trouble has been taken, over a very long time, to make sure that nobody can ever check anything about that. Not the answer — the *checking*. That's the thing that keeps getting removed. Every single edit takes out a connection and leaves the weather.

"So I don't know if you'll see her again. I still don't. I'm probably never going to.

"But I'd like to live in a world where a man can stand at a graveside and say *I don't know* out loud, and go and look, and not have to become somebody's project to do it."

Ana was quiet for a bit.

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me," she said, "and it took four hours and a dead grandmother to get there, and I'd like it noted that I had to ask."

---

She stayed. Not moved back — stayed, that night, which is different and we both knew it and neither of us said it.

At about eleven she was in the doorway of the kitchen with the light behind her and she said:

"Is there anything else? Anything you haven't told me?"

And I said, "No."

There was one thing. There has always been one thing. Forty minutes at four in the morning, three years earlier, in a dressing gown, feeling absolutely ridiculous.

I'm not going to tell you why I didn't tell her, because I've thought about it for a long time and there are three reasons and only one of them is decent, and I can't separate them.

What I'll say is that it was the fourth time in this book that I have lied to my wife to protect her, and it is the only one of the four where I still think, sitting here now, that I was right.
