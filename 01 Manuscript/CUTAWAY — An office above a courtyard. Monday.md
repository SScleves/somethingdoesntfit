# An office above a courtyard
### Monday

The office had been a bedroom until about 1970 and still had the fireplace, and Ruys had never got round to having anything done about the wallpaper, which was a pale stripe and had been there when he arrived.

He took the call at four minutes past nine, which was late, and he let that be the first thing he said.

"You were to ring me at eight."

"I was driving."

"You were to ring me at eight."

A pause on the line, the sound of a man in a car deciding not to say the second thing.

"He knows," Kalder said.

Ruys turned a pen over on the blotter. Below the window somebody was moving bins in the courtyard, and would be for another four minutes; it happened every Monday and he had stopped hearing it years ago and now heard it only when he was displeased.

"He knows," he repeated.

"He gave me a wrong town to see what I'd do with it and I took it."

"You took it."

"Correcting him would have been worse."

"Both were worse," said Ruys. "That is generally the position one is in by then." He wrote a short word on the blotter and looked at it. "How certain is he?"

"He's certain. He was pleasant about it for ten minutes and shook my hand, so he'll be certain and quiet, which is the difficult one."

"And you gave him the boiler."

"I gave him the boiler."

"Mm," said Ruys. He was not, on the whole, opposed to the boiler. It had the advantage of meaning nothing to anybody who overheard it, and it had the disadvantage of being memorable, and there was a version of this in which a man wrote it down. "The wife heard it."

"The wife thought it was morbid. She's left him, incidentally — Tuesday, to her mother's. She took the young dog."

"That is not an improvement," said Ruys. "You understand that is not an improvement."

"No."

"A man with a wife has a great deal to lose on a Tuesday afternoon. A man on his own with an old dog has considerably less, and it is quite remarkable how few people in this business have ever noticed that." He put the pen down. "How is the dog?"

"The old one won't come near me."

"Yes," said Ruys, without much interest. "They do that."

---

He let the silence run for a while, because it cost him nothing and it did something to Kalder that four sentences would not.

Then: "The name he gave you in March. The conservator."

"Haverkamp."

"She is now the more useful of the two and you have spent five months on a golf course." Ruys pulled a folder across the desk, opened it, did not look at it. "He can be managed. He has been offered a job, he has been offered his wife back in so many words, and he has been asked to switch off a rather touching little contrivance which he has not switched off, and none of that has moved him, which tells us what he is. He will go on being a man with a list until something takes the list off him or he dies of ordinary causes, and I am content to wait for either."

"And her?"

"She has hands," said Ruys. "That is the whole difficulty. He can only tell people. She can *show* them, on a table, with a lamp, in about forty seconds, and there is no answer to that in the guidance and there never has been." He closed the folder. "Go and be extremely polite to her."

"How polite."

"As polite as it takes. She has a business, a lease, an accreditation, and nine clients who could all be written to. She is not a person one needs to touch." A dry sound that was almost amusement. "In my experience the conservators are the easiest of the lot. They have spent their whole lives being careful with things and it does not occur to them that anybody would be careless with them."

Below the window the bins stopped.

"And Klippe?"

"Leave him entirely alone," said Ruys. "That is not generosity, it is arithmetic. He has now had a fright and he has no one to tell, and a frightened man with no audience does the work for us for about a year." He looked at the word he had written on the blotter and crossed it out. "Ring me at eight."

He hung up, and sat for a moment, and then wrote three lines in the folder in a small careful hand, and moved it from the left-hand stack to the right-hand stack, which he had been doing on Mondays for nineteen years and which meant nothing to anybody but him and the two women downstairs who did the filing.

Then he went to make coffee, because the machine was free at ten past nine and never after.
