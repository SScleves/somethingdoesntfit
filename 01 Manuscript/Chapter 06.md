# Chapter Six

The publisher took four evenings and got me nowhere I could use, so I'll be quick about it.

*Waters Before Memory* was printed for its first impression in London by a house that no longer exists, for a firm that no longer exists, and its second impression in 1931 by a small printing house in Leiden. That's an hour on the internet and a very good afternoon in a university library where nobody asked me who I was, because nobody ever does. Leiden isn't strange, by the way. Half the awkward English academic books of that decade got their continental runs done in the Netherlands: cheaper, faster, and the compositors could set Greek without weeping.

The Leiden house was absorbed in 1954 by a bigger house, which was absorbed in 1978 by a group, which is now a name on a floor of an office building in a business park with a lawn. I wrote to them. A very nice person in communications wrote back within a day, which is how you know there's nothing there, and said the pre-war records were lost in the war.

I sat with that for a while.

Because it's true. It is almost certainly true. Records were lost in the war, in that country, in enormous quantities, and if you're going to be the man who finds it sinister that Dutch paperwork from before 1945 is missing, then you're going to be busy and you're going to be wrong.

So there's my first lab coat that wouldn't fit anybody. The explanation is boring, plausible, and complete. It just also happens to be the single most convenient sentence in the world, and I couldn't make it feel like either of those things exclusively.

I wrote in the file: *dead end, and honestly dead. Not suspicious. Just dead.*

Then I stopped, because I'd noticed something worse.

---

Here it is, and it's embarrassing.

I had been comparing books for ten years. Hundreds of them. I had a folder full of photographs and measurements and a taxonomy with a dropdown menu in it. I had, as of that month, one finding I actually believed in.

And I had never once run the thing on a book where I already knew the answer.

Not once. In ten years. Every comparison I'd ever done was against an unknown — that was the whole appeal, that's the hunt. Which means I had a method, a confidence, and a reputation with myself, all built on results that nobody had ever marked.

At work I would have found that funny. At work I have said, out loud, in a meeting, to a room of people who did not enjoy it: *how do you know your detection works? Have you ever fed it a problem you built yourself?* It is the single most useful question I know. It's not clever. It's the first question. And I'd never asked it about the one thing in my life I actually cared about.

So I went and bought a marked book.

---

It took a fortnight and thirty-one euros. What I wanted was a title that came with its own changes declared: a second impression that says, in print, on the copyright page, *corrected*, ideally with a publisher decent enough to have listed what they corrected, or a later scholar tidy enough to have listed it for them.

I found one. A 1950s handbook, dull as a bus timetable, second impression 1961, *with corrections*, and — this was the gift — a review in a journal from 1962 by a man who had evidently made it his business to check, and who listed all four.

Four changes. A date. A misattributed quotation. A dropped negative that reversed a sentence, which is the good one. And a name spelled two ways, standardised.

I didn't read the review. I printed it, folded it, and put it in a drawer in the hall.

Then I sat down with the two copies and did my thing, exactly the way I'd always done it, with nobody watching and no way to fool myself, to find out what I actually am.

---

I found two.

The date. And the name.

I missed the misattributed quotation completely — it's on page 88, it's four words inside a longer quotation I'd have had no reason to distrust, and I ran my eye over it twice.

I missed the dropped negative. The dropped negative. The one that turns *cannot* into *can*, the one that changes the meaning of the paragraph, the one that is the entire reason anyone ever checks anything. It is on page 140. I read page 140 in both books and did not see it, and when I finally went back with the list in my hand and found it, I sat there and looked at it for a long time and felt something cold move through me, because I knew *exactly* how I'd missed it. Same way I'd missed page 118 three times. First few words, last few words, length about right, done.

And then the part I've thought about more than the misses.

I found a fifth.

I was certain about it. Page 203, a hyphen situation, a compound that I'd have sworn was set solid in one and broken in the other, and I had it circled and photographed and typed into the file as *5 (unlisted)* before I went back over it in daylight and discovered that both copies were identical and I had been looking at the same book twice, having got up to let a dog out and put the wrong copy back on the left.

Two out of four. And one hallucination.

Fifty per cent, and a liar.

---

I'd like to tell you I took that well.

What I actually did was go for a walk at eleven at night in the rain with an old dog who did not want to go, and come back, and sit in the kitchen, and consider, seriously, for about an hour, throwing the whole thing away. Not the book. The hobby. Ten years of it. Because if the instrument is this bad, then every entry in the Record is a coin toss with good handwriting, and the man who wrote them is precisely the kind of person he spends his life laughing at.

Buddy came and lay against my foot. He does that. He can't do anything about anything and he's never once let that stop him.

And somewhere in there it turned over, the way things do when you've been beaten enough to stop defending yourself, and I thought: *fine. Then fix the instrument.*

Because here's the thing I'd missed while I was busy being ashamed. The control had worked. Not the way I wanted, but it had *worked* — that's what a control is for. Four hours and thirty-one euros had told me the truth about my own reliability, which is more than a decade of triumphs had managed. If I'd never bought that stupid handbook I'd have gone on being a man who was right about everything, at fifty per cent.

So I sat down and wrote a procedure. It's not sophisticated. I'll give it to you as I wrote it, because it matters later that this is all it ever was:

1. Photograph both pages *before reading them.* You get one honest look at a page and I'd been spending it on skimming.
2. Both books flat, side by side, one finger on each line, and the finger moves at the speed of the finger, not the speed of the eye.
3. Read the **middle** first. Then the ends. Always. My eyes go to the beginning and the end and glide the middle; so, presumably, does everyone's; so, presumably, does the person who chooses where to put things.
4. When you find something, stop, and photograph it before you get excited. Excitement edits memory in about four seconds. I've watched it do it.
5. Never compare a book with itself. Mark the copies. Tape on the boards, A and B, like an idiot, because idiots don't make that mistake twice.
6. Write down what you expect to find *before* you look.

Then I ran the handbook again, from the start, four evenings, with the review still folded in the drawer.

Four out of four. No fifths.

---

And then, on a Tuesday, because apparently everything in my life happens on Tuesdays, I went back to page 118 with the better instrument and no confidence at all, half hoping it would dissolve.

It didn't.

If anything it got worse, and here's what I mean by worse. Under the new procedure I photograph before I read, and when you photograph both 118s and lay them next to each other on a screen, blown up, you can see the thing I'd been feeling and couldn't say: the replacement is *the same length.* Not approximately. The line breaks don't move. The paragraph doesn't reflow onto the next page. Whoever did it fitted the new words into the exact space of the old ones, which is more work than changing the words. Considerably more.

You don't do that by accident, and you don't do it in a hurry, and — this is the part that sat me down — you don't do it unless you care whether it's noticed.

I put that in the file in the flattest words I could find, because I'd learned my lesson about my own excitement, and flat is all I trust now.

*The change was fitted. Fitting takes effort. Effort implies preference. Somebody preferred that nobody notice.*

---

I should tell you about the other thing that happened that month, because it starts here and it goes on for the rest of the book, and if I leave it out you'll be reading a different story than the one I lived.

I took a psychedelic. Once. Alone, in the house, on a Saturday when Ana was at her mother's, with the dogs, with a notebook, at a measured dose I'd spent two weeks reading about, having decided in the same spirit I decide everything: there is an instrument available for this question, it is cheap, it is old, and I have never picked it up because of what people would think of me.

I'm not going to make it beautiful. Most of it was uncomfortable and some of it was tedious and at one point I was extremely worried about the dog, who was asleep.

What I'll report is the one thing that survived the next morning, which is my only test for any of it.

It was not a revelation. Nobody told me anything. There was no tunnel, no relative, no light — I'd read enough by then to know exactly what I'd been primed to produce, and I produced none of it, which is either evidence or the world's least useful anticlimax.

What there was, was a change in the *aperture.* For a while — an hour, maybe less — it was as if something that is normally almost closed was open by a few degrees, and through it I could see that there was a very great deal on the other side, and that it had been there the whole time, and that under ordinary conditions I have precisely no access to it and never had.

A window. Not a door. I could see; I couldn't go. And when it shut I was a man on a sofa with a dry mouth and two dogs and a notebook in which I had written, at some point, in handwriting I barely recognised, the single word *ordinary*, underlined twice, and I have never been able to reconstruct what I meant.

I logged it. Dose, time, duration, conditions, findings: *aperture change, no content, no crossing.* And I noted, honestly, the obvious objection, which is that a man who has just started asking what happens when you die is not a neutral observer of his own chemistry.

Then I put the notebook in the folder called Reading and didn't do it again for months.

---

Two nights later I finally opened the thin book with no title on the spine.

I'd like to be able to say why that night. I think the truth is that I'd run out of things to check, and a man who has run out of things to check will eventually open the one item he's been saving.

It wasn't a book. I mean it was a book — cloth, sewn, two hundred blank-ish pages, the sort of thing you'd buy to keep accounts in — but nobody had printed anything in it. It was handwritten. Two hands, and I could tell that at a glance without knowing anything: an older ink, brown-going-to-nothing, in a small tight upright hand, and over and around and after it a second hand in blue ballpoint, looser, faster, later.

And it was a list.

Not prose. Not notes. A list, in columns, ruled by hand with something that wasn't a ruler, running for forty-odd pages and then stopping.

Every line the same shape.

A book. A page number. Two printings. And then two phrases, one arrow between them.

I turned to the eleventh line, because by then my hands knew before I did, and there it was, in the older ink, in someone else's hand, decades before I was born:

*Waters Before Memory,* 1926. p. 118. 1st → 2nd (1931).
*transmitted to the early* → *arisen among the first.*

And in the margin, in the blue ballpoint, in the professor's hand:

*see 17. see 38.*
