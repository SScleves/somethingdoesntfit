# Chapter Seven

I read the whole list twice that night without understanding a single thing about it, which is what you'd expect, because a list is not an argument and this one had gone to some trouble not to be.

Let me describe it properly, because the shape of it is the point.

Forty-one lines. Each line the same: a source, a page, two printings with an arrow between them, and then the before and the after. Occasionally a year in brackets. Occasionally a word in the margin. That's all. No introduction. No conclusion. Nothing on the first page saying what this was for, and nothing on the last page saying what it meant.

I have spent my working life reading other people's documentation and I can tell you that this is not how anybody writes anything. People can't help themselves. Every log file in the world has a human sentence in it somewhere — a swear word in a comment, a note that says *this is a horrible hack, sorry.* Give a man forty pages and he will tell you what he thinks.

This one didn't. For forty pages, two men wrote down what a printed page used to say and what it says now, and neither of them once wrote down why they cared.

I have two readings of that and I've never settled between them.

One: they were disciplined. They understood that the moment you write the conclusion, the list becomes an argument, and an argument can be dismissed. A list can only be checked.

Two: they were frightened. You don't write it down if writing it down is the thing that gets you.

Probably both. It's usually both.

---

The two hands were easy and the third took me a week to see.

The older ink is small, upright, tight, the handwriting of a man taught before the war to write in a way that saved paper. It does about the first fourteen entries. Then it stops, mid-page, with room left underneath — which is its own small horror, if you think about it, because nobody plans to stop in the middle of a page.

The second hand is the professor's. I knew it immediately; I'd been reading it in margins for a month. Blue ballpoint, faster, looser, and it doesn't start fresh — it starts *underneath*, respectfully, continuing the ruling, keeping the columns, matching a dead man's format for another twenty-six entries across what must have been decades, because the ink changes four times and the hand gets worse.

And then the third thing. Not a hand. A word.

At the very bottom of the last page, under the final unfinished entry, in a ballpoint that isn't quite the same blue, in letters that shake:

*Ask.*

That's it. One word. I looked at it under a lamp for a long time and the only thing I could establish is that it was written later than everything above it, by a hand that had got worse still, and that it is not a note about a book.

---

Entry eleven is Tresidder. I've told you that.

What I haven't told you is what it did to me to see it, because I want to be honest about the sequence: I did not feel vindicated. I felt caught.

For six weeks I had been a man alone in a kitchen with a private, ridiculous, faintly shameful idea. Then I opened a book I'd bought for almost nothing from a man with a beard, and found my idea in it, written down before I was born, by somebody I will never meet, as line eleven of forty-one.

That's not vindication. That's discovering the room you thought you were alone in has been furnished.

The margin next to it said *see 17. see 38.*

I looked at 17 and 38 and understood neither, so I did what you're supposed to do, which is start with the cheapest thing on the list.

---

Entry three cost me eleven euros and two days of waiting for the post.

*Ancient engineering*, a popular paperback from the seventies, the kind with a painting of a ziggurat on the cover, page 44, first printing against fourth printing.

*techniques we have not yet reconstructed* → *techniques that were doubtless simpler than they appear.*

I want you to notice how unimpressive that is, because I need you to have the experience I had. It is not a smoking gun. It's a popular paperback softening a line between printings, which is the most ordinary thing in publishing; authors do it, editors do it, everybody does it, and nine times out of ten it's because somebody wrote a letter to the publisher pointing out that the first version was overstated. That is genuinely the likeliest explanation and I put it in the file in those words before the books arrived.

Then I ran the procedure. Photographs first. Both flat. Tape on the boards, A and B. One finger per line. Middle before ends. And I wrote down what I expected to find, which was: *the change, plus other changes, because a fourth printing of a popular book will have accumulated corrections.*

The change was there.

There were no other changes.

Two hundred and nine pages, first printing against fourth, and one alteration in the entire book — a clause on page 44 that stopped saying we couldn't reconstruct something and started saying it was probably simpler than it looked. Everything else identical. Including, on page 61, *Nebuchadnezzar* spelled two different ways in the same paragraph, in both printings, in a book that had been through four runs and, presumably, at least one person who could spell.

I sat back and did not feel clever. What I felt was the specific unpleasant sensation of a thing getting bigger while you're holding it.

Because this was not Tresidder. Different country, different decade, different publisher, different subject, different everything. A 1926 monograph on flood myths and a 1970s paperback about how they cut the stones.

Same move.

---

Here's the part I wrestled with for a week, and I want to show the wrestling rather than the conclusion, because the wrestling is the only thing that makes the conclusion worth anything.

Two entries is not a pattern. Two of anything is not a pattern. Two is a coincidence with ambitions.

And I had a bias I could name, which is the worst kind, because naming it does absolutely nothing to remove it: I now owned a list of forty-one of these, written by two men who were sure enough to spend their lives on it, and I had verified one of them, and the human animal that lives in my chest wanted very badly to believe the other thirty-nine.

So I made a rule, and I've kept it, and it's cost me. I don't get to count an entry until I've held both printings in my hands and run the procedure. Not until I've read about it. Not until it's plausible. Held. Both. Run.

By that rule, at the end of that month, I had exactly two.

But there was something else, and I couldn't make it go away by being disciplined at it, because it wasn't about how many. It was about which way they pointed.

*Transmitted to* becomes *arisen among.* Somebody handed it over becomes it came up by itself.

*We have not yet reconstructed* becomes *doubtless simpler than they appear.* We don't know how they did it becomes it wasn't that impressive.

Two edits, from two centuries and two continents and two entirely unrelated industries, and both of them travel in the same direction: away from *somebody gave us this*, toward *nothing was given.*

I sat with that and then I wrote the most careful sentence I have ever written in that file, and I'd defend it now:

*Two data points establish a direction the way two dots establish a line: perfectly, and meaninglessly. Get more dots.*

---

Ana came into the kitchen the next evening while I had the list open, and I didn't close it, and I've wondered since whether that was courage or the beginning of something worse.

"What is that?"

"It came with the books. Somebody's notes."

She turned it round with one finger, the way she does, and read a page upside down faster than I read it the right way up.

"It's very neat."

"It is."

"He's crossed nothing out." She looked up. "Forty pages and he never changed his mind about anything?"

I hadn't noticed that. I want to be exact: I had looked at that list for eleven days and I had not noticed that there is not one crossing-out in it, and my wife noticed in nine seconds, because she wasn't looking at what it said.

"That's because it's a fair copy," I said slowly. "It's not the working document. This is the version he made *after.*"

"After what?"

"After he had a working one."

She waited to see if I was going to say anything else and I didn't, and she took her tea and went to the other room, and I sat there with a genuinely cold feeling, because that meant there had been another book. Probably several. And a man who keeps a fair copy is a man who expects it to be read by somebody who isn't him.

---

I went through all forty-one entries again that night and this time I read the margins instead of the lines.

Most have nothing. Eleven have something. A year. A word. Twice a name. One says only *poor woman.*

Entry seventeen says: **1994 — the hill.**

Entry seventeen is a survey of the Anatolian highlands, published 1963, page 212, first impression against a 1971 reprint. *eleven thousand years* becomes *a much earlier period.*

I looked up what happened in 1994 near the hills outside a town in southeastern Turkey, and then I sat back in my chair.

I'm not going to do the reveal here, because I didn't get one. It took me another nine days and most of them were spent being wrong. But I'll tell you the word that was written under it, small, in the professor's hand, and which I copied onto a piece of paper and looked at for the rest of the week like a man trying to remember a name.

*redefinition.*
