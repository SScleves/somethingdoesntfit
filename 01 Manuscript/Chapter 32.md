# Chapter Thirty-Two

Buddy made fifteen, which nobody expected, least of all him.

Three more years. I'm not going to give you them in order because they weren't in order; they were a long flat stretch of ordinary time and that is the entire point of them, and if I make them into a narrative I'll have lied at the last minute after four years of not lying.

Here's what was in them.

The mats stayed down. We got a ramp for the car, which he hated for about a fortnight and then used like it had been his idea. He stopped doing the front nine and then he stopped doing the course, and for the last two years he came anyway and stayed in the cart for all eighteen with his ears up like a foreman inspecting a site, and the woman at the counter kept the sausage arrangement going right to the end and I never once said anything about it.

Ana came home in the November. Not a moment — she was there four nights a week and then five and then her mother's spare room was a spare room again and neither of us ever named the day.

Mora turned nine and slowed by about eleven per cent and remained, in her own assessment, a puppy.

I did the eleven-second thing every morning.

---

The last winter he stopped being able to do the stairs at all, so I stopped doing them too. We had the sofa and I had a duvet, and Ana came down most nights for an hour and then went up, and nobody discussed it.

And then in March he didn't meet me at the door.

I'd been out for about forty minutes — the shop, nothing — and I came in and Mora did the resurrection and the hall was otherwise empty, and he was on his blanket, and he lifted his head and did the two thumps.

The vet had told me. Three years earlier, on a floor in a corridor at four in the morning, a woman of about thirty who had been on shift since eleven had said: *it won't be a scrabble at three in the morning. It'll be that he stops meeting you at the door. You'll know, because you'll have been dreading it, and then it'll happen, and you'll think — oh. There it is.*

I stood in my own hallway with a bag of shopping.

*Oh. There it is.*

---

It took nine days and they were good days. I want that written down because I'd expected it to be a descent and it wasn't; it was nine days of a dog who was tired, on a blanket, in a warm room, with people in and out.

I took the week off. Frank said take two.

Ana cooked him chicken every day, which she had spent twelve years refusing to do on grounds of principle, and she did it without any ceremony whatsoever — she just started, on the Tuesday, and put the bowl down, and said nothing, and did it again on the Wednesday.

On the Thursday my wife sat on the floor with her back against the radiator and her hand on him and told him — out loud, in Dutch, which she does when it's real — about her grandmother. The knitting, and the undoing, and the blanket. All of it, to a dog, for about twenty minutes.

I stood in the doorway and didn't come in.

---

He went on a Saturday morning, at home, on his blanket, with the door open to the garden because it was the first warm day.

The vet came to us. That's a thing you can do and nobody tells you and I would tell anybody: they come to you.

I'm not going to describe it. It was quiet and it was quick and my hand was on his chest, and Mora was in the room the whole time because I have read enough by then to know that they should be, and she was extraordinary — she sat about a metre off and watched, and when it was done she came and sniffed him twice, carefully, and then went and lay down in the doorway facing out at the garden, and stayed there for two hours.

The one thing I'll say is that he was looking at me.

Not at the vet, not at Ana, not at the door. He'd spent fifteen years looking at whatever I was doing and he did not change the habit at the end, and I have thought about that more than I have thought about anything else in the last few years, and I have no idea what to do with it and I'm not going to pretend I have.

---

We buried him at the bottom of the garden under the tree, which you are technically not allowed to do and which everybody does.

It took me most of the afternoon. It was warm. Ana brought me a beer at about four and stood there for a while and went back in.

And here's the part I actually want to put down, because it is the exact inverse of a morning four years earlier in gold light on grass so green it looked painted.

**Nobody said anything.**

I did not say *he's at peace.* I did not say *he's in a better place*, or *he's running around up there*, or *he's with the other one now*, or any of the eleven sentences that were available to me, all of which I know, all of which I have heard people say about dogs, several of which are lovely.

Not one of them came out and I did not go looking for one.

I stood there with a spade and a beer at the bottom of my own garden with my hands filthy and I did not have a single borrowed phrase in me, and — this is the thing, this is the whole four years — **it was fine.**

It was not fine that he was dead. That was appalling. I cried more that week than I have cried in my adult life, including at my father's funeral, which is a fact about me that I have taken to two professionals since and which they both, in different words, said was extremely common and not the indictment I thought it was.

But the *grief* was fine. It fitted somewhere. It had a shape and a place and a job to do, and I could do it, and I did.

Four years earlier I had stood at a graveside and been unable to hold a single word anybody gave me, and had concluded, silently, permanently, at sixteen and again at forty-one, that there was something wrong with me.

There wasn't. There was just nowhere to put it.

I had somewhere to put it now. I'm not going to tell you what it is, because that's the honest position and because I don't know myself, and because the moment I make it into a sentence it becomes a thing somebody can edit.

What I'll say is that it is not a belief. I don't have one. I have the same nothing I had at that graveside.

What I have that I didn't have then is **the permission to not know it out loud**, and a wife who says *I don't believe it, and I believe you*, and four hundred entries in a notebook that say the category marked *tired* was never empty.

That turns out to be enough to bury a dog with.

---

One more thing and then I'll stop.

I have spent four years being angry about a machinery that separates. That's the finding, if there is one: every edit, across a century, in four languages, takes out a connection and leaves the weather. *Transmitted to* becomes *arisen among*. A tradition of instruction becomes a tradition. The question of what constitutes an observer becomes a matter regarded as settled. Two people in two shielded rooms becomes a withdrawn paper.

And two pages of the flattest language I have ever read, on a shelf in Anatolia, next to the fire procedures, about maintaining ordinary pressure so that nobody has a spare hour.

Here is what I understand now that I did not understand in that building.

I was twenty-three and my mother told me I was too poor to be depressed, and she was right, and what she meant was *you are about to be too busy for this*. And I was. For eleven years. And what got crowded out was not sadness. Sadness came anyway, sadness always comes. **What got crowded out was the capacity to be with anything.** Anything at all — a question, a grief, a person, a dog. You cannot attend to something while you are calculating whether you can make the rent, and attention is not a luxury version of thinking, it is the *only* thing any of it is made of.

So when I say I had somewhere to put it, in a garden, in March, with a spade, I don't mean I'd found a doctrine.

I mean I had an afternoon.

That's all it is. That's all it has ever been. Four years, forty ruled lines, a plate blemish, a man on a golf course, a room under a hill and two hundred and eleven flashes in a notebook — and what it comes down to, at the bottom of a garden, is that a man had four unhurried hours and nothing more pressing, and could therefore stand still in the presence of the worst thing that had happened to him in twenty-eight years and not have to put it anywhere.

Everybody should get that. That's the whole revolution and it is embarrassingly small.

It isn't knowledge. It was never knowledge. I have the knowledge; the knowledge is thirty-seven diffs and four divisions in a folder and it has done almost nothing for anybody.

It's the afternoon.

The revolution is that a man finally had the time to love a dog properly, and to be wrecked when it ended, and to stand in his garden and say nothing at all.
