# Comp — Sekret Machines, Book 1 (*Chasing Shadows*) & Book 2 (*A Fire Within*)
### DeLonge & Hartley, 2016/2018

> Source: `Documents/Something doesnt fit/`. Book 1: 83 chapters. Book 2: 60+ chapters.
> Read 9 Sep 2026 — full structural map of both books, all interstitial chapters, the complete Morat thread.
> **This is the source you named for two specific techniques.** Both work. One of them is better than you think it is, and one of them collides head-on with your guardrails. Details below.

---

## 1. What it is

A UFO-disclosure thriller written by a rock star and a Shakespeare professor, marketed on the claim that "the events, locations, and moments of wonder are all true. The glue is fiction. The building blocks are not." Four rotating POVs — a disgraced Marine pilot (Alan), an heiress whose financier father is murdered (Jennifer), a debunker who receives a dead man's notebook (Timika), and a Holocaust survivor's journal from 1939 onward (Jerzy) — braided with standalone chapters of "real" UFO history.

The prose is serviceable. The plot goes to Area 51 and then to space, which is where it loses you. **But the architecture is genuinely smart, and it is the architecture you asked about.**

## 2. The mechanisms worth stealing

### 2a. The lore chapters run on a metronome, and there are three distinct kinds.

This is the thing you loved, and it is more precisely engineered than it looks from the reading chair. Book 1's standalone chapters land at **10, 19, 28, 38, 47, 55** — every eight or nine chapters, almost to the beat. They are never "whenever the authors felt like it." The reader learns the rhythm without noticing, which is the whole point.

And they are not one device. They are **three**, deliberately varied so the pattern never calcifies:

| Kind | Example | How it works | Length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dramatized** | ch19 KATY — Roswell, July 1947, from the POV of the seven-year-old daughter of a German scientist | Fiction. A child watches her father take a midnight phone call, waits outside a hangar, glimpses "a giant's cake pan" — and is given a scrap of foil that springs flat and glows. She never understands it. The reader does. | ~90 lines |
| **Documentary essay** | ch28 WILFRIED DEBROUWER — the Belgian triangle wave, 1989–90 | No scene, no dialogue, no character. Straight nonfiction voice: dates, radar stations, F-16 lock failures, the debunkings and why each fails. Named after a real officer who is never a character. | ~29 lines |
| **Primary document** | ch55 ADMIRAL RICHARD E. BYRD — a quoted 1947 Chilean newspaper extract | The artifact alone. No framing, no commentary. | **15 lines** |

That fifteen-line chapter is the most instructive thing in the book. It proves the interstitial can be *tiny* — the reader has been trained by then, and a scrap is enough.

**Why this matters more than as texture:** the Katy chapter is the Roswell foil. The Debrouwer chapter is the radar lock that won't hold. These aren't decorations, they are **the physical evidence the main plot never gets to touch.** The main characters keep being told there's nothing there; the interstitials keep handing the reader objects. The reader ends up *ahead* of the protagonists, and stays ahead, and that is the engine of the book's tension.

**Your version:** you already have this device. It is **the Faucet**, and it is currently doing less work than it could. Reset it as a metronome — roughly every eighth chapter across 33 — and vary the three modes:

- **Dramatized**: a scene from the professor's life. Or Tresidder in Urfa, 1929, from the POV of a porter who carries his cases to a car and never sees him again.
- **Essay**: the flat documentary voice on a real, checkable, *dull* anomaly. Dullness is the credential.
- **Document**: the List entry itself, reproduced. Page 118 as an object on the page. Half a page. Nothing else.

That last one is the one to steal hardest. **A List entry, alone, as its own chapter.** No narrator, no reaction, no interpretation. The book stops and shows you the edit. It will be the most unsettling page in the manuscript precisely because nobody in it is telling you what to think — which is the exact opposite of the Da Vinci Code problem you said you don't want.

### 2b. The villain from three angles — how it actually works, and it's a magic trick, not a character choice.

You remembered this correctly, but the load-bearing part isn't the three angles. It's **where the reveal sits and what costume it wears.**

The setup across Book 1:

1. **Before we ever meet him.** Morat is "the asset": a captured deep-cover operative that a MARSOC team is dying to extract. He is introduced as *the objective.* Three Marines are killed getting him out. The reader's first relationship with the villain is that good men bled for him.
2. **The consoling stranger (ch11).** When Alan is being frozen out by his own unit, Morat steps in and says he saw the flare too, and offers to testify. He gives Alan the only kindness in the chapter: *"Sometimes there is nothing that can be done. We find ourselves at the end of a sequence of events set in motion long ago."* The reader is grateful to him.
3. **The mentor (ch34–39).** He teaches Alan to fly the craft. He jokes. He takes him on an unauthorized joyride to see the Uffington White Horse in the rain, then panics when Alan threatens to land — a moment of apparent human weakness that makes him *more* likable. He says "I'll see you on the other side." By now the reader likes him more than the protagonist.
4. **Inside the money.** In parallel he has been running Jennifer's father's consortium from within — and it was Morat who put the pistol in Edward Quinn's face in chapter 3 and walked him out the window. The reader watched that murder on page 15 without seeing the killer's face.

**Then chapter 51 — at 61% — is titled MORAT, and it goes back to Safid Kuh, September 2014.** Back to the book's opening night. In it, a man named Dubchek walks into a cell, has a courteous conversation with the *real* Jean-Christophe Morat about John Wayne films, shoots him three times, says a blessing over the body, and shaves his beard off to take his face.

The man the reader has loved for fifty chapters has been the killer since before page one.

**The trick — and this is the actual steal:** that reveal is *disguised as a lore chapter.* Fifty chapters of jumping to a strange name in a strange year have trained the reader to accept a chapter headed with an unfamiliar name and a date. Chapter 51 looks like Katy, like Debrouwer, like Byrd. It is the one chapter that isn't random, wearing the uniform of the ones that are.

**The interstitials are camouflage for the reveal.** That is not a side benefit of the structure. That is what the structure is *for*.

### 2c. Book 2's answer to your open question: the villain is turnable, and the mechanism isn't conscience.

Once the reader knows, Book 2 promotes Morat to a **regular POV in the rotation** — chapters 6, 12, 18, 25, 31. He gets a chair at the table.

And what's in his head settles the tee-man question you left open:

> *"Morat was no ideologue, no starry-eyed revolutionary or zealot. He was in it because he was well paid for his skills — which were remarkable — and he had no interest in whatever new world order his superiors wanted to bring about."*

On his employers: *"an amalgam of corrupt financiers, politicians, generals, and other well-connected opportunists. Their names, faces, and wallets opened doors his did not, but otherwise they were no better than he was."*

Then the crack: *"What was the value of being loyal to a doomed venture? They only kept him around because he was useful to them, but what happened when they stopped being useful to him?"* — and he starts building an exit.

**He does not turn because he sees the light. He turns because he runs the numbers and the numbers changed.** He is contemptuous of his own side, professionally proud, and entirely without ideology — and *that* is what makes him turnable, not any goodness in him.

**This is your man at the third tee.** My vote was "turnable," and this is the working model for how: the second tier is staffed by competent professionals who hold the first tier in quiet contempt, take the money, and would leave tomorrow if leaving were survivable. The narrator doesn't convert him with an argument. The narrator changes the arithmetic — and the cheapest way to change it is to make the venture look doomed, which is exactly what a paper Switch mailed to a hundred addresses does.

**So: the Switch isn't only insurance against being killed. It's the thing that makes the tee man's employers look like a bad bet.** That connects your two open threads into one mechanism, and it's the strongest single idea to come out of this read.

(Note also the ex-employee rule: *"People who left the organization tended to vanish from the radar and no one bothered to pretend they were in deep cover."* Your Keepers already do the softer version — correct, ridicule, buy, vanish. Morat's fear is the floor under the tee man's fear.)

### 2d. The recurring outsider — one-off POVs that come back.

Book 2 keeps the interstitial slot but breaks its own rule once: **DEPUTY DEWEY LARSON appears in ch8 and returns in ch14.** A supposedly disposable local-witness POV who turns out to matter. It's a small effect and a good one: it retroactively puts every interstitial under suspicion. Once one of the "random" chapters is load-bearing, the reader can never again fully relax into them — which is the state you want a reader in for the whole back half.

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The multi-POV rotation. This is your guardrail collision and it's serious.** Your rules say *one head, no Keeper POV.* Sekret Machines gets its villain effect from four POVs plus a villain POV. You cannot copy the mechanism directly. **See §4 for how to get all three angles inside one head — that problem is solvable and the solution is better than the original.**
- **The disclosure payload.** The book's actual secret is "we have reverse-engineered craft and go to space." It's inert. The moment the mystery resolves into hardware, the paranoia evaporates and it becomes a competent-people-fly-things novel. Your payload is an *edit* — a changed sentence. Infinitely more durable, because it can't be shown off and it can't be flown.
- **The Nazi-Bell / Antarctica / Argentina lineage.** Fun, and completely off-limits for you: it's the exact "ancient continuity" mythology your Keepers use as a sleeping pill, and if your book endorses one, the book becomes what it's critiquing. Rushkoff already told you why they need it. Don't hand it to them.
- **The foreword.** DeLonge's real-world "the BossMan," "the General," "the Scientist," "IT'S ALL TRUE" framing. It's the book selling its own authenticity, and it's the thing that makes serious readers put it down. **You have the same temptation and must refuse it.** No author's note claiming any of it is real. The book's claim to reality has to be entirely internal — checkable details that check out — or it isn't a claim, it's marketing.
- **Morat's dreams.** Book 2 gives him supernatural nightmares as the crack in his loyalty. It's a cheat: a mystical graft doing work that motive should do (the same failure as PKD's Lantano). Your tee man cracks on arithmetic and one human detail — J.C. Cole's woman at the end of the driveway — not on visions.

## 4. Where it plugs in (Chapter Map numbering)

**The three angles, inside one head.** The narrator can't have Morat's POV. So the three angles become three *channels of report* about the same man, which the narrator collects separately and doesn't connect until late:

| Angle | Sekret Machines version | Your version, single-POV |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Being bad** | ch3, the pistol and the balcony — killer's face unseen | The man at the third tee, ch5–6. Watched, not met. A presence, a physical description, no name. |
| 2. **The one who helps** | ch11 + ch34–39, mentor and friend | A man who is *decent* to him at the worst moment — after the funeral, at the club, in a queue. Gives him something useful. Is never asked for it. The reader must like him. |
| 3. **Inside the money** | Running Maynard from within | A signature, an initial, a name on a foundation letterhead in the professor's papers. Paper only. |

The narrator has three files and no reason to open them on the same desk. **The reveal is the moment he does** — and because he's an IT-monitoring man, the plausible mechanism is exactly his competence: he correlates. A timestamp, a plate, a name in two places. Not a confession. A join.

**The reveal chapter, without a villain POV.** Chapter 51's job — the man's own voice, showing you who he's been — can be done with a **document**, which your book is already built to handle. A transcript. A deposition. A recording the professor made. The tee man's voice, on paper, in the narrator's hands, in the Faucet's slot so the reader's guard is down. **You get Chapter 51 without breaking one-head, and it's better, because the narrator is holding it while he reads it.**

| Book beat | Sekret Machines material |
|---|---|
| Faucet chapters, throughout | the metronome (every ~8th chapter) and the three modes: dramatized / essay / document |
| The List (entries) | ch55 Byrd — **a 15-line chapter that is only the artifact.** Do this at least once. |
| ch5–6 — man at the third tee | angle 1: seen, unnamed, not spoken to |
| Act 1 / early Act 2A | angle 2: the same man being *kind*, unrecognized. The reader must like him or the reveal does nothing. |
| ch17 — the rope has two ends | angle 3: his name on the foundation paperwork, unremarked |
| Act 2B, the pivot | **the join.** Three files, one desk, one correlation. The narrator's job skill is the plot device. |
| the reveal itself | a document in the Faucet's slot — his own voice, no POV break |
| ch20 / ch29–30 — Keepers | second tier: competent, contemptuous of the first tier, unideological, paid. Turnable on arithmetic. |
| the Switch | reframed: not only life insurance — the thing that makes the venture look doomed to the people paid to defend it |
| late Faucet chapter | the Dewey Larson move: one "random" chapter that comes back, to poison the reader's trust in all of them |

## 5. The source's own failures

- **It cannot end.** Book 1 dissolves into 25 chapters of two-to-four pages each (ch59–83), cross-cutting between five locations at machine-gun pace. It reads like panic. The braid that served the first 400 pages has no way to converge, so it just accelerates. **Warning for your Act 3:** a structure that alternates has to be *dismantled* before the climax, not spun faster. Your book has one head, so your equivalent risk is the reverse — a final act that stays in a room. Watch for it.
- **The interstitials stop paying off.** They're at their best in the first half, where the reader is assembling. By ch55 they're padding, because the plot has become "we have spaceships" and there's nothing left for the evidence to be evidence *of*. **The Faucet must be tied to the payload or it decays into trivia.** Every did-you-know should be a load-bearing wall for the edit at the centre, not a curio.
- **The research is worn on the outside.** APG-65 radar, AN/AAQ-28(V) LITENING pods, MH-60L DAPs — paragraphs of hardware that prove the authors did homework rather than proving anything about the story. Weir and Watts don't do this: their technical detail is always *load-bearing*, something the character uses to reach a conclusion. Yours must be the same. If the narrator's monitoring expertise appears, it appears because he *tests* something with it.
- **Nobody in it checks anything.** Timika is a professional debunker — the book's one person whose entire job is verification — and from the moment she opens the notebook she never debunks a single claim in it. She's told to "subject it to your most rigorous debunktion" and then simply believes. **This is the exact failure you named when you said you don't want Da Vinci Code.** The book installs a sceptic and then doesn't use her, because using her would slow the plot. Your narrator has to be the character this one refuses to be — the checking has to be on the page, and it has to sometimes come back *negative*, or it isn't checking, it's ritual.

---

**One line to carry forward:** the metronome hides the ambush. Fifty chapters of "random" history teach the reader that a strange name at the top of a page is just texture — so the chapter that isn't texture walks straight past the guard. Build the Faucet as a rhythm, put one List entry alone on a page with no commentary, and save the slot for the tee man's own voice. And take the second lesson from Morat's head: your second-tier man doesn't need converting, he needs the numbers to change — which is a job the paper Switch is already doing, and nobody has told him yet.
