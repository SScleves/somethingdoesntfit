# RESEARCH 01: craft theory on what makes a plot move

Purpose: find established craft theory for the missing mechanism in *Something Doesn't Fit*, where each step must force the next and James acts by asking, comparing, waiting and persuading. Each entry gives the rule, the source, a published example, and a fit line.

Verification note: only two pages were fetched and read in this session: Justin Alexander's Three Clue Rule (https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule) and Story Grid's thriller page (https://storygrid.com/thriller-genre/). All other claims come from the writers' well-known published teaching and are cited to the source's home page or a reference page. Check the specific page before quoting any of them. Quotes are limited to one short sentence per source.

## 1. Causality

**1.1 But / therefore.** Between any two story beats the connecting words should be "but" or "therefore", never "and then". Each beat either causes the next or complicates it, which is what makes events feel forced. Trey Parker and Matt Stone gave this at an NYU talk, reported widely and summarized at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trey_Parker_and_Matt_Stone (unfetched; search "rule of replacing and then"). Example: in *South Park* episodes, a boy wants a thing, therefore he lies, but the lie is overheard. For James: write the chain "he finds the edit, therefore he asks the printer, but the printer has been told to expect him." Any beat that only fits "and then" is a document delivery and should be cut or rebuilt.

**1.2 Try-fail cycles and "yes, but / no, and."** The hero tries something, and the result is either success with a cost ("yes, but") or failure that makes things worse ("no, and"). Plain successes and plain failures stall the book. Brandon Sanderson teaches this in his BYU lectures (https://www.youtube.com/@BrandonSandersonYT) and Mary Robinette Kowal in her Writing Excuses episodes on plot and in her craft posts (https://writingexcuses.com; https://maryrobinettekowal.com). Example: in *The Hunger Games*, Katniss wins an alliance (yes), but it ties her to a rule she must break (but). For James: each request to a person should end in "yes, but" (she gives the file, but she wants his source) or "no, and" (she refuses, and now the organisation knows he asked).

**1.3 Scene and sequel.** A scene runs goal, conflict, disaster. A sequel runs reaction, dilemma, decision, and the decision sets the next goal. Dwight Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965), and Jack Bickham, *Scene and Structure* (1993), both from University of Oklahoma Press/Writer's Digest (https://www.writersdigest.com). Example: in *Gone Girl*, Nick's reaction to each new clue ends in a decision to hide something, and that decision is the next scene's goal. For James: the sequel is where a man with no powers earns his keep, because the dilemma is "who do I ask next, and what does it cost me to ask?" Put the decision on the page as a choice with a price.

**1.4 Promise, progress, payoff.** The opening makes promises about what kind of story this is. The middle shows progress toward them, and the ending pays them off in the way promised. Sanderson, BYU lectures (same link as 1.2). Example: *Mistborn* promises a heist against a god-emperor in chapter one and shows each crew member's skill before using it. For James: the promise is "the book was edited, and someone knows why." Every chapter should visibly advance that one question, or open a smaller promise that closes inside the chapter.

## 2. Mystery and investigation structure

**2.1 The three clue rule.** For every conclusion the reader or hero must reach, put at least three clues pointing to it, so that one missed or misread clue does not stop the story. Justin Alexander, https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule (fetched). Example: Sherlock Holmes stories such as "Silver Blaze" give the dog, the horse's calm, and the missing lamp. For James: three clues per reveal lets different people hand him different ones, which multiplies his interactions, and a missed clue costs time rather than the plot.

**2.2 Node-based design.** Treat each scene as a node holding clues that point to other nodes, so the investigation is a web rather than a line. The hero can arrive at the end by several paths. Alexander, "Don't Prep Plots" and the Three Clue Rule series, https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule (the fetched page links it but does not define it; the node articles are in the same blog). Example: *Chinatown* offers several threads (water, the permit, the daughter) and each node leads to two more. For James: build a node map of ten to twelve people and places, each node leaking a clue to two others, and let his order of visits change what each person says.

**2.3 Fair-play clueing.** The reader must be shown every fact needed to solve the mystery before the solution, even if disguised. The Detection Club oaths and Ronald Knox's "Ten Commandments" of detective fiction, summarized at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knox%27s_Decalogue (unfetched). Example: Agatha Christie's *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* states the key fact plainly in the narration. For James: the reader should be able to guess the edit's purpose a chapter early, because comedy and dread both land harder when the reader is ahead by a step.

**2.4 Red herrings.** A false lead should be plausible, cost the hero something, and be dropped for a reason. Story Grid lists "investigative red herrings" as a thriller convention (https://storygrid.com/thriller-genre/, fetched). Example: in *The Big Sleep*, the Sternwood blackmail line misleads Marlowe about what the story is. For James: a red herring can be a small, real conspiracy that is itself embarrassing (a publisher's cover-up of a plagiarism claim), which gives the book its "each small conspiracy shows a piece of the bigger one" structure.

**2.5 The detective's active methods.** Investigation is a set of verbs: interview, compare documents, test a hypothesis, set a trap, bluff, stake out. Each verb gives a scene shape. Reference: the working methods in Raymond Chandler's essay "The Simple Art of Murder" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Simple_Art_of_Murder) and the procedural tradition. Example: in Tana French's *In the Woods*, interviews are the scenes where the plot moves. For James, limited to ask, compare, wait and persuade: the testable verbs are "compare printings" (an object), "ask the same question of two people" (a contradiction), "wait at a place" (a stakeout without trespass), and "persuade by offering something the person wants."

**2.6 Amateur sleuths and access without authority.** Amateurs get doors opened by being nobody: harmless, useful, kind, or funny. Their ordinary skill replaces a badge. See the cozy-mystery tradition discussed at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cozy_mystery. Example: Miss Marple's knowledge of village life, and Rowan Atkinson-style bumblers in farce. For James: his job is monitoring, so he notices anomalies in other people's behavior as well as in text. Let a clerk open a door because James noticed her work was good, or because he fixed her small problem first.

## 3. Thriller and conspiracy-thriller structure

**3.1 Story Grid obligatory scenes and conventions.** Shawn Coyne's thriller genre requires an inciting crime pointing to a master villain, a speech in praise of the villain, the protagonist becoming the victim, hero at the mercy of the villain, and a false ending. Conventions are MacGuffin, red herrings, a clock, and making it personal. Source: https://storygrid.com/thriller-genre/ (fetched). Example: *The Silence of the Lambs* has Lecter's praise-scenes and Clarice at his mercy. For James: the crime is the edit; the "speech in praise of the villain" can be a gatekeeper explaining, almost admiringly, why the organisation exists.

**3.2 Hero at the mercy of the villain.** Late in the story the hero must be in the villain's power, and survive using his unique gift rather than force. Same Story Grid page. Example: in *North by Northwest*, Thornhill is at Vandamm's mercy and escapes by playing the role they assigned him. For James, since the organisation discredits rather than kills: mercy means a public humiliation scene where his gift (noticing, comparing) is what turns it, for instance a discrepancy he holds that the room can verify.

**3.3 The MacGuffin.** The object everyone wants matters because they want it, not for what it is. Alfred Hitchcock's account is at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGuffin. Example: the briefcase in *Pulp Fiction* is never opened. For James: the edited book is the first MacGuffin, but each person he meets wants something specific from it, which creates the side-character wants in section 5.

**3.4 The ticking clock.** A deadline converts curiosity into pressure and forbids dawdling. Story Grid lists "clock" as a thriller convention (https://storygrid.com/thriller-genre/). Example: the Reichenbach-style deadlines in Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* (one night in Paris). For a hero who waits, the clock should come from the other side: the organisation is about to complete a printing, an archive transfer, a funeral, a destruction date. Waiting itself becomes expensive.

**3.5 Escalation ladders.** Each stage should raise the cost and the scale of the thing the hero is up against. Reference: Story Grid's thriller escalation (https://storygrid.com/thriller-genre/) and Dent below. Example: *The Fugitive* moves from police to a marshal to the conspirators. For James: a ladder of costs he can bear without violence: embarrassment at work, a lost client, a flagged account, a ruined reference, a wife's doubt, a public smear.

**3.6 Conspiracy-thriller stages.** The paranoid thriller follows a pattern: a small anomaly, a first official denial, the discovery that the denial was prepared, an ally who turns, a false resolution (the official story), then the hero's decision to publish or act. Discussed in genre studies of 1970s paranoid cinema, e.g. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranoid_fiction. Example: *The Parallax View*, *Three Days of the Condor*, *All the President's Men* (Woodward and Bernstein check one source against another). For James: "who can be trusted" is answered by testing people with small lies or small asks, which is another use of his four verbs.

**3.7 Lester Dent's master plot formula.** A 6,000-word pulp story is four 1,500-word parts; each part brings a new trouble, a physical conflict, and a surprise twist; the hero fails at each turn until the end. Described at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_Dent (unfetched; the formula text circulates widely). Example: *Doc Savage* stories. For James: use the rhythm, not the violence. Each quarter of the book needs a new obstacle, a human encounter, and a twist that reverses what the last object seemed to prove.

**3.8 Puzzle-box pacing (Brown, Eco).** Dan Brown's chapters end on a clue or reversal, and the story is a chain of coded objects. It works because each answer opens a new question; it fails when characters exist only to hand over clues. Umberto Eco, *Foucault's Pendulum* (1988), and his postscript to *The Name of the Rose* discuss the same appetite and its danger, and *Foucault's Pendulum* is a joke about people who believe a conspiracy too much. Postscript: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflections_on_The_Name_of_the_Rose. For James: this is the failure mode to avoid ("he just receives documents"). Each clue needs a person who has a reason to give it and a reason not to.

## 4. The passive protagonist

**4.1 Make the hero cause the plot.** A hero who only reacts is moved by events; a hero who chooses is moved by his own decisions. Kurt Vonnegut's "every character should want something, even a glass of water" applies (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Vonnegut, eight rules). John Truby's desire line in *The Anatomy of Story* (https://truby.com). Example: Marlowe causes scenes by walking into rooms. For James: each chapter should open with a decision he made, such as an email sent, a favor offered, a wait he chose, and the scene is the result.

**4.2 Midpoint shift from reactive to proactive.** Around the middle the hero stops responding and starts planning. Many structure writers describe this: K. M. Weiland, "Structuring Your Novel" (https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com), and James Scott Bell, *Super Structure* (https://jamesscottbell.com). Example: in *Rear Window*, Jeff first watches, then sets a test. For James: first half, he finds; second half, he designs a test that makes a person reveal themselves, for instance printing a deliberate false detail and seeing who corrects it.

**4.3 Cost of being wrong.** A decision matters when the wrong choice hurts something specific. Weiland and Bell, above. Example: in *The Lives of Others*, a wrong move costs lives. For James: wrong means a wrongly accused person, a ruined friendship, or a public error that hands the organisation a way to discredit him.

**4.4 Irreversible choices.** Cross a line from which there is no going back. Truby calls this the "point of no return" in the story structure at https://truby.com. Example: Frodo leaving the Shire. For James: quitting his job, publishing a claim, or giving a source's name. Since he cannot pressure, his irreversible acts are disclosures and promises.

## 5. Character webs and side characters

**5.1 Truby's character web and four-corner opposition.** Each character is defined by how they differ from the hero in approach to the same problem. Four-corner opposition: hero, main opponent, and two more opponents who each want something different. Source: John Truby, *The Anatomy of Story* (2007), https://truby.com. Example: in *Chinatown*, Mulwray, Cross and Evelyn each want something different in the same affair. For James: the organisation should have at least three people who oppose him in different ways (one by kindness, one by procedure, one by money).

**5.2 Ally, fake-ally opponent, fake-opponent ally.** A helper who is secretly an enemy, and an enemy who secretly helps. Truby, same source. Example: Dr. Chilton and Lecter in *Silence of the Lambs*, or Gandalf's counterfeit, Saruman. For James: his best interviews should be with someone who appears hostile but gives him a thread, and a friendly one who reports to the organisation.

**5.3 Function of each side character.** Archetypal roles: gatekeeper, mentor, skeptic, mirror, tempter, herald. Source: Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey* (https://www.thewritersjourney.com), drawing on Joseph Campbell. Example: *The Matrix* uses Morpheus as mentor, Cypher as tempter, Tank as helper. For James: the gatekeeper is the clerk or archivist who decides whether a door opens, the skeptic is his best friend, the mirror is someone who stopped asking, the tempter offers a reward for dropping it.

**5.4 Give each character a want that conflicts with the hero's.** A character who has no goal of their own can only supply information. Truby (as above) and Vonnegut (as above). Example: Lestrade's ambition drives his interactions with Holmes. For James: the archivist wants her department left alone; the editor wants the old author's reputation intact; each person pays James with a clue only if he gives something back, so every clue is a trade.

**5.5 Dramatica's character roles.** Dramatica Theory (Melanie Anne Phillips, Chris Huntley, https://dramatica.com) defines eight archetypal functions such as Protagonist, Antagonist, Guardian, Contagonist, Reason, Emotion, Sidekick and Skeptic, and says that a character's role is a function, not a personality. Example: Reason and Emotion pairs in many buddy stories. For James: the Contagonist (a character who tempts him off course without opposing his goal) is the useful one; he is often a friend with good intentions.

**5.6 Make a spouse or best friend a source of plot.** A listener only receives exposition; a source of plot has an interest. Give them a conflict with the hero's investigation (it costs them money, safety, or standing) and a piece of information the hero lacks. Source: Truby's character web (https://truby.com); advice in K. M. Weiland's posts on supporting characters (https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com). Example: Marge Gunderson's husband Norm in *Fargo* offers a small, unrelated fact and an anchor. For James: the spouse notices the pattern before he does, or she is approached by the organisation, so the home scenes carry plot.

## 6. Delivering exposition and lore

**6.1 The Pope in the pool.** Put the necessary explanation inside a scene where something else is happening, so the audience watches that instead. The term comes from Robert McKee's *Story* (1997) and the phrase is in https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_(narrative). Example: in *Chinatown*, Gittes learns about the water scandal while Evelyn lies to him. For James: the lore should be said while someone is lying, bargaining, or doing something awkward, such as taking apart a bad office chair.

**6.2 Exposition as ammunition.** Characters should use facts to hurt, trade or defend, so a fact appears when it changes the situation. McKee, *Story*, same source. Example: in *A Few Good Men*, "You can't handle the truth" delivers lore as a weapon. For James: his documents become ammunition when he knows something a person did not know he knew, which stops him from "receiving".

**6.3 Withholding.** Hold the information back and let the audience feel the gap. Alfred Hitchcock's bomb-under-the-table distinction between surprise and suspense (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspense) fits here: show the reader the bomb and let the hero not know it. For James: let the reader learn what a document means before James does, and let him ask the question the reader is already asking.

**6.4 The reveal as a reversal.** A reveal works when it changes what earlier scenes meant, rather than adding a fact. Story Grid and McKee both call these "turning points" (https://storygrid.com). Example: *The Sixth Sense*. For James: each object should reinterpret the last, such as the printing that looked like a correction turning out to be the original.

**6.5 Iceberg theory.** Show a small part and let the unshown parts be felt. Ernest Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon* (1932), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory. Example: Hemingway's "Hills Like White Elephants." For James: the organisation's lore (reincarnation, resets) can stay mostly offstage; the reader senses it from what people refuse to say.

**6.6 Pacing a large secret.** Ask a question, delay the answer, and ask a bigger one. Lee Child has described this as the engine of his books in interviews (https://leechild.com; unfetched). Example: *Killing Floor* poses a murder, then a counterfeiting ring, then a larger one. For James: use one full chapter of answer to each small conspiracy, and let it end by naming the next question. Reserve the explicit statement of reincarnation for the last third.

## 7. Comedy inside a thriller

**7.1 Jokes from character, not interruption.** Humor holds up when it comes from characters who take the situation seriously and are limited. Douglas Adams, *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*; Terry Pratchett, *Guards! Guards!* and his essays (https://www.terrypratchett.com). Example: Pratchett's Vimes is funny because he is grimly literal and the stakes stay real. For James: let him be literal about procedure and have the organisation be equally bureaucratic.

**7.2 Keep the stakes serious while the surface is funny.** The Coens in *Fargo* and *Burn After Reading*, and Mick Herron's Slough House novels (*Slow Horses*, https://www.mickherron.com), play incompetence for laughs and then let a death or a loss be real. Example: Jackson Lamb is rude and the consequences are real. For James: since the organisation discredits rather than kills, the real cost has to be social and emotional (humiliation, a lost friend) and must be felt with a straight face.

**7.3 Let comedy do plot work.** A joke can plant a clue, such as a ridiculous detail that turns out to be the evidence. Example: in Adams's *Dirk Gently*, absurd coincidences are the plot. For James: plant the clue as a joke and pay it off as a fact.

## The ten techniques that fit this book best

1. **1.1 But / therefore.** It gives the test for every scene: does this scene cause the next, or just follow it?
2. **2.5 The detective's active methods.** It turns "receives documents" into verbs James can actually perform with only ask, compare, wait and persuade.
3. **5.4 A want that conflicts with the hero's.** Every clue becomes a trade, so each side character carries plot.
4. **1.3 Scene and sequel.** The sequel's dilemma is where James chooses whom to ask, which is the decision the book currently lacks.
5. **2.1 and 2.2 Three clues and node-based design.** They multiply interactions and make the investigation a web rather than a mail delivery.
6. **4.2 The midpoint shift to proactive.** It gives the second half a trap or test that James designs.
7. **5.2 Ally, fake-ally opponent, fake-opponent ally.** It supplies the turns, and the "who can be trusted" stage of the conspiracy thriller.
8. **6.2 Exposition as ammunition.** It converts delivered lore into leverage James can use without force.
9. **3.2 Hero at the mercy of the villain, adapted.** It fits an organisation that discredits, and it forces James to use his noticing at the climax.
10. **7.2 Serious stakes under a funny surface.** It keeps the comedy from deflating the social and emotional costs that replace violence.
