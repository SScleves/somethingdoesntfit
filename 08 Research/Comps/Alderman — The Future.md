# Comp — Naomi Alderman, *The Future* (2023)

> Read in full 9 Sep 2026 (~123k words, six parts + epilogue). Brief: *how the elite prepare for the bunkers, and how they track the moment to go.* Answer: this book is that question. The whole plot is the trigger.
> Standing rule holds: the three CEOs are thin analogues of real people (Fantail/Zuckerberg, Anvil/Bezos, Medlar/Apple). Mechanisms transfer; the analogues don't.

---

## 1. What it is

Three tech billionaires buy **AUGR**, an AI that claims to predict catastrophe and tell them *when to go* — ten days ahead of anyone else. Lenk's assistant **Martha Einkorn** (daughter of a survivalist cult prophet; posts Bible-study on a prepper forum as "OneCorn") and three insiders (a billionaire's wife, an ousted founder, a billionaire's kid) built AUGR as a con. They fake the alert, fake the plane going down, strand the three on a wildlife-sanctuary island, feed them a fake plague through the visors of their "survival suits" — and take the companies. Two twists: **AUGR never worked**, and **the apocalypse never happened.** Interleaved throughout: forum posts retelling Lot and Sodom (*when is it time to leave? would you save the city for fifty?*) and the cult founder's sermon on hunter-gatherers vs. farmers.

## 2. The mechanism worth stealing

### 2a. The trigger, on one slide — and the con inside it.
Si Packship's pitch inside the fake mountain (Part 3, "when to go") is the cleanest statement of your CLOCK thread anyone has written:
- *"By the time you know the world's ending, it's already too late."*
- Pilots don't trust instinct; they have instruments that say *pull up.* The tech industry is optimistic — "we keep thinking we can fix it, until it's too late." So: a machine to decide when.
- **The defense perimeter**: leave ten days before the event. Then the cascade of practical questions — *riots on the streets? how does your limo get through? is your plane still there? has the fuel been drained and sold? how secret is your bunker? you trust your pilot's next-door neighbour, on the day food prices spike for the seventeenth time?*
- What AUGR ingests: economic data, weather, seismic, currency, population movement, traffic, searches, posts, phone usage, bird flight patterns, bee pollination, ant migration. *"We don't ask whether a dataset is relevant — we presume anything can be."*
- And the "lifestyle" layer: a black dot in the corner of your screen nobody notices; AUGR spoofs emails to build a plausible cover story ("sudden business opportunity"); you still have time for dinner before the plane.

**For the book:** this is the schedule as a readable document (ch. 29) — a *sales deck*. And the con is your predict-vs-cause ambiguity resolved from an angle you haven't used: **the people who control the instrument control what the powerful believe is coming.** Your Keepers blind the watchers by editing the defaults. Alderman shows the reverse is also available — someone can edit the Keepers' own instrument. The first tier trusts its dashboards more than any living person. That's a vulnerability the narrator, of all people, would see.

### 2b. The evacuation protocol is a document. She wrote it.
Part 1, Ellen: *"do not pack all your belongings. Only small items of sentimental value. Your needs will be provided for."* No phone calls. The cover story is pre-agreed ("closed-session negotiations"). Transponder off out of radar range. Separate planes to a staging post, then onward. *"Normal transportation. Nothing that draws attention to our leaving."* Guests: "You can bring two people. Tell them to leave their phones. Tell them it's a vacation."

That last line is the roster, from the top down — *two* people, and they don't get to know. The whole protocol is a thing the narrator could hold in ch. 29: numbered, dry, written by the person who'll follow it. It reads as HR. That's the register you want for the binder.

### 2c. The conservation zone is the bunker.
**FutureSafe zones**: wildlife sanctuaries bought from "beleaguered post-pandemic governments," human-free for a hundred years, no-fly, drone-patrolled with authorized lethal force, monetized as VR — and *confidential clauses in the agreements* allow the owner to go there in an emergency. Lenk's island is a 300-year monastery hidden inside a nature reserve.

That is your ch. 17 rope, literalized: the foundation funding "heritage stabilization" and the continuity bunker are one deed. **The narrator could find the covenant.** A conservation agreement with a donor-access clause is an object, checkable, and exactly the kind of thing that hides in a legal document nobody reads past the summary.

### 2d. Three bunkers = three psychologies. Steal the method, not the buildings.
Alderman characterizes each CEO through the bunker they built:
- **Ellen's hill** (NZ): eight storeys down, daylight drawn in by fiberglass filaments, a reading room of *printed* books, a curve modelled on an extinct bird's beak, 500 people, ~$700M. The architects are **contractually forbidden to remember it** — their lawyers said that's impossible; hers said "we prefer to keep the clause, to assure ourselves they are aware of their obligation to forget." That is a Keeper edit-of-instruments, absurd and real: *a legal document that orders forgetting.*
- **Zimri's honeycomb** (Haida Gwaii): two storeys, every wall a blast door, "if you own both cells you control the wall," an interrogation room, a pool. Security built by teams who never met: *"the war of all against all could make you safe, as long as they were fighting each other and not you."* The whole thing is a portrait of a man who trusts nobody.
- **Lenk's island**: arrowheads and the instructions to make them; germ theory carved into the wall in pictographs; laptops plasti-sprayed to last seventy years; seed banks; an algae that eats poison; a chapel with no symbols. A monastery for the dark ages. Martha understood his vision "better than anyone alive" and built it — which is why he needed her.

The lesson for your ch. 27–29: *don't describe the hoard; describe what its builder was afraid of.* A bunker's floor plan is a confession.

### 2e. The guard problem, Alderman's answer — and where it fails.
Zimri solved Rushkoff's question by design: no single contractor knows enough. It fails anyway: **four separate engineers were fans of a survival influencer and told her**, across several years, the location, the codes, and the defenses. Compartmentalization breaks through *fandom*, not betrayal.

That's your countdown and your entrance. The Keepers' method assumes the people who build the thing don't talk. They do — to people they admire. That's how the crate got out of the professor's house; that's how a guard who "only ever checked the ends" ends up answering a question.

### 2f. The Switch, captured. (Read this one twice.)
Lenk has a tracker behind his ear and a **dead man's switch**: if he doesn't confirm daily, drones deploy and search-and-rescue is notified. In the final chapter, Martha — now running his company — sits at his old laptop every morning at 5:30 and **turns the alarm off with his password.** Every day. For three years.

Your narrator's Switch is the spine of Act 3. Alderman has just demonstrated the failure mode: *a digital dead man's switch belongs to whoever holds the credentials.* Which is the whole argument for the version the revision already chose — **a notary and stamps.** Paper can't be logged into. Keep the digital forum-post version as the decoy the Keepers *think* they've captured; let the envelopes be the real payload. Alderman's Martha is what happens to the narrator's switch if it lives on a server.

### 2g. The instrument is matchboxes and beads.
Marius's lecture on MENACE (Part 4, "MENACE"): a 1960 tic-tac-toe machine made of 300 matchboxes and coloured beads. It "learns" by an operator adding beads for wins and removing them for losses. *"Bead can't learn. Human learns. Machine iterates."* And: *"a book is stored thoughts; an AI is stored thinking."* And the line that matters most: **"Who knows if the machine has lost? I do. We are the ones who can tell. We know when to take a bead, when to give a bead."**

For ch. 18 this is the philosophy under the config diff: *the defaults are beads, and somebody chose which beads to add.* An anomaly classifier that's been taught "this class of failure is noise" is a matchbox someone reached into. Same hands as page 118. And AUGR — the billion-dollar oracle — turns out to be a tactical-options tool that "presents options"; it never saw the future. The people who trusted it over their own senses were the ones who could be steered. **Instruments don't lie. Their operators do.** That sentence is the book.

### 2h. The mask, worn by the winners.
Zhen says one careless line about a dead prophet on a hot mic; a three-second clip travels; a real assassin follows her into a mall. Later, the true story of the Tech Plane is dismissed on air as an "UrbanDox theory that cultural Marxists disappeared the plane," and Martha smiles: *"there will always be people who can't accept the truth."* **The truth is protected by sounding like a conspiracy theory** — and here you see it from inside the people the mask protects. Your ch. 21 is the mask from the victim's side; this is the same mask from the Keepers' side. Both scenes are in the book you're writing.

### 2i. The forum posts — a parallel text that earns its place.
OneCorn's Bible study (Sodom, Lot, Abraham bargaining God down from fifty to ten; Lot's daughters in the cave; Abraham refusing the spoils — *"more than greed causes wealth, wealth causes greed"*) runs alongside the plot in a different register. It works because it does three jobs at once: theme (*when is it time to leave? is it OK to give up on a place? how few good people is too few?*), **character** (OneCorn is Martha — the reveal converts the essays into a portrait of the woman running the con), and **plot** (the forum is where the conspirators met; the forum crowd-locates a suicidal poster by his kimchi brand and Martha saves his life). Your Faucet's reader-facing closers and the List entries are the same device. The lesson: a parallel text must eventually *do* something in the plot, or it's decoration. Alderman's does; it also runs long.

### 2j. Smaller usable pieces
- **"Would you save the city for fifty?"** used as a refrain through a montage of corporate harms, counting down to ten. A countdown structure that's also an ethical one — the Keepers' own question, inverted: how few must be *awake* before the switch is justified?
- Enoch's sermon: **"The Future. This is the essential problem."** The anxiety about not-knowing is what turned hunters into farmers; the certainty of a bad thing was preferable to the uncertainty of a hunt. *"If you can't tolerate uncertainty, you will try to make things certain. Sometimes the only way to make them certain is to make them bad."* That's the Keepers' psychology in two sentences, and it's why they'd rather schedule a reset than risk an awakening.
- Lenk **had himself kidnapped by bikers** to see how he'd cope, and used the safe word two hours in to make a conference call. A first-tier portrait in one anecdote: needs to control the future, can't stand the present.
- The engineer at Fantail whose one imperfect keystroke, baby crying, takes the platform down worldwide; the polio doses wasted; the family who froze because *"if it was really bad, there'd have been an alert."* — the cascade, shown as consequences on people, no dashboard in sight. That's the register for ch. 29's night.
- The transmitter that makes a private jet's Wi-Fi show its passengers a filtered internet. A bunker's *information supply* is as controllable as its food. Rhymes with the AI defaults: what you see is what someone decided you'd see.
- The epilogue: **"MANY, MANY YEARS LATER"** — historians debating; the last survivor confused and welcoming settlers to his island; a recording found decades on. *The record outlives the people.* Your Coda II is reincarnation-shaped, not archival, but the move — a later age reading the residue — is the same, and it refuses a clean victory (the next president is a cultist; "the work is never over").

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The double twist.** *It was all fake* is the exact move your guardrails forbid ("real anomalies may be real"). Alderman can do it because the book is a satirical heist; in yours it would be the Da Vinci Code move in reverse — the reader hand-fed the answer, then told the answer was a trick. Your book's bomb is that the edits are *real.* Don't let the con model tempt you.
- **Multi-POV.** Three CEOs, Martha, Zhen, an omniscient narrator who steps in to moralize. You're one head; the book's best pages are the ones inside Martha alone.
- **Analogues of real people.** Fantail/Anvil/Medlar are Zuckerberg/Bezos/Apple with the serial numbers filed off. Satire licenses that. Your register doesn't. Custodians, not CEOs; a method, not a man you can name.
- **The invented religion.** Enochites, the Five Sermons, a mark tattooed on a finger. Your Keepers are institutional, not sectarian — a foundation letterhead, not a cult. The moment the opposition has scripture, it's a different book.
- **The comedy-and-sex register** (hotel keys, "no fucking shit," the sex suits). Not yours. The one thing to notice: Alderman uses comedy to make the reader forgive a heist. You don't need forgiving.
- **The hand-waved happy ending.** "Things had gotten better… as if people had gone suddenly sane." A montage with no mechanism — precisely what your coda must not do (your log: *no formula stated*). Alderman states it and it reads as a wish.

## 5. The source's own failures

- **The middle sags.** Zhen hiding in Madrid, Bucharest, the hackspace fire, the bunker break-in — a chase without discovery. Set-pieces standing in for plot. Your ch. 5–9 sag risk is the same shape; note that Alderman's cure was more incident, and it didn't cure it.
- **The Marius lecture is an essay in a costume.** Brilliant content, delivered as a classroom scene the plot pauses for. Your Not-Langdon rule exists for exactly this.
- **Only Martha is a person.** The three CEOs are types (the rager, the paranoid, the widow) and Zhen is a competence. The forum reveal gives Martha depth the others never get. If your book has one antagonist with an inner life — the tee man — it's already ahead.
- **The twist costs belief.** Three of the most capable people alive never independently verify a global plague because a filtered Wi-Fi told them not to. Alderman covers it (the transmitter; the drugs) but the reader feels the thumb on the scale. Your Competence Ledger rule — *never dumber than the 1931 plate* — is the defense against this.
- **It's dated by design.** Post-pandemic, EV rollouts, Great-Reset-adjacent, 2023 billionaire gossip. The forum posts (Genesis, Lot, Fox and Rabbit) will outlive the rest of the book by decades. Note which layer aged: the ancient one didn't.

And the sentence under the whole comp: *instruments don't lie; their operators do.* Your Keepers know it. So, from now on, does your narrator.
