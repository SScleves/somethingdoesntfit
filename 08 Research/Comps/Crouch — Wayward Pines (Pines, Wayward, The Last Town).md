# Comp — Blake Crouch, *Pines* / *Wayward* / *The Last Town* (2012–14)
### Read 9 Sep 2026 — reveal and recruitment sequences read closely

> Your brief: *"wayward pines same help with plot."*
> It delivers, but not where you'd expect. The town, the mystery and the abbies are the least useful part. **The useful part is what Pilcher says to Ethan after the reveal, and what Pilcher does when the truth finally gets out.** Both go straight into your Act 2B and Act 3.

---

## 1. What it is

A federal agent wakes in an idyllic Idaho town he can't leave, investigating two missing colleagues. Everyone is lying, the phones don't work, the fence is electrified, and the townspeople periodically hunt and murder a neighbour at a public "fête." Halfway through book 1 the floor drops: it is not 2012, it's roughly AD 3813, humanity has speciated into predators, and the town is a billionaire's ark, watched from a bunker in the mountain by the man who built it.

Book 2 makes Ethan the sheriff — the investigator recruited to run the lie. Book 3 is the siege.

---

## 2. The mechanisms worth stealing

### 2a. **The lie has a control group, and it's in the data.**

This is the best thing in the trilogy and it is directly aimed at your book. Ethan asks the obvious question — why not just tell people the truth? Pilcher's answer isn't philosophy. It's an experiment he already ran:

> *"We did that very thing with the first group. We'd just finished rebuilding the town, and we brought everyone down to the church and said, 'Look, here's the deal.' Told them everything."*
>
> *"And?"*
>
> *"Within two years, thirty-five percent had committed suicide. Another twenty percent left town and were slaughtered. Nobody married. No one got pregnant. I lost ninety-three people, Ethan."*

He tried the truth. It killed a third of them. **The lie is evidence-based.**

Set this next to Erskine in *Shift* (*"when there's only God to blame, we forgive him; when it's our fellow man, we destroy him"*) and Rushkoff's green room, and you have the same argument arriving from three independent directions. That convergence is your signal: **this is the argument your Keepers must make, and it is the strongest one available to them.** Not "we're protecting our power." Not "people are sheep." Something much worse to answer:

**We ran the experiment. Telling them kills them. We have the numbers.**

Your narrator has to hear that and go anyway. That's the book.

### 2b. The recruitment line — how the buy actually works.

The single most useful sentence in three novels:

> *"The people in town, for the most part, can't handle the truth of what's out there. But you... you can't handle the lie. The not knowing. You're the first resident I've ever shared any of this with."*

**This is your ch20, and it's better than a bribe.** The Keepers don't threaten your narrator and they don't buy him with money. They *diagnose* him — correctly, flatteringly, in a way he recognises as true — and then offer him the only job in the world that fits the diagnosis. Come inside. Help us run it. You're rare; that's why you found it; that's why you belong here rather than out there with the people who can't take it.

Note what makes it work: **Pilcher is right about Ethan.** He *can't* handle not knowing. The offer is attractive because it's accurate. The buy that your narrator nearly takes should be the one that sees him clearly.

And the terms are stated plainly, which is scarier than menace: *"You can reign in hell here on the outside, or serve in heaven, back in Pines... And my terms come with severe penalties."*

### 2c. The investigator is made the enforcer.

Book 2's whole premise: the man who uncovered the town is given a badge and told to run it. He now has to conduct the integrations, watch the surveillance feeds, and decide whether to call a fête on a neighbour.

**This is the sharpest structural idea in the trilogy** and it maps onto your book with almost no adjustment: the narrator, having found the List, is offered the custodianship of it. Not silence — *stewardship*. Become the next professor. The archive needs someone careful, and he has proven he's careful. **The Keepers' succession problem is solved by the people who find them out.** That reframes the professor entirely: he wasn't a whistleblower who failed. He may have been a Keeper who defected late, and the List is his resignation letter.

(Which also gives you a chilling reading of the List's final entry — *"Ask."* Not an instruction to the reader. A question he was asked, and answered wrong, or right, or too late.)

### 2d. The surveillance room — and it should be your narrator's, not theirs.

Pilcher's mountain complex has a bank of screens showing every street and most interiors. A crew of watchers. Nothing happens in town without someone seeing it.

**Your protagonist is a monitoring expert. He is professionally, temperamentally the man on the other side of that glass.** Crouch never does anything interesting with this — the watchers are functionaries — but you have the character who would understand what he's looking at.

The strongest version, and it connects to the climax you've already chosen (*a night of alerts firing on screens supposed to stay green*): **the narrator's expertise is that he knows what a dashboard is for.** A dashboard is not a window; it's a set of decisions about what counts as an event. Someone chose those thresholds. Someone decided what gets classified as noise. That person had a name and a job title and a Tuesday afternoon. Rushkoff gave you *"if you don't know what it is, it's noise."* Crouch gives you the room where the screens are. **Your book should let the narrator walk into a version of that room and read the config instead of the screens** — because reading the config is what he's for, and it's the one thing nobody in any of these eight sources does.

### 2e. What happens when the truth gets out — the retaliation is not aimed at you.

Book 2's climax: Ethan gathers the town in the theatre and **tells them everything.** Shows them the footage. The screen comes down and, in a genuinely good beat, *people stay in their seats.*

Pilcher's response is not to kill Ethan. He goes to the control room and **cuts the power to the fence, then opens the gate.**

> *"Pilcher killed the power... No, I mean he killed the power to the fence. He also opened the gate."*

**Read that against your Switch.** The narrator's paper insurance — photographed List pages, notarised, mailed to a hundred addresses — protects *him*. It does not protect anyone else, and the Keepers' most credible threat isn't "we'll kill you," it's "we'll let go of something we've been holding." A managed cascade is exactly that shape: an accelerating hand on things already failing deniably. **They don't have to attack. They only have to stop maintaining.**

That is the true stake of your Act 3, it is far more frightening than a hitman, and it makes the tee man's arithmetic (see the Sekret Machines comp) matter enormously — because the people who'd have to *do* the not-maintaining are exactly the second-tier professionals who'd be weighing whether the venture is worth it.

### 2f. He is not the first.

Ethan learns this is his **third** attempt to escape; the previous two were wiped by amnesia. The reveal is that the story has already happened, twice, and been reset.

You can't use amnesia. **You can use the professor.** Your narrator is not the first to find page 118 — he's the second, and the first one is dead and left him a list of forty edits ending in a question. Crouch's version is a gimmick; yours is a genuinely mournful structural fact, and it puts a floor under the narrator's dread: *whatever I'm about to do, someone smarter already tried it, and I have his notes and his grave.*

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The enclosure.** Wayward Pines is a *box* — fence, cliffs, no exit. Your book's horror is the opposite and much better: **there is no fence.** The narrator can leave any time. He can fly home. Nothing stops him, and that's worse, because it means the thing holding him is his own conclusion. Never build a wall in this book.
- **The single villain in a mountain.** Pilcher is one man with a bunker, a helicopter and a god complex. Your Keepers must be a *habit*, distributed, older than any member, with no room to storm. The moment there is one man to defeat, the theme dies.
- **The twist economy.** Crouch's engine is escalating reveals, roughly one per fifty pages, and by book 3 they've eaten the story — nobody in it can be a person because everyone must be a surprise. Your Reveal Rules exist to prevent exactly this. **The convergence of every source I've read is the same warning: a twist is a bad substitute for a method.**
- **The abbies.** Monsters solve the third act by making it a siege. You have no monsters and shouldn't want any.
- **The violence.** The fêtes — public murder as social cohesion — is a strong idea served with a lot of gore. Your Keepers' equivalent is already better and quieter: *they never fight stories, only instruments.* Nobody bleeds. That's the whole aesthetic.
- **The wife and children as leverage.** Pilcher's final threat is against Ethan's son. Your narrator has a dead wife and an old dog, and the temptation to threaten Buddy will be strong. **Resist it.** Buddy survives and dies of age; that's already established and it's the right call. A threatened pet would turn a serious book into a thriller in one page.

## 5. The source's own failures

- **The reveal is delivered sitting in a field.** Two men on the ground, one explaining the premise for eight pages. Same disease as Howey's morgue scene and Sekret Machines' briefings — *the best content in the book arrives as a lecture.* Three of your eight sources make this exact mistake, which should tell you how hard it is to avoid and how much it costs. **Your reveals arrive as objects in the narrator's hands.** That is the single discipline that will most distinguish your book from its comps.
- **Nobody tests anything, again.** Ethan is a federal investigator in a town of obvious lies and he mostly runs, hides and gets caught. In three books there is close to no investigative *procedure*. Crouch's mysteries are solved by being told, or by walking somewhere and seeing. **The gap between this and Weir is the whole of the book you said you want to write.**
- **The prose reaches.** Lots of one-line paragraphs for percussion. It's effective and it's cheap, and after two hundred pages it reads like a man tapping the table. Watts and Weir both trust the content.
- **Pilcher never has a real interior.** He gets the best arguments in the trilogy — the control group, the freedom-is-a-construct line, "it's not their decision to make" — and he's still played as a megalomaniac so the reader knows who to root for. **Don't insulate your reader like that.** Your Keepers should say true things, and the narrator should have to go anyway.
- **Book 3 is a siege.** Once the fence is down, thinking stops and running starts. Watch your own Act 3 for the same collapse: your climax is a night of alerts on screens that should be green, and the temptation will be to make it physical. It shouldn't be. The most frightening version is a man watching a dashboard go amber, alone, knowing exactly what each one means, and being the only person in the building who does.

---

**One line to carry forward:** Pilcher already ran the experiment your narrator wants to run. He told a town the truth and lost a third of them, and he has the numbers, and he will show them to your narrator kindly. Then he'll say the sentence that nearly works — *they can't handle the truth, but you can't handle the lie, come inside* — and offer him the professor's old job. And when the narrator refuses and publishes anyway, the Keepers won't come for him. **They'll just stop holding the things they've been holding, and let everyone find out what the fence was for.**
