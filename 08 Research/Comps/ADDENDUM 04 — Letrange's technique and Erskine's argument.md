# Addendum 04 — the two things I had thin, read properly
### *Sekret Machines* bk 1: how Letrange actually works. *Shift*: why they actually did it.

> Read 10 Sep 2026, at Santi's instruction. He was right that these were the two gaps. One of them changes the book's thesis statement — see §2.4.

---

## 1. Letrange — the technique, scene by scene

I had this wrong in the HANDOFF. I had the operative getting cold brutal POV cutaways early. **That is not what Hartley does, and what he does is better.**

### 1.1 The reader is never ahead of Jennifer
Through the whole London sequence the reader is locked in Jennifer's POV. Letrange is charming, spiky, and *plausible*. There is no cutaway warning us. The reader is played exactly as she is.

### 1.2 The four moves, in order

**Move 1 — he insults her father.** *"Don't canonize him, now that he's not around to disappoint you."* She nearly throws a drink.

**Move 2 — he apologises, immediately and well.** *"That was out of line. Perhaps I should leave you to finish your drink in peace."* And this is the actual mechanism: **the apology is what disarms her, not the charm.** She notes it herself — *"the heat was already draining, as if his apology had released the pressure."* A man who provokes and then withdraws gracefully has established that he is safe to be angry at, which is a far deeper trust than being liked.

**Move 3 — the operational question, dressed as concern.** *"Who else have you told?"* And then, when she says no one: *"Not even Deacon?"* She feels the alarm and can't name it: *"Was it obvious that she might confide in her dead father's manservant? Would anyone make that guess, or did it suggest Letrange knew more than he'd let on?"* **She notices. She proceeds anyway.** That's the whole thing.

**Move 4 — he gives up a smaller secret to buy a bigger trust.** *"My name is not Letrange."* And produces an Interpol card. He *reveals himself as a liar*, on his own terms, in a controlled way — and it works, because a man who confesses to a deception is assumed to have stopped deceiving.

### 1.3 The tells, all noticed too late, all physical
At the cement works she assembles it in about forty seconds, and every item is behavioural, not evidential:
- he took the car keys (of course he did — but she checked)
- **he went into the shed first, ahead of her.** *"He'd been so attentive in the city, but here, it was like he'd wanted to reach the shed before her."*
- **he never asked her to bring the bag with the drive in it** — which is wrong if the meeting is real
- and then: **he used her first name for the first time.** *"He called to her from inside the shed. He didn't come to the door and look out… It was the first time he had used her first name. And then she knew."*

**A change in address is the tell.** Not a slip of fact — a slip of *register*. That is enormously better than what I had planned for ch. 22 and I am changing it (§4).

### 1.4 How the reader finally learns — and it isn't a villain POV
The chapter after the shooting is **Saltzburg's** POV. Letrange is on the phone, being dressed down by his boss:

> *"There's nothing more to say," said Letrange, made defiant by failure. "She got away."*

Four things this does at once, at almost no cost:
1. Confirms he's theirs.
2. **Shows the organisation by showing its contempt for its own operative.** Saltzburg is bored by him. *"Other operatives will tidy up your mess."*
3. Establishes there are many more of them.
4. Gives the villain a *superior* — which is what makes an organisation feel real. A lone operator is a slasher; a man being told off on the phone is an institution.

Saltzburg then does the other thing worth stealing: he tells an anecdote about a hobby-horse and his father beating his mother, entirely calmly, to make a point about **how buried memories surface**. It's horrible, it's characterising, and it's *plot* — it's about what Jennifer might remember. Villain interiority delivered as an anecdote about furniture.

### 1.5 What I'm changing in our book
- **Cut the two early cutaways** (the lock-up, the Zug office) that show the operative being cold before James meets him. They telegraph, and they hand the reader a superiority over James that the London sequence proves you don't need.
- **Keep one cutaway, moved late**, and make it *Saltzburg-shaped*: not his POV, but the POV of the person handling him, in which he is an employee who has failed. Place it after ch. 22.
- The reader therefore rides with James — Option A in structure — but gets the **click** at the BBQ (ch. 21) from *inside James's head*, missed by James and caught by the reader. That preserves Santi's Option C feeling (the reader is ahead, briefly, agonisingly) without a single villain-POV chapter before the reveal.
- **Dave's four moves in ch. 18/21/22 are Letrange's four moves, in order.** Provoke, apologise, ask the operational question dressed as concern, confess a small lie.

## 2. Erskine — the argument, and what it fixes

### 2.1 The setup
Donald, half-mad and being managed, is taken aside at a funeral by a *kindly stranger* — and notices he's being managed: *"He wondered if he'd been set up to have a kindlier man, a stranger, take him aside and tell him what Thurman thought he needed to hear. It was hard not to be paranoid about being manipulated, not to feel the strings knotted to his joints."*

**That is exactly the second-tier Keeper in our ch. 20**, and the note to steal is that **James should notice he's being handled and take the meeting anyway**, and say so.

### 2.2 The reveal that reframes everything backwards
Donald has spent a book believing he was *poisoned* in that chamber. Erskine: *"We were healing you."*

The most frightening sentence in the trilogy, and it's four words of good news. Every protective act was a **selection**. The reader retroactively reinterprets a hundred pages.

**For us:** the Keepers' generosity is the same shape. The digitisation grants, the conservation, the open access, the beautifully catalogued archive — none of it is destruction disguised as help. **It is help, and the selection is the crime.** James should have this inversion land in ch. 29: he walks in expecting a vault of stolen things and finds a *preservation* facility, run by people who love records, doing genuinely excellent work — for a list of people he isn't on.

### 2.3 Why the solution isn't worse than the problem
Donald asks the reader's question directly. Erskine's answer is **not** "people can't handle the truth." It's narrower and it's about attribution:

> *"Imagine the first discovery that one of these epidemics was man-made — the panic, the violence that would ensue. That's where the end would come. A typhoon kills a few hundred people… we come together. We put the pieces back. But a terrorist's bomb does the same damage, and it throws the world into turmoil."*
>
> **"When there's only God to blame, we forgive him. When it's our fellow man, we destroy him."**

And the supporting examples are all about **agency, not magnitude**: modified food versus selective breeding, vaccines versus natural immunity, cloning versus twins. Same outcome, different author, completely different human response.

### 2.4 **This is our thesis, and it was sitting in our book already**

The Keepers do not delete catastrophes. They delete **hands**.

*transmitted to the early* → *arisen among the first*
*techniques we have not yet reconstructed* → *doubtless simpler than they appear*
*a tradition of instruction* → *a tradition*

Every one of those edits performs Erskine's operation. It takes an event with an author and returns an event with no author. **It moves the record from "our fellow man" to "only God."**

Which means the Keepers' argument, when James finally hears it in ch. 30, is not *we lie to keep you calm.* It is:

> *A population that believes it was given something believes it can be taken away — and goes looking for who took it. A population that believes it built everything itself, alone, in a straight line, has nobody to blame when the lights go out, and rebuilds instead of hunting. We did not delete the flood. We deleted the hand. You have no idea how many people that has kept alive.*

That is a genuinely good argument. It is the best thing in the reading programme, it is *already* what our page 118 does, and until this read I did not know why our own central edit was the right edit.

**Ch. 30's counter-argument (Juliette's) survives intact and gets sharper:** *you didn't spare us the panic. You spared us the search. And the search is the only thing that has ever found anything.*

### 2.5 Two smaller ones
- **The motive is a daughter in a box.** Erskine's justification is one pod with his only child in it: *"When I found nanos in her blood, I knew this was the right thing to do."* Every Keeper argument in our ch. 30 must be attached to one specific person, not to humanity.
- **"It only takes one of them to get lucky."** Erskine on why patching is hopeless. That's the Keepers on why *one* honest historian is an unacceptable risk — and it explains why they act on drawings, not opinions.

---

**One line to carry forward:** Letrange wins Jennifer's trust by apologising, asking who else she's told, and confessing to a lie of his own — and she is undone not by evidence but by a change of address. Erskine explains that they buried the world because when there's only God to blame we forgive him, and when it's our fellow man we destroy him. **Our Keepers have spent a hundred years quietly moving the record from the second column to the first, and page 118 is one line of that work.**
