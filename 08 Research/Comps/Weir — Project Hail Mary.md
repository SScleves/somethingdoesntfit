---

## 1. What it is

A junior-high science teacher wakes from a coma on a spacecraft with amnesia and two dead crewmates, and has to work out who he is, where he is, and what he's supposed to do — by experiment. Interleaved with flashbacks to how Earth got there: a solar-dimming microbe, and a global authority figure named Stratt who commandeers whatever she needs.

It's a book about a man alone in a room with a problem, and it's a page-turner. That's the trick you need.

---

## 2. The mechanism worth stealing — Weir's actual procedure, in order

I read the Astrophage sequence closely because it's the purest example. Here's what he does, step by step. **This is a template, not an inspiration.**

### 2a. The reasoning is elimination, stated out loud, and the options are finite.

> *"Whatever their navigation system was, it was based on light. What else could you use in space? There's no sound. No smell. It would have to be light, gravity, or electromagnetism. And light's the easiest of those three to detect."*

Three candidates, named. One chosen, with a reason. The reader can follow and — crucially — can *disagree*. Compare: Dan Brown's character simply knows a thing about symbols and the plot moves. Weir's character narrows a field in public.

**Your version:** when the narrator hits the page-118 discrepancy, he must name the possible explanations *out loud and completely* — a printer's error, a second edition, a translation variant, a misremembering, a deliberate change — and then eliminate them one at a time, on the page, by doing things. The reader must be able to think "wait, what about a reprint?" and find the narrator already there.

### 2b. **The first two experiments fail.** This is the whole thing.

Grace tries three ways to work out how Astrophage navigate:

1. **A white LED in a dark box.** Nothing. → Conclusion drawn from the failure: *it's not sheer brightness, or they'd have gone for it.*
2. **A bulb tuned to 462°C — Venus's actual temperature, its infrared signature.** Nothing. → Conclusion: *it's not thermal IR.*
3. **A filter emitting carbon dioxide's spectral lines, 4.26 and 18.31 microns.** They vanish off the slide instantly.

Two failures, each of which *narrows the field*, and then a success. Between failures he says *"What do you want from me?!"* and throws his goggles on the floor.

**This is the most important paragraph in this document.** A negative result that teaches something is not a delay — it is the thing that makes the positive result *earned*, and it is the reason the reader trusts the character. **Da Vinci Code has a 100% hit rate on first attempts.** That is precisely why it feels greased. Your narrator must be wrong, in public, at least twice per act, and each wrongness must delete a possibility.

Build a rule into the Guardrails: **no conclusion arrives on a first attempt.** If a test works first time, it's because it's a *setup* for a later failure.

### 2c. The anomaly that shouldn't be possible gets chased, not smoothed over.

> *"The first thing that bothered me: CO2 spectral emissions are 4.26 and 18.31 microns. But Astrophage are only 10 microns across, so it couldn't really interact with light that had a larger wavelength. How could it even see the 18.31 micron band? ... It should be impossible for Astrophage to interact with a wavelength that big. I mean… literally impossible!"*

He has his answer. It works. And he stops, because one number doesn't fit — and the not-fitting is the door to the next discovery.

**Your book is called *Something Doesn't Fit*.** Weir has written you the demonstration of what that title means as a *practice* rather than a mood. The narrator's defining habit should be: **he cannot leave a discrepancy alone even when the discrepancy is inconvenient to a conclusion he likes.** That's the character, that's the title, and it's also — pointedly — the exact opposite of what the Keepers count on everyone doing.

### 2d. The unexplained sensation, planted early, resolved late.

Early on, before Grace knows he's in space, he falls off a ladder and:

> *"Something bugs me about how I fell off the ladder. I replay it in my head. I can't put my finger on it, but there's just a…'wrongness' to it."*

He can't name it yet. The reader can't either. It's the gravity — he's under thrust, not on Earth. It pays off much later.

**This is the Howey "fifty times more fibre optic" beat done properly**: the anomaly is *felt*, *flagged*, and *unresolved*, and the book returns to it. Howey dropped his in a subordinate clause and never came back. Weir plants and harvests.

**Give your narrator two or three of these**, all in his professional register: a graph that settles too smoothly, an alert that should have fired, a latency figure that's wrong in a way he can feel before he can name. Flag them. Come back.

### 2e. Deduction about *himself* from trace evidence.

Before he knows his own name, he works out his nationality:

> *"Ten-foot ladder. I think in imperial units. That's a clue. I'm probably American. Or English. Or maybe Canadian... I ask myself: How far is it from L.A. to New York? My gut answer: 3,000 miles. A Canadian would have used kilometers. So I'm English or American. Or I'm from Liberia."*

Then the joke lands off the method itself: *"I know Liberia uses imperial units but I don't know my own name. That's irritating."*

**Two things here, and both are for you.**

**One:** the humour is *generated by the method*, never bolted onto it. He doesn't stop being rigorous to make a joke — the rigour *is* the joke, because it's absurdly thorough about a trivial thing while the big thing stays unknown. That's your answer to "balance of humour and hard sci-fi": you don't balance them, you make one produce the other. Same with naming the specimens Larry, Curly, Moe — the humour comes from a lonely man's relationship with his problem, and it also does characterisation, and it also makes three identical cells trackable for the reader. **One device, three jobs. That's the standard.**

**Two — and this is the answer to your golf question, see §4.**

### 2f. The amnesia is a permission structure. You need your own.

Weir gave his protagonist amnesia for one reason: it licenses a competent adult to derive everything from first principles, on the page, without the reader asking "wouldn't he already know that?" It is a *machine for making explanation dramatic.*

**Your narrator's equivalent is already in your book and you should use it much harder: he is a foreigner and an outsider to the field.** He doesn't read Dutch well. He isn't an academic. He has never held a rare book. He doesn't know how a second impression differs from a second edition, what a printing signature is, how paper is dated, what a notary actually does. **Every one of those is a thing he must find out by doing, and every one is a chapter where the reader learns something checkable alongside him.**

That's not a weakness to be written around. It's the engine. Weir's man doesn't know his own name; yours doesn't know how books are made. Both have to build the knowledge in front of us, and in both cases the building *is* the plot.

### 2g. Self-review as an explicit act.

> *"I should have shared all my findings with the rest of the science community, but I wanted to be sure. Peer review may have fallen by the wayside, but at least I could self-review. Better than nothing."*

He names the standard he's failing to meet, and then constructs the best substitute available. **Your narrator, alone, with no one to check his work, must do this consciously** — and it's also the thematic spine: the Keepers' whole method depends on there being no one left to check. A man checking his own work, badly, alone, is the smallest possible resistance and the book's entire moral content.

### 2h. Stratt — the authority figure who is not a villain.

The woman running the global response commandeers labs, ignores nations, and says things like *"Because I don't have time for this bullshit. We are building a ship to literally save our species."* She's ruthless, correct, and never made evil.

**Useful for your first-tier Keepers.** The scariest version is not a cabal enjoying itself — it's a competent person with a deadline who has stopped considering whether they're allowed. Rushkoff's men, Howey's Thurman, and Stratt are the same character at three temperatures. Yours should be nearer Stratt than nearer a cabal.

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The chirpy default.** Grace's voice is relentlessly upbeat — exclamation marks, "little brats," "Craaaaaap." It works because his situation is absurd and he's a middle-school teacher. **Your narrator is nine months into grief and his voice cannot be this.** Take Weir's *structure* of humour (generated by method, never at the expense of stakes) and set its temperature far lower. Your funny should be dry, deflected, and occasionally cruel to himself. Watts's coldness with Weir's clarity, not Weir's bounce.
- **Everything is solvable.** In PHM every problem yields to sufficient cleverness. Yours must not: some things the narrator tests come back *inconclusive*, and he has to act anyway. That's the difference between a puzzle novel and a novel.
- **The alien friend.** Rocky supplies companionship, a second competence, and the emotional payload. You can't have one, and reaching for an equivalent — a plucky ally, a hacker, a sympathetic archivist who does the hard parts — would wreck the book. **Buddy is your Rocky.** A dog who can't help, can't be told, and is going to die of old age. That's harder and better.
- **The flashback structure.** Weir alternates ship/Earth to feed exposition. You've got HEART/LADDER/INNER/CLOCK doing similar work. Fine — but Weir's flashbacks exist to explain, and yours should exist to *cost*. Don't let a memory of Ana become a delivery mechanism for backstory.
- **The stakes ladder.** All of humanity, from page one. Yours is one man, one dog, and a sentence in a book. **Do not inflate.** Your Book B decision — big story, small evidence — is right, and Weir is the proof that a book about one man in a room testing things can carry any size of implication, so long as the testing is real.

## 4. The two questions you actually asked

### "Is the golfing too much, too goofy, out of place?"

**No — but it's currently doing the wrong job, and that's why it feels goofy.**

Right now golf reads as a quirk: a thing the protagonist does, character-colour, a place to put the man at the third tee. Quirks are goofy. Weir's book has no quirks — it has *instruments*. Larry, Curly and Moe are funny **and** they're how you tell three identical cells apart. The imperial-units riff is funny **and** it's how he learns something true about himself.

So: keep the golf, and make it load-bearing. Some options, in order of my preference:

1. **Golf as the measurement discipline.** It is the one sport that is entirely a record-keeping exercise: a card, a number per hole, a handicap that is a *statistical construct*, a course rating, strokes measured against an expected value. **Your narrator is a monitoring expert who plays a game that is fundamentally about baselines and deviations.** A man who notices that a sentence changed between impressions is the same man who notices his playing partner's handicap doesn't reconcile with his scores. Golf stops being goofy the moment it is the *same habit of mind* as the job and the investigation. Three things, one character.
2. **Golf as the place the grief is visible.** It's what he and Ana did, or what he did while she was ill, or the thing he kept doing because stopping would be an admission. Played alone, badly, in Dutch drizzle, with an old dog in the car. That's not goofy — that's devastating.
3. **Golf as the social membrane.** A club is where a foreigner is let in without being let in, and it's a completely plausible place for a man like the tee man to be near him without either of them arranging it. That's already how you're using it, and it works.

**What makes it goofy** is only this: if the golf is comic relief, or if the reader can feel the author enjoying it more than the character does. Cut any golf scene that isn't doing one of the three jobs above. My guess is you lose two scenes and the rest gets stronger.

**One warning:** if the narrator is *good* at golf, he becomes a competence fantasy. He should be mediocre and precise about it — a man who knows his own numbers exactly and isn't impressive.

### "What to redo in the four completed chapters"

I want to read chapters 1–4 against these findings before I give you a list — that's the next thing I'll do after the last two sources, and it deserves its own document rather than a paragraph here. But the criterion is now clear, and you can apply it yourself in the meantime:

**In every scene, ask: does the narrator find something out by doing something?** Not by being told, not by remembering, not by noticing in a way that requires him to be unusually perceptive — by *doing*. If a chapter has no attempt, no method and no result, it is atmosphere. Atmosphere is allowed in chapter 1. It is not allowed twice.

## 6. The source's own failures

- **The prose is functional at best.** Weir is a superb *explainer* and an unremarkable stylist. You've named Watts alongside him, and Watts is where the sentences come from. Take Weir's transparency of process and Watts's compression and coldness. Neither alone gets you the book you described.
- **Emotional range is narrow.** The one genuinely moving thing in PHM is Rocky, and Rocky is a rock alien who says "amaze." Everything human — the dead wife equivalent, the guilt of how Grace ended up on the ship — is handled in a paragraph and moved past. **Your book's centre is exactly the thing Weir can't do.** A marriage being quietly lied to, a dog running out of time, and a man who said yes at eighty-five percent. Don't let the method chapters push it out: your risk is that the investigation becomes so satisfying to write that the HEART thread thins.
- **The world outside the room is cardboard.** Nations, politicians, the public — all functionaries. You cannot afford that, because your antagonists *are* the world outside the room.
- **The coincidence engine.** Grace happens to be the one man alive with the specific heresy needed (life without water), and happens to be the sole survivor, and happens to meet an alien with complementary skills. Weir gets away with it via pace. **You will not get away with it**, because your book's contract with the reader is that things check out. Every convenience in your plot has to be paid for with a plausible mechanism — which is, incidentally, exactly what your Keepers would say about the List.

---

**One line to carry forward:** Weir's secret is not that his characters are clever — it's that they are *wrong first, on the page, twice, and learn something specific from each wrongness.* The greased feeling you hate in Da Vinci Code comes entirely from a 100% first-attempt hit rate. Make failure informative and the reader will follow your narrator anywhere, including into a room with a sentence in a book. And the golf isn't too goofy — it's just not load-bearing yet. Make it the same habit of mind as the job: baselines, deviations, and a number that has to reconcile.
