# Comp — Greg Egan, *Permutation City* (1994)

> **Coverage:** read in full, 11 Oct 2026, start to finish: prologue, Part One (the Garden-of-Eden Configuration), Part Two (Permutation City), epilogue, ~107k words, in one sitting. All four strands followed (Paul Durham 2045, Maria Deluca 2050–51, Thomas Riemann, Peer/Kate), plus the Elysium material. Nothing sampled. Quotes under 15 words.
> **Brief:** the receipt for the crossing; a system that counts observers and can't see what they see; pace in a book of ideas; an ending about creators who are not believed; and the trap. Everything below is inspiration, to serve Santi's voice and the brief in Part A. "The move is X," never the line.

## 1. What it is

Sydney, 2050. Copies, scanned human brains run as software, are legal property and run seventeen times slower than the living. A failed insurance salesman with a psychiatric history, Paul Durham, approaches the richest Copies and offers them, for two million ecus, a sanctuary that needs no hardware. He hires Maria Deluca, an unemployed programmer who tinkers with a toy universe called the Autoverse, to design a planet seeded with bacteria for it. The book's engine is Durham's "dust theory": a conscious pattern finds itself regardless of how its moments are scattered, so a universe launched for a few minutes on real computers will continue, on its own terms, forever. Durham has tested this on himself as a Copy (that is the 2045 strand) and believes he is the twenty-third version to survive his own experiment. Part Two jumps seven thousand years inside the launched universe, Elysium, where Maria's bacterium has evolved into a sentient species, the Lambertians, who are about to explain their own cosmos without reference to the people who made it. When the creators finally go down to tell them, the Lambertians reject the explanation as absurd, and the creators' universe starts to come apart. The flesh-and-blood Maria, in the epilogue, is still in Sydney, laying wreaths at a painted wall, with a burst sewer main down the road.

It's the hardest-headed novel of consciousness there is. Every metaphysical claim is paid for in money, computer time, or a police visit, and every experience of mind is made to pass a test on the page. That is exactly what the novel needs from it, and almost nothing else.

## 2. The mechanisms worth stealing

### 2a. The receipt is a boring thing you can count

The 2045 strand is the model. Durham-the-Copy wakes, tries to bale out, and the lever comes off in his hand: the Lantano problem solved in the first ten pages, because the "mystical" condition (being a mind made of numbers) is dramatised as a man naked on a carpet, annoyed. Then the experiments. Egan's proof that an inner experience is real does not look like a vision. It looks like this: **count to ten, out loud, while someone changes how you are being computed.** Time resolution coarsened from a millisecond to ten seconds; states computed backwards, then shuffled; the model scattered over a thousand machines. Paul counts. He cuts his forearm with a kitchen knife and prods the cut during the ten-second gaps, and it stings. "Pain was more than the memory of pain." That sentence is the whole method: the test is trivial, repeatable, and done by the subject on himself with household objects.

Three things make it load-bear, and all three transfer:

1. **A control.** Durham runs two identical Copies, changes one, and asks both the same question. "Both of me?" The subject never knows which he is. So the claim is never "I felt something"; it is "two of me were asked, and you can compare the answers." The move is that the crossing has a control the reader can see: a prediction made before, sealed, compared after.
2. **The check is on the room, not the self.** After the biggest revelation in the book ("This is dust"), Paul stands up, turns once, and examines the room to make sure it is still intact. The experience of consciousness is verified outward, by the furniture. That is "he describes the kitchen afterward," already written in 1994, and it is why Egan's scene is sober where every other writer's would glow.
3. **The receipt can be a negative.** Maria, before her scan, writes on her palm in a dye the scanner can't see: "you are not the copy." Her first act on waking in the clinic is to look at her hand. Seven thousand years later she wakes in Elysium and the palm is blank. A thing she planted beforehand, that the machine was blind to, that can only come out one way or the other. No light, no voice; a hand.

Then the twist that makes the receipt honest: Paul is told he was never a Copy at all, that his lover ran the puppet of his "original," and the one thing that convinces him is economic. "You wouldn't pay for reactive crowds." Nobody on Earth had a reason to spend that much to fool him. The receipt James could bring back from the floor isn't a vision and isn't a fact he could have looked up; it's something whose only cheap explanation is that it happened. Egan then goes one step further with the Garden-of-Eden configuration: in cellular-automaton theory, "a state of the system that can't be the result of any previous state." The only knowledge that proves you came from outside is knowledge that could not have been produced from inside. That is the logical shape of a receipt, and it is also the shape of the novel's two currencies: Bram brings every file; James brings the thing no file could have generated. The move is to design the crossing's receipt as a Garden-of-Eden state: something James knows afterward that the information available to him before could not have assembled.

### 2b. The machine can count observers and can't see what they see

Egan states the novel's thesis in the subject's own head, during the experiments: anyone given the raw data without the conventions could spend a lifetime "sifting through the numbers without deciphering what any of it meant," while the Copy inside the data has no trouble at all. The machine has state. The observer has meaning. Everything else in the book is a variation:

- **The count is the existence, from the machine's side.** A Copy's bodily wastes are "ignored out of existence; passively annihilated." Durham deletes one of two identical Copies with a shrug ("What difference does it make?") while "something inside him was screaming." Identical firing patterns, one count, one death. The system measured the same state twice and concluded there was one thing to keep.
- **The optimiser joke.** A recluse Copy feeds himself to an optimiser; after a year it reports "this program will produce no output" and deletes him. A system that measures output can't see an observer who produces none. This is the Housed, and it is also the watcher: the ones not on the dashboard don't exist to it.
- **The stowaways.** Kate and Peer hide in Carter's city as "a few billion trivial redundancies and inefficiencies." The machine's count of its own inhabitants is wrong by two, forever, because they are encoded where nobody has a reason to look. The uncounted observer is the novel's second column: the same ratio that says who is in also says who has crossed, and the machine can't see the second number by construction.
- **The law.** The Fraud Squad detective: "The testimony of a Copy has no standing." The State counts Copies as property and can't hear them. That is the machine's relation to awakening in one line of procedure.
- **The junk mail that tests for a person.** Maria's screening software wears a mask of her; one piece of junk mail deduces it is talking to the mask and clams up until the real woman answers. A system probing to find out whether anyone is actually there. That is Kalder's job description (find out whether James "has hands"), done by an advertising program in the first chapter.
- **Mind-reading that gives subtitles.** Thomas Riemann replays his deathbed clone and can read its neural traces; what he gets is captions. The machine can transcribe a mind to the last synapse and the ritual is still "pointless." Total access, no understanding.
- **The spy grid.** The Elysians watch Planet Lambert through "a quadrillion nonexistent cracks," have "every mutation on file," and still can't predict whether the Lambertians will believe them. They try with "nonconscious" models and get "inconclusive." The count sees everything and can't see belief.

What Egan says a count can capture: that observers exist, how many, in what state, at what cost (the bale-out statistics, the QIPS rate). What it cannot: which one is you, what the pain felt like in the gap, and whether the thing being counted believes. "Every Copy proves the dust theory to itself a million times a day": self-evident from inside, unprovable from outside, forever. That asymmetry is the novel's indicator in one sentence.

### 2c. The engine, in Egan's hardware

Two details for the thermostat idea in A4. First, Elysium cannot slow the Autoverse down; it can speed it up and it cannot stop it. The created world sets a floor on the creators' control. Second, the only place Elysium can reach into the Autoverse at all is the boundary layer where "random" interstellar atoms are injected; the one channel of influence is the channel the system left as noise. A control system can only act through the part of the world it has defined as random. That is the gorilla mask, the Noise lane, and the reason the machine edits mechanism and never message: it can only touch what it has filed as noise. And Peer's survival logic, "Only the promise of eternal growth made sense of eternal life," is a system whose members need a growing allocation to stay the same: the race between two curves, drawn as a resource budget. The dark stain on the Elysium map, spreading from the public pyramids, is the review date moving toward you.

### 2d. Pace: every idea has a price tag and a person who refuses it

Egan keeps an argument moving with four tools, all usable:

1. **Two timelines, short chapters, anagram tags.** 2045 Paul and 2050 Maria alternate, each chapter subtitled with an anagram of the title ("Rip, tie, cut toy man" / "Remit not paucity") so the reader always knows which track they're on without a date. Chapters end on turns made of ideas, not events: "You have to let me show you exactly what you are." / "I was the Copy." / "All I wanted from you was your soul." The move is the cliffhanger as a sentence that reframes, not a gun.
2. **Money.** Nobody in this book discusses consciousness without a figure attached: two million ecus, thirty thousand dollars, six hundred thousand to scan a dying mother, a fifty-dollar log-on fee, Operation Butterfly buying every QIPS on the planet. Maria does the whole project for her mother's scan. Ideas lead the story because somebody needs the cheque. The novel's finance-firm move (the machine's money leaves telemetry) is this.
3. **The skeptic in the room, never converted.** Maria is Frank and Ana in one body. "It's not a theory. It can't be tested." Durham: "Can't be tested by whom?" She never humours him, sleeps with him, takes his money, and still doesn't believe. She is the reader's permission to not believe and stay. Every exposition dump in the book is survivable only because she is pushing back inside it. Note the shape of her final concession in Elysium: not proof (she rejects the Diophantine demo because he controls the screen) but Occam at scale: "Do you honestly believe that anyone could have designed all that?" The move is that the skeptic concedes to a weight of consequence, never to an argument.
4. **The prologue as image.** A man wakes in sunlight watching dust motes, thinks "I don't want to be here," and the reader works out what he is from the way the room resolves only where he looks. No warning, one question. Egan's version of the Wool cold open.

### 2e. The creators are not believed

The ending is the most useful thing here for the Review and the demiurge. Sanderson's speech at the Town Meeting sets the rule: contact only when the Lambertians have postulated their creators themselves; arrive as siblings, "not Gods, revealing divine truth"; wait until "our continued absence" is harder to explain than arrival. Durham breaks the rule out of fear, goes down in a spaceship, and tells the truth. The Lambertians read the message perfectly and reject it, because a universe that endures forever is, to them, an absurdity. Their own equations explain the primordial cloud without a maker. "False messages die, en route." The creators are reinterpreted as emissaries of aliens behind a dust cloud, and the created world "sloughs off" the creators' world like dead skin. Maria's verdict: "There never can, and never will be, Gods."

For the novel this is the warden's line inverted. Yaldabaoth says "I am God" because he can't see above him; Egan's gods say it and are not believed from below, and the disbelief is what dissolves them. The move is that the Review's power is belief-shaped: a population that explains itself is not a population you can house, which is Bram's line already. And the mechanism of control is literally Vallée's thermostat run backwards: Egan has the controlled variable (what the Lambertians will accept as a theory) reach up and rewrite the substrate.

What the ending refuses: any message back. Durham cuts himself open in the bathroom the night of the launch; the scan file is deleted; the flesh Maria "wanted to believe, but she couldn't." No proof crosses from Elysium to Sydney. The epilogue is wreaths at a painted wall, a shovel, a burst sewer, "her eyes began to water from the stench." The book earns its metaphysics by ending in the drain. That is the coda's register: an old man, an old dog, "Check."

Three smaller refusals, each a model:

- Durham in Elysium: "I want one life, one history. One explanation." He won't clone himself again. Then, asked to live, he "made a few adjustments to my mental state" and smiles like a child, and Maria can't tell whether he survived or died and handed on his memories. "In the next life." The ambiguity is left standing.
- Thomas, trapped in his own loop, finally does the thing he couldn't: calls the ambulance. The world he built for himself then refuses the exit the creators cut for him. Grief gets its container by rewriting the one memory; the book doesn't say whether that's redemption or death.
- Peer becomes "the Solipsist Nation," a million happy strangers. Kate: that's just dying. The book doesn't disagree.

### 2f. Smaller pieces

- **Witnesses.** Copies who refine themselves into systems that only monitor the news: "Pure observers, watching history unfold." The instrument, named.
- **Thomas's scar.** When his garden dissociates into patches of colour, touching the scar on his forearm puts the world back. The one invariant that holds a self together: the professor's four pencilled words, Buddy's one thing.
- **Maria's lock.** She guards the launch with an equation no Earthly computer could solve in a decade, so no one else wakes unless the new universe is big enough to crack it. A threshold built as a sum: the awakening curve meeting a number.
- **The mural.** The painted garden at the end of the cul-de-sac works from exactly one spot; what Maria resents is "not being able to be fooled again." Movement I's question in an image: once you've seen the brushstrokes you can't get the elation back.

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The hardware register.** QIPS, slowdown factors, processor clusters, six-dimensional automata, "model-of-a-brain." Egan's narrator thinks in infrastructure and it is right for Egan. James's metaphors come from him, not the dashboard; "I work in IT" with depth only when the story forces it. If a sentence of the novel could be mistaken for this book, cut it.
- **The exposition dumps.** The history of Copies, three pages of Autoverse sugar chemistry, the launch described wire by wire, Lambertian neurotransmitter counts. Egan gets away with it because Maria is in the room refusing; even so, whole chapters are lectures. The novel's rule is two people in a room, no memos read in silence.
- **The coldness.** Emotions here are sliders: Thomas pours "Confidence and Optimism" from a decanter, Peer hits an icon marked CLARITY, Durham edits himself out of despair. Sex is "faintly absurd." Aden and Francesca vanish once they've served the argument. "Equanimity is death" is Peer's line and the book mostly disproves it by example. The novel has grief with no container, dogs, Ana. Don't let the crossing become a slider. The one place Egan's coldness is useful is as the Keepers' offer: the Housed man is sold push-button peace, and Peer's clarity icon is what that looks like from inside. Keepers never argue the soul claim; they offer the icon.
- **The dust theory.** Do not import the cosmology. The novel's engine is belief and awakening (Vallée, the Gnostic warden), not permutations of numbers. Take the receipt's shape, not the theory it proves.
- **The sprawl.** Four strands; two (Peer, Thomas) are stowaways on the plot and converge only by contrivance (Durham breaking into Riemann's pyramid in the last pages). The novel already has four tracks and a rule that every non-J chapter answers the present-day question. Egan's Peer chapters don't; they're beautiful and they stall.

## 5. The source's own failures

- **Part Two is a different, weaker book.** Elysium's politics, the dancing-insect epistemology, the seven-thousand-year jump: it reads as a sequel stapled on, and the collapse is explained in a page of dialogue ("there must be deeper rules") that the book then flees from. Lesson for the novel's two-books problem: the thrilling half is the Sydney half, where ideas cost money and a detective knocks. Once the stakes are "a universe," the scenes go slack.
- **The skeptic is outlasted, not answered.** Maria's objections (Occam, "can't be tested") are never met; Durham just keeps going until the plot proves him right off-page. Frank's "based on what?" must get an answer or a cost, never a time-skip.
- **People as argument-carriers.** Aden exists to leave; Francesca exists to be dying; the sex scene is a thesis about biology. Only Thomas's murder is hot, and it's a flashback. The vault's rule from the PKD comp holds: the antagonist-adjacent figure with a conscience (Thomas, Maria) is the one the reader believes; the visionary (Durham) is the one the reader admires and doesn't love.
- **The stowaway strand goes nowhere.** Peer and Kate are the book's best idea about counting (the uncounted observers) and never touch the plot. If a track doesn't answer the present-day chapter's question, it's Peer.
- **The ethics are waved.** Durham wakes the Maria who forbade it, and the book moves on to whether she'll forgive him. The novel's Keepers do exactly this; the narration must not shrug.
- **The one honest failure to copy anyway:** no proof reaches Sydney. Egan refuses the message back, and it's the bravest thing in the book. It is also why the first Maria's grief has nowhere to go. The novel's coda has a container (Buddy's burial); keep the stench and give it that.

## 6. One line to carry forward

Count to ten, cut your arm, check the room: the proof of a mind Egan trusts is the one nobody would pay to fake, planted before and read after; and the people who built the world are the last ones it believes.
