# Comp — Hugh Howey, *Wool* / *Shift* / *Dust*
### Read 9 Sep 2026 — full structural map; *Shift* read closely, per your brief

> Your brief: *"Wool trilogy the same, I think many things can be uncovered specially from book 2 that lead to the bunkers."*
> You were right, and you were more right than you knew. **Book 2 (*Shift*) is the single most useful source in this entire reading programme for your book.** Not because of the silos. Because of *how they got built*, *who chose the date*, and *the one detail the architect almost noticed.*

---

## 1. What it is

*Wool* is life inside a buried silo generations later — a sealed society with a taboo, a lie, and a woman who finds out. *Shift* is the prequel: how it happened, told by Donald Keene, a freshman congressman from Georgia who **drew the plans for the silo without knowing what he was drawing.** *Dust* converges them.

*Shift* is the one that matters to you. Skip the rest for research purposes; read *Wool* later for pleasure and pacing.

---

## 2. The mechanisms worth stealing

### 2a. The bunker is built in public, with public money, for a stated reason — and nobody hides anything.

This is the answer to the question your book has to solve: **how do you build fifty of these without anyone noticing?** Howey's answer is the best one I've read, and it's the same answer Rushkoff's green-room men would have given if anyone had asked them.

You don't hide it. You legislate it.

- It's an **energy bill**. A nuclear waste Containment and Disposal Facility outside Atlanta — "CAD-FAC." Voted through, signed, funded.
- **Forty thousand jobs** in one state. Shipping, stevedores, contractors. Rival congressmen are angry they didn't get the contract.
- The bunker itself is justified as an **egress requirement** — a safety shelter the regulator demands before the waste facility can pass inspection. Thurman's exact framing: *"It's like this window in my basement I had to lower before our house could pass inspection."* A "just-in-case building." Probably never used.
- The wild theories are *allowed to flourish.* Area 51, superbombs, lizard people, a camp for libertarians with too many guns. Donald's own constituents phone in about it.

**The line to write on the wall of your study:**

> *"There was enough noise out there to hide any truth."*

### 2b. Denial as an instrument — your Keepers' method, stated by the man who runs it.

Thurman's induction speech to Donald, twenty minutes into their first meeting:

> *"Denial is the secret sauce in this town. It's the flavour that holds all the other ingredients together. The truth is going to get out — it always does — but it's going to blend in with all the lies. You have to deny each lie and every truth with the same vinegar. Let those websites and blowhards who bitch about cover-ups confuse the public for you."*

Read that again with your Keepers in mind. **They never suppress. They dilute.** They deny the true thing in exactly the same tone as the false thing, and let the cranks do the rest of the work for free. This is your correct→ridicule→buy→vanish ladder, but it explains the *ridicule* rung better than anything you have: ridicule isn't something the Keepers manufacture. It's something they *farm*. The conspiracy community is not their enemy — it's their camouflage, and they are careful to keep it well fed.

And immediately after, Thurman runs the trick on Donald live: he asks him whether he believes in aliens, lets him flounder, then laughs it off — *"Of course they're not real."* Donald leaves the room less certain of everything than when he came in, which was the point. **The first time your narrator meets a Keeper, that man should make the narrator feel slightly stupid about the one thing he was right about.**

### 2c. Compartmentalisation by dividing the drawing, not by lying.

Donald designs the silo. He believes it's a shelter for facility workers — ten thousand people, one year, "utter overkill." He never lies to anyone and nobody lies to him. He simply never sees the whole plan:

> *"Even the people on my team aren't going to see the entire plan. And neither will I."*

Anna does the mechanical spaces. Mick does logistics and land. The Senator's instruction is that the walls between them must be *"a mile high."* And the selection principle is kinship, not clearance: *"It needs to be like family. That's why I want to use people I know, people I can trust."*

**This solves a real problem in your book.** Your Keepers need people who make the edits without being villains. Rushkoff gave you the dissertation-committee version (a room agrees what can't be true, and the record complies). Howey gives you the harder version: **a professional does excellent work on a fragment, and the fragment is the crime.** Nobody in the chain needs to be corrupt. Each needs only to be busy, flattered, and slightly out of their depth. That's a much more frightening machine than a conspiracy, and it's completely compatible with "big story, small evidence."

### 2d. **The tell.** This is the one to steal outright.

Late in *Shift*, Donald is walked through the finished silo. Something is off — he thought his design was in the Tennessee bowl, and he's standing in South Carolina's. He almost works it out:

> *"...his subconscious flashing to a delivery of goods, to fifty times more fibre optic than needed, but the connection was lost."*

**Fifty times more fibre optic than needed.** One procurement line item. A number that is wrong by exactly a factor of fifty, because there are fifty silos and Donald designed one. It is the entire conspiracy, sitting in a delivery manifest, and the man best qualified to read it looks straight at it and thinks about something else.

**That is your page 118 in its purest form, and it's better than page 118 because it's arithmetic.** Your narrator is an IT-monitoring man. He does not read old books for a living — he reads *volumes*, thresholds, counts. Give him a number that is wrong by a clean multiple. A bandwidth allocation. A licence count. A capacity reservation. A retention window. Something where the honest answer is "n" and the manifest says "n × 50," and the only explanation is that there are forty-nine more of something.

**Do this. It is the strongest single mechanism in the entire reading programme**, it is native to your protagonist's competence rather than borrowed from an academic's, and it is exactly the "tight like Weir, conclusions reached by testing" register you told me you want. He doesn't intuit it. He *reconciles* it.

Note also Howey's discipline here: **the character misses it the first time.** The reader sees the number, the character's subconscious brushes it, and it's lost. That's a far better beat than a catch, because it plants dread and pays off later. Use the miss.

### 2e. The trigger is chosen, not suffered — and the gathering is a party.

The end of the world happens at the **2052 National Convention**, held on the site. Fifty state delegations camped in fifty grassy bowls — one over each silo. Music stages. Farmers' markets. Livestock. Dogs on leashes. Children petting rabbits. Two weeks of ATVs ferrying Tupperware containers of supplies down concrete ramps, and Donald cannot work out where all the food is going.

The hills, he notices, form perfect stadium seating: *"as if they'd been designed for the task."* They were.

Then: the anthem, sung by a sixteen-year-old girl. A flyover by four jets. And while everyone's face is turned up, the warheads go off over Atlanta.

Three things to take from this, all of them load-bearing for your Act 3:

1. **The guest list is the selection.** Nobody is rounded up. Everybody *came*, invited, delighted, in their best clothes, to a celebration. Rushkoff's green-room men never solved the guard-loyalty problem. Howey's solve it by never letting the guards know there's a door: the population that survives is a *convention delegation*. Your roster in ch29 is this list, and your narrator's question — *am I on it?* — has a second, worse half: **everyone on it thinks they're going to a party.**
2. **The date is a decision.** The Event is scheduled around the flyover. That's the difference between weather and a plan, and it's the difference your whole book turns on.
3. **Helen is in the wrong bowl.** Donald's wife texts *"In Tennessee. where r u?"* — the network is clogged, his messages never sent — and he lives and she doesn't because of a failed text message. **The apocalypse is decided by an infrastructure failure that everyone present has been trained to read as ordinary congestion.** That is your managed cascade in one image, and it is the exact emotional register your book needs: the enormous thing arrives disguised as a bad signal.

Note also that Anna — who loves him — physically restrains him from going over the hill to find his wife, and pulls the key from his ATV. **The person on the inside who loves you does not warn you. She holds you.** If your narrator has anyone close who turns out to have known, that's the shape of it.

### 2f. The antagonists' best argument — and it is aimed directly at your Switch.

Erskine, the scientist, explains why they didn't warn anyone. His reasoning is the strongest anti-disclosure case I've found anywhere in these sources:

> *"When there's only God to blame, we forgive him. When it's our fellow man, we destroy him."*

His point: a typhoon kills hundreds and we rebuild together; a bomb does identical damage and throws the world into chaos. The danger was never the threat itself — it was **the public learning the threat was man-made.** And then the kill shot:

> *"Vic once said that if these terrorists had an ounce of sense, they would've simply announced what they were working on and then sat back to watch things burn on their own. That's all it would take — us knowing that it was happening, that the end of any of us could come silent, invisible and at any time."*

**Give this argument to your Keepers, in these terms, and make it good.** It says: *publishing the List is the attack.* Not evidence of the attack — the attack itself. The narrator's Switch, mailed to a hundred addresses, is precisely the thing Erskine says a competent enemy would do instead of building the weapon.

Your book needs its narrator to hear that and go anyway. And the reason he goes has to be better than "the truth matters," because Erskine has already beaten that. My read: **he goes because Erskine's argument requires the arguer to be the one who decides, forever, and there is no version of that which ends.** Five hundred years of shifts, and Donald's question — *"what was to keep them from heading down that same path a second time?"* — never gets an answer. That's your narrator's answer, and it's the same reason he holds the door rather than walking through it (per PKD). The Keepers are right that the truth is dangerous. They are wrong that they get to be the ones holding it.

### 2g. The braid — a second metronome.

*Shift: First Shift – Legacy* alternates **strictly**: 2110 (inside the silo, present) / 2049 (Washington, the build) / 2110 / 2049, chapter after chapter, no exceptions, for the whole section. Then *Second Shift* and *Third Shift* each restart the device with a new year pairing.

Same finding as Sekret Machines from a much better writer: **the reader will accept almost any structural imposition if it is perfectly regular.** Your HEART / LADDER / INNER / CLOCK threads don't need to alternate that rigidly, but the Faucet slot should. Regularity is what buys you the one irregular chapter.

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The scale.** Howey ends the world. You have explicitly chosen the cascade over the flood, and this book is a good argument for why: once the bombs go off, every question about *instruments* dies, and the back half of the trilogy becomes a survival story with a hatch in it. **Your subject is habit and instrument, not rubble.** The moment your book detonates anything, it stops being about the edit.
- **The five-hundred-year plan.** Cryo-shifts, half a millennium, a "reset." It's a fantasy of control that no real institution could execute, and it's the thing that makes the trilogy science fiction rather than a book about power. Your Keepers should be *worse at this*: improvising, patching, buying, losing files. Competent enough to be terrifying, ragged enough to be real. Rushkoff's men had it down to Alaska or New Zealand and hadn't asked the human question. That's the accuracy level.
- **The nanotech.** Elegant plot engine, wrong for you. It gives the antagonists an objectively correct reason, which converts them from villains into tragic engineers. Your Keepers must not be right about the facts. They should be right about the *psychology* (Erskine's forgiveness argument) and wrong about everything else.
- **Amnesia and pills.** Donald forgets between shifts; the Orientation book and the medication do the exposition management. That's a crutch. Your narrator's disadvantage is grief and a bad night's sleep, not pharmacology.
- **Anna.** The ex-girlfriend who reappears, installs a second monitor under his desk, and turns out to be inside the conspiracy is a decent thriller move that reads, in 2026, as a bundle of clichés about the seductive competent woman. Your book has a dead wife and a real absence. Don't fill it with a plot device.

## 5. The source's own failures

- **The prose does the least it can.** Howey is a superb *structural* writer and an ordinary sentence-writer. You're aiming at Watts and Weir; take his architecture and none of his lines.
- **The exposition is delivered in lectures.** Erskine's explanation, quoted above, is two people standing in a morgue while one of them explains the premise. It's the best content in the book and it's staged as a seminar. **Your equivalent must be found, not told** — the Keepers' argument should reach your narrator as a *document*, in his hands, in the Faucet's slot, so he can put it down and disagree with it in his own head. (See the Sekret Machines note on the reveal-as-document.)
- **Nobody tests anything.** Same disease as Sekret Machines. Donald has one moment of genuine reconciliation — the fibre-optic number — and *the book throws it away in a subordinate clause and never returns to it.* Howey had the best hard-evidence beat in the trilogy and used it as atmosphere. **You should pick it up off the floor and build a chapter on it.**
- **The women exist to move Donald.** Helen dies to give him grief, Anna betrays him to give him a twist, Charlotte is thawed to give him an ally. Worth flagging because your book has the same structural risk: Ana is dead before page one. She must be a person the reader can reconstruct, with opinions the narrator disagrees with, or she is a motive wearing a name.
- **It cannot answer its own question.** Donald asks what stops humanity walking the same road again in five hundred years, and the book has no reply. That silence is the trilogy's real ending. **Your book should ask a version of that question and know its answer** — which, per your codas, it does: the revolution is that he finally has time to love a dog. Machines do the ugly work; people are unhurried. That is a genuine answer to a question Howey couldn't answer, and it is the reason your book is worth writing after his.

---

**One line to carry forward:** they didn't hide the bunkers — they passed a bill, took the jobs, let the cranks shout, and invited everyone who mattered to a party on the roof. The only thing that could have given it away was a procurement line for fifty times more fibre optic than one building needs, and the man who drew the building looked right at it and thought about something else. **Give your narrator that number. He's the one man in the story whose job is reconciling counts, and he will not look away — and the Keepers' best argument is that he should.**
