# Addendum 02 — *Project Hail Mary*
### The method, read properly

## 1. The core discipline I missed: **design the test to discriminate between hypotheses**

My first comp said "two failures before every success." That's true but it's the shallow version. Here's what Grace actually does in the opening.

He feels a "wrongness" when things fall. He drops a test tube off a table and times it — **twenty times**, averaging, explicitly to cancel his own reaction-time error. Result: gravity is 15 m/s², not 9.8.

Now the important part. He has **three** candidate explanations and they are named: a centrifuge on Earth, a centrifuge in space, or another planet. And rather than reason about them, he builds an instrument that **tells them apart**:

> *"I need an accurate accelerometer... And only one thing will do the job: a small piece of string."*

He makes a pendulum. Counts 346 cycles in ten minutes in the lab. Then he carries it **four and a half metres down** to the dormitory and counts again: 346 cycles. Identical.

Why that specific test? Because in a centrifuge, force increases with distance from the centre. **Same reading at two heights kills the centrifuge hypothesis.** Then arithmetic kills the rest: an Earth centrifuge would need a 700-metre radius spinning at 200 mph (he'd hear it); a space centrifuge 1,280 metres (nothing that big exists); no solid body in the solar system has that gravity.

**Each candidate dies by a number. Not by intuition, and not by the plot needing it to.**

**For your book, this is the upgrade on my earlier advice.** When the narrator hits page 118, listing the candidate explanations out loud isn't enough — I said that and it was half the job. He has to **devise the check that separates them.** A reprint, a revised edition, an author's correction, a compositor's error, and a deliberate edit all predict *different observable things*: a corrected typo, a changed copyright line, a reset line-break, a different plate, an announcement in the trade press. He goes looking for the one observation that only one explanation survives.

He already half-does this in ch4 — the three checks (typos, blemish, copyright line) — and it's the best passage in the manuscript. **Make it explicit, and make it the house style.**

## 2. **Calibrate the instrument on a known sample before believing an anomaly**

This one I would never have thought to recommend and it's the most transferable single habit in the book.

Grace scans the alien cylinder with a handheld x-ray spectrometer. It reports pure **xenon** — impossible; xenon is a noble gas that doesn't bond and is a gas at room temperature. So before he believes it:

> *"I use the spectrometer on the steel lab table to make sure it's working correctly. It reports iron, nickel, chromium, and so on. Just what it should say. So I check the cylinder again and get the same wacky results... I test it four more times."*

**He tests his instrument against a case whose answer he already knows, and only then trusts the impossible reading.**

**Your narrator must do this with page 118 and it will be one of the best chapters in the book.** Before he can claim *Waters Before Memory* was silently altered, he has to run his method on a book where he knows the answer: a title with a properly declared corrected second impression. Does his comparison technique detect a change he knows is there? Does it correctly show the typos fixed and the copyright line updated? **The control case is what turns a hunch into evidence** — and it's exactly the sort of unglamorous, patient, slightly obsessive work that defines the man, costs him an evening, and can go wrong.

And it gives you a free gift: **run the control and it might fail.** His method might turn out to be sloppier than he thought. That's your negative result for Act 1, it costs him confidence, and he has to build a better procedure. (Which is Weir's escalation: crude test first, then better instrument.)

## 3. The heresy that gets disproved — and the motive that survives it

I flagged Stratt as the model for a first-tier Keeper. I missed the far more important thing, which is about the *protagonist*.

Grace's career-ending paper argued that life doesn't require liquid water. He was ridiculed out of academia for it. Then he analyses Astrophage and finds it's mostly water:

> *"It means every scientific paper I ever wrote is wrong."*

**He proves himself wrong, publicly, with his own experiment, and reports it.** That's the single strongest character move in the book.

And then Stratt tries to dismiss him — *"Shake it off... I don't have the luxury of leaving samples here just to stroke your bruised ego"* — and he redirects:

> *"Ego?! This isn't about my ego! It's about my children!"*
> *"You don't have children."*
> *"Yes, I do! Dozens of them. They come to my class every day."*

**The motive that survives being wrong is not curiosity and not vindication. It's somebody specific.**

For your narrator: he should be wrong about something he has believed for years — something from the Record, one of his prize anomalies, a did-you-know he has deployed a hundred times — and **he should be the one who disproves it**, and say so, out loud, to someone. It costs him the thing he uses to be interesting at tables.

And what survives is Ana and a dog. Not the truth. **That's your answer to why he keeps going after the Keepers make their best argument** (Pilcher's control group, Erskine's forgiveness) — because the reason was never epistemological.

## 4. Model the other party's information state

Before the Eridians ask for anything, Grace works out what they must need:

> *"Okay. So what would I do if I were them? I'm an Eridian. I want to build a tunnel that connects to the weird human ship. But I don't know what the human ship's material is made of... They'll need a sample of my hull. And they'll need to know it's a sample of my hull."*

So he goes outside and chisels a disc off his own ship **in full view of their telescope**, so that the act itself carries the message.

**This is the discipline your Act 2A counter-investigation needs.** The narrator's question shouldn't only be *what do I know?* It should be *what do they know, what don't they know, and what does their behaviour reveal about which?* When the Keepers touch one of his decoy posts (per the Conspiracy Theory comp), the information isn't in the post — it's in **which one they touched, how fast, and what that implies about what they were already watching for.**

## 5. Reading a system's design intent from its artifacts

Small, repeated, and very much your narrator's register:

- The Petrovascope shows a black disc where the star should be. He infers: *there must be a physical metal plate protecting the sensor.* Then: *"Good design."*
- No decompression step before EVA → the whole ship runs at 40% atmosphere → *"Good design"* again, and he works out why (nobody's aborting to Earth, so why carry the pressure?).
- The lab has tables, chairs and test-tube trays — furniture that only makes sense with gravity — on a ship that will spend years weightless. **The inconsistency is the clue**, and it leads him to the centrifuge.
- The stopwatch has an unpulled battery tab. Everything is unworn. *"Brand-spanking-new... Not sure what to make of that."* Noted, filed, unresolved.

**Your narrator reads systems for a living, and the thing he's actually good at is inferring intent from configuration.** Every artifact in the professor's estate — how the books are ordered, what's annotated and what isn't, which volume has no title on the spine, the Borges filed with the science fiction — is a design decision by a dead man. Ch3 already has him noticing the filing error with satisfaction. **He should be able to reconstruct the professor's rule from the shelf order**, be wrong about it once, and correct it later.

## 6. Selection by accident, not merit

The crew were not chosen for excellence. Long medically-induced comas kill or brain-damage almost everyone; a rare genetic marker confers "coma resistance," present in about **1 in 7,000** people.

> *"We wouldn't be able to send the most qualified people. We'd be sending the seven-thousandth most qualified people."*

Grace is aboard because of a junk-DNA sequence, not because he's the best. He says so plainly.

**This solves a problem your book has and I hadn't named.** Why is *this* man the one who finds it? Not because he's the cleverest. Because of an accidental intersection: a hobby of comparing printings, a job spent deciding what counts as an event, a foreigner's outsider ignorance, a grief that made him look up, and a Sunday-market habit. **None of those is a qualification. All of them together are.** State it — the reader will trust the book more, not less, and it inoculates you against the competence fantasy.

## 7. Preparation fails; endurance works

When the engines cut and gravity vanishes, Grace has spent days talking himself through it:

> *"I panic. No amount of mental preparation would have worked. I straight-up panic... I was stupid to think I could just talk myself out of a primal fear."*

He vomits into his own collar to contain it. Then: *"I clench my teeth. I clench my fists... I'm doing something by aggressively doing nothing."*

**The competent man's competence does not extend to his own body.** He is not brave; he simply doesn't stop. That's the register for your narrator's worst night, and it's the opposite of the thriller move.

## 8. Two more, compressed

- **Verification against a database.** He identifies the alien star map by pulling the star catalogue out of the ship's library, plotting it in Excel, and rotating the model 30 degrees until the positions match. **The alien artifact is confirmed by cross-referencing a reference work.** Your narrator will do a version of this — a claim in the professor's papers checked against a catalogue, a census, a shipping register — and the *fit* is what convinces him, not the content.
- **He flags his own bias while still committing.** *"Yes, I'm jumping to a conclusion there... There are a bunch of different things this could mean. But the most likely, in my admittedly biased opinion..."* And, earlier, of a hypothesis he likes: *"Interesting theory. Nothing to back it up, though."* He never pretends a guess is a finding, and he never refuses to guess. That's the exact voice your narrator needs in the Record.

## 9. What this changes

- The synthesis's Rule 1 ("nothing concluded on a first attempt") stands, but the operative rule is sharper: **name the candidates, then build the check that separates them.**
- **Add to the Guardrails: run the control case.** Before believing an anomaly, test the method on something whose answer is already known. This is now the single most under-used move available to your narrator and it is completely native to his job.
- The Weir comp's §4 answer on the golf stands; the §6 note about Weir's narrow emotional range also stands, and reading further only confirmed it — Yáo and Ilyukhina are *told* to us in a paragraph each and then jettisoned.

**Open gap:** I haven't read the Rocky relationship. That's the part that bears on Buddy — a companion who can't be told anything, who can't help in the way you need, and who is the only other presence in the book. If you want that answered properly, say so and I'll finish the novel.

---

**One line to carry forward:** Weir's man doesn't reason his way to the truth — he builds the specific instrument that makes two explanations give different answers, and then he checks the instrument on something he already knows before he believes what it tells him about something he doesn't. **Your narrator has two copies of a book on a kitchen table. Before he trusts what they tell him, he needs a third book whose answer he already knows.**
