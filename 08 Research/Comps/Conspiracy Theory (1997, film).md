# Comp — *Conspiracy Theory* (1997, dir. Richard Donner)
### Short comp — the source is a film, and only one mechanism in it matters. That one is worth a lot.

> Your brief: *"and finally the plot of the movie conspiracy theory."*
> Plot verified against the film's synopsis, 9 Sep 2026. Where the record is thin I've said so rather than filled it in.

---

## 1. What it is

Jerry Fletcher, a New York cab driver, produces a paranoid newsletter with a tiny subscriber list, harangues his fares with theories, and is fixated on Alice Sutton, a Justice Department attorney whose father — a judge — was murdered. Almost everything Jerry believes is nonsense. **One thing isn't**, and the people it's about come for him.

He was a subject of an unofficially continued MKUltra programme run by Dr Jonas, conditioned to kill. He was programmed to kill Alice's father, and didn't — the sight of Alice stopped him, and he befriended the judge instead. The tell that marks a conditioned subject is a compulsion to buy copies of *The Catcher in the Rye* that they never read.

Critics liked the first half and thought it collapsed into a conventional action film. **They were right, and where it collapses is instructive for you.**

---

## 2. The mechanisms worth stealing

### 2a. **The true one is identified by the reaction, not by the evidence.** This is the whole reason to watch it.

Jerry publishes dozens of theories. He has no way to tell which are junk. **The agency tells him, by responding to one.** The signal isn't in his research — it's in *what gets touched.*

**You already have the raw material for this and I don't think you've noticed how much it's worth.** Your revision put forum posts in as a **decoy** for the paper Switch. This film is the argument for promoting that decoy from misdirection to *instrument*:

> The narrator posts several claims. Some true, some plausible, some deliberately wrong. He is a monitoring professional — **he does not watch the posts, he watches the responses.** Which one gets engagement from a fresh account. Which one gets a polite correction from someone who knows too much. Which one quietly stops being indexed. Which one earns him a friendly email.
>
> **He runs a controlled experiment on the Keepers, using himself as the reagent.**

That is Weir's method (§2b of that comp: two failures, each deleting a candidate), applied to people instead of Astrophage, executed with the narrator's actual professional skill — **and it is the answer to "how does he learn anything when the evidence is a single sentence in a book?"** He can't get more evidence. So he stops trying, and starts generating *responses*, which are evidence of a different kind.

Build a chapter on this. It's the best plot mechanism in the entire eight-source read after the fibre-optic manifest, and unlike that one it's active rather than observational — the narrator *does something* and the world answers.

**Warning built into the same idea:** the moment he runs that experiment, he has told them he exists. The film's Jerry is found because he published. Your narrator's cleverest move is also the one that puts him on the board. That's a real cost, and it should be paid in the same chapter.

### 2b. The compulsion as physical evidence.

Alice doesn't believe Jerry until she watches him buy *The Catcher in the Rye* — again — a book he never reads and can't explain. **A behaviour he cannot stop is the proof, and he is the last person able to see it.**

This is the human version of your page-118 edit: a small, repeatable, checkable anomaly that is meaningless alone and damning once you know what it indicates. Note the structure — **the sceptic tests the crank by watching him, not by arguing with him.** If your narrator ever has to convince someone, that's the shape: don't hand them the List, let them watch him do something he can't help doing, or watch the world respond to him.

### 2c. The crank is right about one thing and unbearable about everything else.

Jerry is exhausting, wrong, and unwell. The film's engine is that being a crank and being correct are not mutually exclusive — and that a functioning suppression system *depends* on people assuming they are.

That's your ridicule rung, dramatised: the reason Jerry is safe for years is that he sounds exactly like what he is. And it's the reason the Keepers farm the noise (Thurman: *"let those websites and blowhards confuse the public for you"*).

**For your narrator, this is a trap to walk toward and not into.** He must be at real risk of becoming Jerry — the notebook, the wall, the thing he can't stop talking about at the club — and his defence is not sanity or restraint. It's **method**. The difference between your narrator and Jerry Fletcher is a control group and a written-down negative result. Make that difference visible on the page, early, so the reader knows which kind of book they're in.

### 2d. He didn't do the thing he's accused of, and the reason is love.

Jerry was programmed to kill Alice's father; seeing Alice stopped him. Sentimental in the film. But the underlying move is sound and you can use a sober version: **the narrator has one thing that stops him, and it isn't a principle — it's a person.** When the Keepers run their diagnosis on him (per the Wayward Pines comp — *"you can't handle the lie"*), they'll price him correctly and misprice Ana. She's the reason he can be bought, and she's also the reason the buy fails: what they can offer is safety, and what she actually needs from him is that he stop confirming things he hasn't checked.

## 3. The trap — what not to take

- **The romance.** Alice's arc is "sceptical professional woman is worn down and then charmed." Your book has a dead wife and must not import a live love interest to replace her. Cut this entirely.
- **The MKUltra hardware.** Programming, triggers, a doctor with a syringe. Concrete, filmable, and completely wrong for you: it makes the conspiracy a *machine* rather than a *habit*. Your Keepers edit sentences. Nobody is brainwashed; people simply misremember, because they were helped to.
- **The second half.** Once the premise is explained, the film becomes chases and a helicopter. Every long-form source on this list does the same thing at the same point. **The collapse is always the same: the book runs out of method and substitutes motion.** Your protection is that your narrator's method never runs out — there is always another thing to check — and your Act 3 climax is a screen going amber, not a chase.
- **The faked-death ending.** Jerry is officially dead, watching Alice from a car, waiting until the rest of the subjects are caught. It's a cop-out that gives him safety and love and vindication at once, at the price of meaning anything. Your codas are the opposite: Buddy buried with no borrowed phrases, a child waking up, and a man who finally has time. **Keep them.**

**One line to carry forward:** Jerry Fletcher couldn't tell which of his theories was true — the people it belonged to told him, by reacting. Your narrator can't get more evidence out of one edited sentence, so he should stop looking for evidence and start manufacturing responses: post the true thing beside the plausible thing beside the deliberately wrong thing, and watch the room. He is a monitoring expert. **Monitoring is the discipline of learning what a system is by watching what it reacts to** — and it's the one method on this entire list that nobody in any of these books thought to use.
