# Addendum 01 — *Wool* book 1
### What I missed the first time, and why

> **Read properly on 9 Sep 2026** — the Holston novella and Juliette's investigation, start to finish. My original Howey comp covered *Shift* and read **none** of *Wool* book 1.
>
> Santi's criticism was correct and more precise than "you read less": **targeted reading can only confirm what you already suspect.** Every mechanism in the first nine comps is something I went hunting for. Not one of the findings below could have been found by a search, because I didn't know to look for any of them.
>
> This is now the **single richest source in the programme for your narrator's method** — more useful than *Shift*, and it contains the best "small evidence" chain I have read anywhere.

---

## 1. The heat tape — the best plant on the entire list

This is the one to study. I'm laying out the whole chain because the *sequencing* is the craft.

1. **A requisition dispute, in a job interview.** Juliette is being recruited as sheriff. IT has filed a complaint accusing her of stealing supplies. She explains: she needed heat tape for a heat exchanger, Supply told her the backing material was all spoken for, and she found out it was all going to IT — *"miles of the stuff for the skins of all their test suits."* So she had some intercepted. It reads as office politics. Petty inter-departmental grievance. **The reader files it as characterisation.**

2. **A joke in a thank-you note.** Years earlier, a friend sent her some of IT's tape. It was rubbish; the exhaust they wrapped almost caught fire. She wrote back that *"the tape couldn't have self-destructed better if it'd been engineered to."* Sarcasm. Throwaway.

3. **A dying man's last message.** Her friend Scottie, hours before he's murdered, wires her: *"That tape I got you? Your joke was truth."*

4. **The conclusion, reached by inference and stated plainly.** Juliette and Walker work it out over a running air compressor (noise cover): the suits are *engineered to fail*. Not because outside is deadly — it is — but so that no cleaner ever gets out of sight. *"They want us all right here where they can see us."*

5. **The same instrument, reversed.** Walker calls in decades of favours with Supply. They quietly substitute Mechanical's parts into IT's order. Juliette walks over the hill because the tape on her suit is good. **The conspiracy and its defeat run through the identical mechanism: the parts list.**

**Why this is better than the fibre-optic beat I flagged in the first comp:** the protagonist *said the crucial fact herself, as a joke, and didn't hear it.* Weir's ladder-wrongness is felt and unnamed. PKD's Boeing 707 is spotted by an expert. Howey's fibre-optic is glimpsed and lost. **This one was spoken aloud by the person it would save, as a punchline, years early.**

**For your book:** the narrator should have already said the true thing. Somewhere in ch1–4 — or in the Record, years back — there is a line he wrote as sarcasm, or a did-you-know he deployed at a table to get out of a conversation, that turns out to be literally correct. He is the world's foremost noticer and he *filed his own best observation under charm.* That is devastating, it's completely in character, and it makes the payoff his own fault.

## 2. Juliette's method — Howey states the transfer explicitly

I recommended "make his professional competence the plot device." Howey does it better and says it out loud:

> *"Approaching the scene of a crime or an active dispute was no different from walking into a pump room where something was broken. Someone or something was always at fault... following a chain of events all the way down to the bedrock itself. There were always confounding variables — you couldn't adjust one dial without sending something else a-kilter — but Juliette had a skill, a talent, for **knowing what was important and what could be ignored**."*

And Jahns, earlier: *"people were like machines. They broke down... Being sheriff, like being a mechanic, was as much the fine art of preventive maintenance as it was the cleaning up after a breakdown."*

**"Knowing what was important and what could be ignored" is your narrator's literal job description.** Monitoring *is* the discipline of deciding what counts as an event. Howey gives you the sentence that licenses the whole book: the competence transfers, and the reader accepts it because it's been demonstrated on machines first.

Chapter two already does this (the payment system, the value climbing for six weeks under green dashboards). **The upgrade is to name it** — one line, in his voice, that tells the reader the two skills are one skill. Then everything he does afterwards is pre-justified.

## 3. The storage pattern as the tell — pure monitoring insight

Juliette gets a data drive containing every keystroke from her dead predecessor's two computers, five years' worth. Far too much to read. She runs a **global spellcheck** looking for names.

She doesn't find names. She finds blocks of gibberish — and what makes her stop is not the content:

> *"...how often the data appeared in nested directories, sometimes a dozen or more folders deep. It was as if someone had taken pains to keep them hidden, but had wanted multiple copies stashed away, terrified of losing them."*

**She identifies the most important file in the silo without being able to read a character of it, purely from how it was stored.** Redundancy plus depth equals fear. The filesystem is the confession.

**This is the most natively "your narrator" moment in any of the eleven sources.** He audits systems. He reads access patterns, retention policies, backup schedules. If he ever gets the professor's machine, or a share, or an archive — **he should read the metadata before the documents**, and be right. Nobody else in this genre thinks that way and it is exactly the Watts/Weir register: a conclusion reached from structure, not content.

## 4. The price of a wire — an economic parameter as a control instrument

Juliette asks her deputy why it costs a quarter chit *per character* to send a message, when pressing pulp, drying it, and paying a porter to physically carry a note is cheaper. He gives the innocent answer: servers, electricity, oil, cooling.

She isn't satisfied:

> *"But what if it's for a different reason? What if someone made it expensive on purpose? ... to make conversing with each other more difficult? Or at least costly. You know, separate us, make us keep our thoughts to ourselves."*

Later, confirmed in her own head: *"they didn't want people talking. Thinking was fine; they would bury you with your thoughts. But no collaboration, no groups coordinating together, no exchange of ideas."*

**Communication priced to prevent coordination.** Not censored — *taxed.* This belongs in your Keepers' toolkit immediately, and it's the purest expression of your own rule (*they never fight stories, only instruments*) that I have found in any source. It is also, again, a **number that doesn't reconcile with its stated cost** — the same shape as the fibre-optic manifest, but about pricing rather than volume, and therefore even better suited to a man who audits systems for a living.

Note also the discipline of the scene: **she raises it, gets a reasonable answer, can't disprove it, and drops the subject rather than pushing.** She says "forget about it, it's just a silly thought." She's learned she can't trust the room. That's how a careful person behaves and it's how your narrator should behave in ch17–20.

## 5. The blur is a clock — the pressure mechanism

I completely missed this, and it's the engine of the whole novella.

The camera lenses outside fog over with grime. The view degrades. As it degrades, tension in the silo rises — people become irritable, superstitious, watchful. And a cleaning becomes *needed*:

> *"He could feel the pressure, as sheriff, to find someone. It was growing, like steam building up in the silo... It was like one of those self-fulfilling prophecies where the nerves finally made someone twitch, then lash out or say something regretful, and then they'd find themselves in a cell, watching their last blurry sunset."*

**The society manufactures its own transgressors on a schedule set by dirt on a lens.** Nobody decides to persecute anyone. The pressure builds, someone cracks, and the ritual resolves it — after which the mood is euphoric and permissive, taboos relax for a month, and the cycle restarts.

Two things for you:

- **This is your CLOCK thread, and it's a better model than a countdown.** A deadline is a date. This is a *rising variable* that makes the ugly thing inevitable without anyone choosing it. Your managed cascade is exactly this shape — things degrading deniably until something has to give — and the Keepers' genius is that they don't have to act, they only have to let the gauge climb.
- **And it's economic.** Howey's throwaway observation, which is superb: *"the barbaric practice brought more than psychological relief... it also buttressed the silo's economy. There was suddenly an excuse to travel. An excuse to trade."* **The atrocity is load-bearing fiscal stimulus.** That's why it can never be abolished — too much depends on it. Give your Keepers something like that: the instrument they maintain is now holding up things that have nothing to do with the original purpose.

## 6. Why everyone cleans — the lie administered at the point of maximum leverage

The central mystery is *behavioural*, not factual: every condemned person swears they won't clean the sensors, and every one of them does. Nobody knows why.

The answer: the helmet visor is a screen. It shows them green grass, blue sky, a gleaming city. **They clean out of pity and joy** — wanting to give the people inside a clear view of a world they believe is beautiful. Holston, dying, waves at the camera and scrubs with *"raw pity and unconstrained joy."*

Bernard, watching from the cafeteria, calls it *"this delusion he and his predecessors had honed to perfection... no matter the individual psychology, the sight of all their false hopes eventually drove them to do what they promised they wouldn't."*

**The suppression system's masterstroke is that it makes its victims complicit, voluntarily, out of love.** Not fear, not coercion — hope, delivered two inches from the eye at the one moment they cannot check it.

For your Keepers: **the edit is not what stops people. What stops people is that the edited version is kinder.** "Arisen among the first" is a *nicer* sentence than "transmitted to the early" — it makes us authors of ourselves rather than recipients. Your narrator's hardest problem shouldn't be that the truth is hidden. It should be that the lie is *better company*, and that Ana would rather have it, and that she isn't stupid.

## 7. The visor's edges — where the lie stops

When Juliette crests the hill, the program breaks down at its boundary:

> *"While the upper levels of the distant monoliths appeared whole and gleamed in the false sunlight, below these sharp panes of glass and bright steel lay the rotted dinginess of an abandoned world... the extra and unfamiliar buildings had no supports at all, no foundations. They hung in the air with dark sky beneath them."*

And earlier, the corpses of previous cleaners are rendered as **boulders** — she walks past Holston and Allison without knowing, and only later recognises that every scattered rock on that hillside is a person.

**The fabrication is only maintained where anyone was expected to look.** Step outside the modelled region and it fails visibly — not with an error, but with buildings floating and a hard painted line at the horizon.

**Directly usable:** the Keepers' edits will be dense where scrutiny was expected and absent where it wasn't. Your narrator should find the *edge* of the editing — a region of the record where nobody bothered, because nobody was ever going to check — and the edge is what proves the centre. That's a genuinely new plot mechanism and it's better than finding another edit.

Also the dead-pixel beat, planted 400 pages before it pays: Holston notices white stuck pixels on the wall screen and idly imagines a future where the *true* pixels are mistaken for the broken ones — then thinks, *"Or was that what Holston and his people were doing even now?"* **The one accurate thing on the display is classified as a malfunction.** That is your page 118 rendered as a physical object, in chapter two, as a passing thought.

## 8. Three more, compressed

- **Amnesty as evidence destruction.** Bernard, newly installed, announces a *"forgiveness moratorium"* — all pending cases dismissed, framed as healing and continuity, adopted as his election motto: *"Forgive and forget."* He drops the folders into the recycle bin himself. One of them is the murder investigation in which he is the prime suspect. **They don't suppress the inquiry. They forgive it — publicly, generously, as a gift to a tired population.**
- **The system accuses; the human complies.** Juliette is arrested by her own deputy, who says: *"The computer says you're a suspect, ma'am. I'm just doing what it tells me to do."* Written in 2011. That is your AI-as-mechanism-of-blindness, exactly, and it costs one line.
- **The successor offer, again.** Bernard tells Lukas — the man closest to the secret — *"I need a shadow. I want you to be that man."* This is now the **third independent source** with the same move (Pilcher recruits Ethan; the Keepers recruit your narrator). Three out of eleven sources converge on it. **The buy is a succession offer, and it's the strongest single antagonist mechanism in the whole reading programme.**

## 9. What this changes in the earlier documents

- The Howey comp's headline mechanism (**the fibre-optic manifest**) stands, but the heat-tape chain is the better model, and I'd build the crossing on that shape instead: *a thing the narrator himself said, lightly, that turns out to be exact.*
- The synthesis §5 list of three mechanisms should now be **four**: add **read the metadata, not the documents.**
- My claim that *Wool* book 1 was "worth reading later for pleasure and pacing" was wrong. It's the most useful text in the programme for procedure, and *Shift* is the most useful for the conspiracy's origin. Different jobs, both essential.
