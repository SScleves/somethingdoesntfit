# Jorge Luis Borges, "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" — the whole story (parts I, II and the Postscript)

> Coverage: the whole story: parts I and II, the dated close (Salto Oriental, 1940), the 1947 Postscript and footnotes 1–6 (about 6,000 words), read in full, 11 Nov 2026. Edition: Andrew Hurley translation, *Collected Fictions*, converted text, chunk file 01. The file marks only parts I and II before the Postscript, so those three are the "chapters" cited here.

## Why it grips
- **The checks make the dread.** Part I is a run of controls, and each fails: the index, the spellings, two atlases, the volumes side by side, the National Library, Mastronardi's bookshop copy. Each blank removes a dull explanation until only one is left: this copy is different. Part I ends on the third copy's absence, not on a find.
- **The fake gives itself away by what it points to.** The Uqbar article is plausible and even boring; the tell is that its borders are drawn by rivers and mountains inside Uqbar, and its few real names are put in loosely (I). An invention can only refer to itself, and the narrator tests for that.
- **Each find is more physical than the last.** Four pages, a 1001-page book, a letter, a compass, then a cone that marks his flesh. The narrator does almost nothing: Bioy brings the volume, Ashe leaves the book in a bar, the compass arrives in someone else's silver. He is as passive as James, and it works for fifteen pages because the evidence gets heavier while his part shrinks. (My judgement: over a novel it would need a cost he chooses.)
- **The Postscript turns the story into a document.** Parts I and II, the narrator says, are reprinted from a 1940 anthology, so what we just read becomes one more object in the case. Then he hands the rest to the reader's own memory.
- **Funny throughout.** Englishmen afflicted with unreality, a friendship that soon eliminates conversation, and an ending about translating Browne while the world dissolves.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The conspiracy moves the story by sending objects, never people. Nobody threatens or blocks the narrator; Orbis Tertius never notices him. It puts things in his path, and each forces the next search: the variant sends him to the library, Vol. XI sends scholars after its companions, the letter answers what Vol. XI raised.

The society is revealed in three steps, each an object inside another object: a stamp on a page (a name only), a theory drawn from the book's coherence (a plan too vast for one inventor, II), then a letter in a Hinton book with an Ouro Preto postmark (the history). The organisation never appears in person.

The Postscript's turn is that the conspiracy edits its own text to be believed. The Memphis copy has cut or muted the hrönir, and its discovery may have been guided; the narrator thinks so. Part II's lore then becomes the plot: hrönir are objects produced by expectation, and the whole Tlön project works like one, on a world that, he says, wanted to cave in.

## How the lore gets in
- **The Uqbar article (I), about 450 words, a document paraphrased.** Wrapped in the act of checking it, so the lore is evidence; no stall.
- **Tlön's languages, philosophy, nine coins, geometry and hrönir (II), about 3,500 words, a lecture from a book.** More than half the story, and nothing happens. It holds because who invented Tlön stays open until the Postscript's letter, each doctrine is a straight-faced joke, and the last item, the hrönir (prison and school digs that produce what the diggers expect), sets up the Postscript.
- **The society's history (Postscript), about 450 words, a letter summarised.** One block, followed at once by two objects.
- **The takeover (Postscript), about 350 words, told as public record.**

## Mechanism notes
- **A (gate), inverted.** He rules out the smaller explanations first: Bioy invented it, Bioy misremembered, the index is incomplete. Only control copies settle it, and Borges uses two (his own, Mastronardi's). For SDF: the "given/arose" first impression should be checked against a third copy before James may believe anything, and that copy should come back blank or ambiguous.
- **B (canary), from the other side.** Tlön is a planted invention, and the hrönir digs state the canary's rule: the test fails if the tested know it is a test, and hope and greed spoil it (II). Andrea's imaginary Rosy Cross, later founded by imitators (I), is the same idea.
- **C (front), strong.** Uqbar is a front for Tlön (its literature refers to Tlön); the First Encyclopaedia is a draft for *Orbis Tertius*; the society meant to invent a country until Buckley said a planet (Postscript).
- **D (lock and key), as a missed key.** Ashe's Norwegian in Rio Grande do Sul was the key, and the narrator let it drop (II). His one admitted cost of being wrong is a question he did not ask.
- **Fifth: the organisation revises its own book to be believed.** The Memphis copy differs from Vol. XI because strange parts were cut to fit the real world. That matches "given/arose": an edit that makes a text more acceptable, not more false.

## Quotes
- "instead of 917 pages, Bioy's volume had 921." (I): the variant stated as a count.
- "Naturally, he found not the slightest mention of Uqbar." (I): part I ends on an absence.
- "Thus it was discovered that no witnesses who were aware of the experimental nature of the search could be allowed near the site" (II): the canary's rule.
- "the cuts obey the intent to set forth a world that is not too incompatible with the real world" (Postscript): the conspiracy edits for belief.

## Flags
- Real people named as members of the secret society: Dalgarno and George Berkeley (Postscript). Borges's real friends (Bioy Casares, Mastronardi, Reyes, Martínez Estrada, Amorim) are witnesses, not conspirators. SDF must not name real people as complicit.
- The invented pages sit in one copy of an encyclopedia the text calls a reprint of the 1902 *Britannica*, a real work. Whether the *Anglo-American Cyclopaedia* existed I cannot confirm [from memory, not from the text]. SDF must not attach an invented edit to a real book.
