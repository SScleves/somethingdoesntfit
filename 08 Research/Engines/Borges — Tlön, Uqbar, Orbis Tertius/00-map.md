# Borges, "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" — the map

> **Coverage:** Hurley translation, *Collected Fictions*, read in full, 11 Nov 2026: parts I and II, the 1947 Postscript, footnotes 1–6 (about 6,000 words). Evidence: `01-the-story.md`.
> **Brief:** how one variant copy (four extra pages in one encyclopedia volume) becomes dread in fifteen pages, and how the invented world it points to replaces the real one. Santi's standing ask: "really capture what made those books great and how the conspiracies actually helped progress the story."
> **Standing-rule note:** the Postscript names real people (Dalgarno, George Berkeley) as members of the society, and the invented pages sit in a reprint of the 1902 *Britannica*, a real work. SDF must never do either. Borges's real friends appear only as witnesses.

---

## 1. What it is

Bioy Casares quotes a heresiarch from an encyclopedia article on a country called Uqbar. The narrator's copy of that volume has no such article; Bioy's copy has four extra pages. Every outside check (index, atlases, the National Library, a bookshop copy) comes back blank. Two years later a dead engineer leaves behind Volume XI of an encyclopedia of an invented planet, Tlön, stamped *Orbis Tertius*. A 1947 Postscript reports the rest: a letter giving the secret society's history, a Tlön compass and an impossibly heavy cone turning up in the real world, the forty volumes found in Memphis, and the world adopting Tlön while the narrator translates Browne in a hotel.

It works because every check fails in the same direction, so the dread is built from explanations he has ruled out, not from anything he is told. And each piece of evidence is heavier than the last, from four pages to a metal cone that marks his palm, while his own part in finding it shrinks.

Read the cost column honestly: he pays almost nothing. It works for fifteen pages because the evidence keeps getting heavier. Over a novel that is exactly the Part Two problem.

## 3. How the lore gets in

- **The Uqbar article (I), about 450 words, paraphrased.** No stall: it arrives inside the act of checking it, so every fact is a test result. The tell is self-reference: its "points of reference were rivers and craters and mountain chains of the region itself."
- **The Tlön volume (II), about 3,500 words, a lecture from a book.** Over half the story, and nothing happens. It holds because the author is still unknown, each doctrine is a deadpan joke, and the last item (the hrönir: objects dug up because the diggers expected them) is a plant for the Postscript. It does stall for anyone not there for the philosophy; only the story's length saves it.
- **The history (Postscript), about 450 words, a letter summarized.** One block, then two objects within a page. It answers a question carried since Part I, so it reads as payment.
- **The objects, about 150 and 300 words.** Short scenes with a witness and a physical detail; explanation in one line at the end.
- **The takeover, about 350 words, as public record.** Fast and flat; it claims nothing it has to prove.

## 4. The mechanism, writer to writer

**Setup:** a quotation over a late dinner, nothing at stake. **Rule:** a text can be checked against another copy of itself. **First use:** 917 pages against 921, and the apparatus (spine key, index) doesn't know about the extra four. **Escalation:** each check fails the same way and removes one dull explanation (Bioy invented it, misremembered, the index is incomplete); then the objects get heavier: pages, a book, a letter, a compass, a cone. **Cost:** a moment of doubting a friend, one unasked question, a palm, a childhood history. **Payoff:** he stops; the world doesn't.

- **A (gate): supports, inverted.** He must rule out the small explanations before he allows the big one. But passing the gate never lets him *do* anything; it gates belief, not action.
- **B (canary): supports.** The hrönir digs state its rule: the test fails if the tested know it is a test.
- **C (front): strong.** Uqbar is a front for Tlön; the First Encyclopaedia a draft for *Orbis Tertius*; a country becomes a planet.
- **D (lock and key): adds a missed key.** Ashe's Norwegian was the key, and the narrator let it drop. His one admitted cost of being wrong is a question he didn't ask.

**Fifth mechanisms.** *Checks as the engine:* the plot is a run of blank results, each ruling something out. *The apparatus that wasn't updated:* whoever changes a text leaves the furniture around it alone, and the furniture is the witness. *The heavier object:* escalate by weight and deniability, not information. *The organization prunes its own lie to be believed:* the Memphis copy cuts the hrönir to fit the real world. That is "given / arose" from the editor's side: an edit that makes a text more acceptable, not more false.

Borges adds three things: a control on the *other* side (is the odd copy the only one?), checks outside the book, and a price for every check.

## 6. Do not take

- **The withdrawal ending.** The narrator translates Browne, "though I never intend to publish." James publishes. *The other ten* already ends on James not acting; don't collect more.
- **The lecture.** 3,500 words of doctrine survive in a short story on jokes and an open question. The thin book stays entries; speeches break at about 150 words.
- **The impossible object and the takeover.** The cone is "a metal not of this world," and reality "caved in." SDF's reveals are paper and flasks, and it ends with a sum stepping down.
