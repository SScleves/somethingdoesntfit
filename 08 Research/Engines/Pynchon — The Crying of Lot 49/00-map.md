## 1. What it is

Oedipa Maas, a California housewife, is named co-executor of her dead ex-lover Pierce Inverarity's estate. In San Narciso, his town, she keeps meeting a muted post horn and the word Trystero: on a latrine wall, in a Jacobean revenge play, in forged stamps in the estate's own collection. The trail suggests an underground mail system centuries old. Everyone who could confirm it or rule it out leaves her: her lover elopes, her shrink goes mad, her husband dissolves on LSD, the director drowns himself. She ends in an auction room, waiting for a hidden bidder to surface for lot 49, the forgeries. The book stops before he does.

What makes it work: a legal duty makes her look, so every clue is also an asset she must inventory, and stopping would mean failing the job. And every reading (real, hoax, madness) has evidence and a weak spot and leaves a cheap physical test, so she keeps acting without ever settling it.

## 3. How the lore gets in

- **Peter Pinguid and the lake bones (ch. 3), about 600 words each, bar talk and a roof.** Each ends on an event (the mail call; a remark about a play), so one piece of history hands her to the next.
- **The Courier's Tragedy (ch. 3), about 3,500 words, a play summarized for laughs.** Where it doesn't stall: she and the reader are waiting for the bones, which come in Act IV, and the act ends on the one word. A long lecture holds if the reader is waiting for one item inside it.
- **Inamorati Anonymous (ch. 5), about 1,000 words, a drunk stranger's story.** Farce that ends on an object: gasoline lifts a stamp's ink and the horn shows in the watermark.
- **The Tristero's origins (ch. 6), about 1,500 words in Bortz's costume-drama voice.** Where it stalls, on purpose: she has stopped checking. The Zapf's sticker on the catalogue addendum restarts it, and Bortz turns the last document into an order: "Why don't you?"

## 4. The mechanism we came for: signs left findable, and the trail that runs home

**Setup (ch. 3–5).** The signs are public and cheap: a latrine wall, chalk, a laundromat card that anyone can find and only the already sensitized can read (quote 1). That is your opening's "we leave them findable": the seats are in the open, and only a man who has already asked will read them.

**The rule (ch. 4).** Cohen's forgeries each carry a planted error "like a taunt", a signature for checkers. SDF's typos do the reverse job (left in so nobody checks). The same device reads two ways, depending on who sees it.

**First use (ch. 5).** The disproof test: she drives away from the estate to "watch nothing happen," and the horn finds her within an hour. SDF already has this in The control case, so it is confirmation.

**Escalation (ch. 5–6).** Everyone who could rule on her sanity goes. The organization never blocks, threatens or speaks to her; it decides who stays in the room. The one possible killing can't be read (quote 6): your "a body is a fingerprint" with the print smudged.

**The turn (ch. 6).** "Every access route to the Tristero could be traced also back to the Inverarity estate." The findable trail becomes the evidence it was laid. She names "Those symmetrical four": the Tristero is real; she is imagining it; Pierce set it up; she is mad. The point for SDF: the old man's "we leave them findable" puts James in that room. If he believes the trail, he is counted. If he thinks it was laid for him, he doubts, and the final conversation tells you what doubt is worth to them ("we need you unsure"). The opening line works on James whichever way he reads it.

**The clown.** The Tristero hides among cranks (Pinguid's society, Nefastis, Inamorati Anonymous), and a crank club is its cover. The twist: the chief clown, Fallopian, gives the sanest advice in the book, the two-column gate, and she hates him for it. SDF has both halves already: Beard Man's bucket turns out right, and Frank keeps the two columns in Frank's kitchen.

**Cost and payoff.** The cost is people, not danger; the proof stays locked in a room with her at the end. Part Two can hold the four readings open until What was on the phone closes them.

**Against A–D.** *A:* stated by a clown and refused; the refusal drives ch. 6. It supports ADDENDUM 05's Frank as keeper. *B:* small, run on lies SDF can't use. *C:* fronts four deep (Pinguid covers W.A.S.T.E., W.A.S.T.E. covers Tristero, Tristero may be Pierce's), and still something learned, never done. ADDENDUM 05's verdict stands. *D:* doors open by choice (the lapel man, ch. 5; Driblette; the sailor). Confirmed.

**New, checked against ADDENDUM 05's list:**
- **Evidence put up for sale.** The estate lists the forgeries as lot 49, so the hidden party has to come, through an agent, and pay. When his agent asks to examine the lot in advance, the house says no (ch. 6). Legal and public: the opponent surfaces because the hero followed the rules of a sale.
- **The hero as carrier.** Oedipa posts a crying sailor's letter through the underground (ch. 5): a one-errand helper, out of pity, before she knows what she carries. Orwell's "homework" is the same family, but Oedipa is kind, not proud, which suits James better.
- **Duty as the engine** confirms ADDENDUM 05's first change. Receiving paper grips when the job obliges looking and stopping is a breach, which is what the Orwell fix for The money gives James.

## 6. Do not take

- **The open ending.** The proof stays locked in a room. SDF's reveals are objects, and James publishes. Hold the four readings open in Part Two only.
- **Oedipa's methods.** Small lies for reactions, and her plan to make a scene at the auction "to bring the cops into it." In SDF a person has to choose to open the door.
- **The real-world graft.** An invented footnote in a real history book, and real postal history recast as a cover-up.
