## Why it grips
- **The detective proves himself before the case begins.** Brunellus (Prime) is a small closed case: hoofprints, twigs broken at five feet, black hairs on a bramble. The best part is invented. He describes the head and eyes from Isidore, because monks see a fine horse the way the authorities describe one. William reads the world and how this community reads. He also slows his mule so the story reaches the gate first, so the method is a performance too.
- **The abbot's scene is built on what he will not say.** William gets the closed windows from one fact, the burial in consecrated ground (Terce), then hits a wall: the abbot knows something under the seal of confession. The reader gets the outline of the secret and none of its contents.
- **The forbidden room is withheld all day.** The reader gets its outside, the floor below and the rules, and nothing more. The waiting is built from a refusal.
- **A crowd gives itself away** (After Nones): laughter at the marginalia, Jorge's rebuke, Venantius's "It is strange you should not remember", the word "dear" said to Berengar. Each reacts to one remark, and nobody explains any of it. William only reads the room.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Section 01 is all setup, but it sets up exactly how the archive guards itself. The guard has layers, and only one of them is a lock:
1. **A prohibition, not a door.** No door separates the scriptorium from the library; the abbot's ban must be stronger than any door (Vespers).
2. **An open but useless catalogue.** Any monk may read it, but it runs by date of arrival, and its shelfmarks mean nothing without the room. The librarian decides who gets what (Terce, After Nones).
3. **Knowledge held by one living person.** The plan goes from librarian to assistant only, which makes Berengar the weak point. William marks him by Vespers.
4. **Fear for the simple, rumour for everyone.** Servants are kept out by hints of supernatural punishment (Terce). Nicholas reports a monk who came out of the labyrinth half mad, and night lights taken for dead librarians (Vespers). The secret is surrounded by stories that make anyone who looks for it seem gullible, which is SDF's method of discrediting instead of preventing.
5. **The confessor.** Jorge hears many monks' confessions (After Nones) and is the one who "forgets" the Aristotle debate. The text does not link the two. [from memory, not from the text: Jorge is the guardian and the killer, and the book is Aristotle's lost second book of the Poetics, on comedy.] In this section the forbidden book is present only as a subject (laughter, Aristotle, "metaphors and puns and riddles") and as Jorge's anger.

**Compare SDF.** Aylin's archive already has layers 1 and 2: a public reading room, and a reference format James notes and does not connect (The drill). Layer 3 is the one to borrow: a keeper who knows what the catalogue cannot say. The reading room also sits on top of what it hides, as in Eco.

**William and James.** William states his method: signs, then the fewest causes ("less costly for our minds", Vespers). Ubertino gives James's advice: "question faces, do not listen to tongues" (Sext). Eco gives William both. He notices Severinus's "naturally" and lets it go, and he probes Salvatore with one word. For SDF: the method can be wrong where the nose was right. William's tidy suicide theory comes just after a room full of faces said otherwise.

## How the lore gets in
- **Prologue politics (about 1,500 words, summary):** stalls, held only by the promise of terrible events.
- **The abbot on the library (about 900 words, speech in a negotiation):** no stall. William wants entry and the speech ends in a refusal, so the lore is the obstacle.
- **The portal (about 2,000 words, ekphrasis):** a deliberate stall that pays off when Adso reads the abbey into the carvings and Salvatore breaks in.
- **History of the Spirituals (about 2,500 words, Adso's thoughts):** the longest stall, held because Ubertino stands waiting.
- **The catalogue (about 500 words, an object chained to a desk):** read in William's hands through his lenses.
- **Nicholas's rumours (about 600 words, gossip):** rules arrive as hearsay that the speaker doubts.

## Mechanism notes
- **A (gate), in reverse.** William rules out the bigger explanation (murder) before acting (Vespers), and Adso has warned it will cost him. For SDF: a gate convinces more if James once passes it wrongly.
- **B (canary), near version.** "Apostles" makes Salvatore flee (Sext); "visions" makes Severinus stiffen (Toward Nones). One word is dropped to see who reacts. No invented item is needed, and James can do this without pressuring anyone.
- **D (lock and key).** Malachi will not decode the shelfmarks (After Nones). In SDF, Aylin's one truth could be a location rather than a document.
- **C (front), weak.** The abbot's request to cover things up makes the inquiry itself a cover.
- **Fifth: rumour as a guard.** The keepers let the library be haunted instead of denying its secrets. That is close to SDF's funded nonsense.

## Quotes
- "Acute in uncovering, and prudent (if necessary) in covering." (Day 1, Terce): the investigator is hired by the institution under investigation.
- "But a list of titles often tells very little; only the librarian knows, from the collocation of the volume, from its degree of inaccessibility" (Day 1, Terce): a public catalogue and a private key.
- "No one should. No one can. No one, even if he wished, would succeed. The library defends itself" (Day 1, Terce): the archive guarded by belief.
- "Often the learned man must make seem magic certain books that are not magic, but simply good science, in order to protect them from indiscreet eyes." (Day 1, Vespers): hiding a secret by making it look absurd.

## Flags
- Real historical people speak and are spoken of as plotters: John XXII, the curia trying to assassinate Ubertino (his claim, Sext), and Ubertino's later murder "by a murderer never discovered". SDF must not name real people as complicit.
- The library list mixes real and invented titles. William's doubts about De plantis and De causis are real scholarship. No invented edits on real books in this section.
