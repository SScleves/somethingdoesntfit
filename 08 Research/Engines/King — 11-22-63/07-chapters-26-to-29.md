## Why it grips

The reader knows the date, the hour and the window, so King cannot get suspense from "who" or "where". He gets it from two withholdings.

First, in chapter 26 the hero's own memory is the locked room. Amnesia is the antagonist's weapon, so every recovery is a small fight: pushing too hard brings on sudden sleep. The answer comes through a found object, a church sign with letters missing, which Jake reads the way he once read a name half-covered by a hand. That is the scene that does the most work: a broken sign gives him the notebook, then the key, the checkbook, the address, and only then the face in the photo (ch. 27 §6).

Second, in chapter 28 King sets a clock (a time every few pages, 8:15 to 12:28) and lets the world fail one ordinary piece at a time: a wheel, a dump truck, a jammed door, a locked lobby. Jake predicts it ("Look ahead, because that’s where the next trouble will come from") and is still caught. The reversal is that Sadie, the person he lied to and left behind for her safety, is the one who gets him there: she finds his hiding place, cuts the car thief, buys the crutch, and is killed by the bullet meant for him.

The jokes never stop (the beggar haggling over one crutch), which keeps the losses from going numb.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here

The "bigger plot" question closes here in an odd order: Jake acts before he is certain. Only after Oswald is dead does he say he now knows Oswald was alone, and he counts that as a gain for the next attempt: next time he will not have to wait. The gate did not open because he proved something; the clock ran out and he went through it anyway. The certainty arrives afterwards, bought with Sadie's death, and it lowers the price of the second run (ch. 29 §8).

Then the conspiracy turns on the hero. Fritz and Hosty's first theory is that Jake and Sadie were in on it and lost their nerve (ch. 29 §5); Jake's private comment is that every home should have a conspiracy theory. Hosty later decides he is a planted agent: CIA, the Russians, Angleton (§9). Being suspected of a conspiracy is what gets him out. The FBI has its own secret to protect (its handling of Oswald, the note Hosty destroyed), so it does not lock Jake up. It writes him a cover story, "The Modest Hero", hands him a disguise, a service-elevator key and cash, and sends him away. Jake keeps his freedom by threatening lies about the Bureau it could not disprove.

## How the lore gets in

- **Al's notebook, last page (ch. 27 §10).** A document read in Jake's hands: about 250 words of dated entries. Its gap matters more than its content: the rooming house is missing. He acts at once (Kenopensky, the gun). No stall.
- **The rule of the past (ch. 26, repeated through ch. 28).** Delivered in a dream conversation with Al (about 400 words) and in one-line statements during action ("When you try to change the past, it bites"). Slight stall, ended by a nightmare image.
- **The memory flood (ch. 27 §7).** A list of fragments, about 150 words, triggered by a photo. It works as the payoff to a chapter of not remembering.
- **Hosty's offer (ch. 29 §9–10).** A conversation of about 2,500 words, with objects laid on the coffee table (glasses, hairnet, key, cash, kitchen whites). It holds because it is a negotiation: each side has something on the other.

## Mechanism notes

- **A, the gate.** What shows here is the gate's price. Jake waits until the 22nd because acting earlier means prison (ch. 27 §13), and waiting is what puts Sadie on the stairs. The certainty he paid for (lone gunman) only becomes useful after the act. For SDF: a gate that is still partly shut when the deadline arrives, so James acts on incomplete proof and pays for being wrong in a way he cannot undo, would supply the missing cost.
- **"The past pushes back" without a face.** It works through the hero's body (amnesia, sudden sleep, the knee), through ordinary objects (a wheel, a door handle), and through bystanders who don't listen (police hanging up on Deke, Bonnie Ray laughing). The Yellow Card Man only appears in dreams. Steady, impersonal, expected.
- **D, lock and key.** The locked Depository door opens only when Jake recalls a co-worker's name from Al's margin notes, and the man chooses to let them in (ch. 28 §12). A helper who needs one specific truth, and a door that opens only because a person decides to open it, fits SDF's rules exactly.
- **C, front.** Hosty's "Modest Hero" story is a front made in real time to cover the Bureau's failure. The organisation discredits and redirects rather than kills ("We don’t kill people").
- **Reveals as objects.** Key stamped FC 775, checkbook, notebook photo, phone bug, Hosty's two notes: every step forward in chapters 27 and 29 is something Jake holds.

## Flags

- Real people are shown as complicit or negligent: Agent James Hosty (handling of Oswald, destroying his note "as per Mr. Hoover’s orders"), Hoover, and Angleton named as a possible handler; Dallas police hanging up on tips. SDF cannot copy this; its organisation and its people must be invented.
- Jake invents testimony about real people to the police (de Mohrenschildt, Oswald). SDF lacks that licence.
- No invented edits on real books.
