# Stephen King, 11/22/63 — Part 2, "The Janitor's Father" (chapters 5–8)

> Coverage: chapters 5–8 (about 42,000 words), read in full, 11 Nov 2026. Edition: converted text, chunk `02-part-2-to-chapter-8.txt`; the file carries no edition data.

This section is the rehearsal, not the Oswald case. Jake goes to Derry in 1958 to stop Frank Dunning killing his family on Halloween, because the trip exists to find out what saving the janitor's family does to the future (ch. 7). So the gate appears here in small: a test case Jake must run before he may try the big one. Oswald's own gate gets one line: Jake remembers Al's lingering doubt that Oswald acted alone (ch. 7).

## Why it grips
- **The reader knows the date and the method from the start.** Harry's essay (Part 1) is the script, and every Derry scene is measured against it. The suspense is watching where the script holds and where it slips: the orange card instead of the yellow one (ch. 5), a sledgehammer where the essay had a hammer, Doris saying different words from the ones Harry remembered (ch. 8).
- **The waiting has jobs to do.** Shadowing, the notebook, the fake property tours look like dead time, but each plants something that pays off on the night: the Rec and Wyemore Lane give him a hiding place, the TV Guide gives him a clock (Ellery Queen at 8), and Frati's gossip turns out to be Turcotte's probe.
- **Ch. 8 §§8–10 carries the most weight.** Jake is trapped behind a garage with the target house in view, a gun he no longer holds, and a watch he keeps checking. Turcotte's backstory (his sister and her baby, missing since 1938) is told while the clock runs, so the lore itself is the obstacle; Jake admits he let the story distract him when he could have grabbed the gun. The reversal: the one person who knows Frank is a killer chooses to let it happen.
- **A late retrospective line pulls the floor away** (ch. 8 §1): the narrator tells us that a scene we read as warm (Frati at the bar, ch. 7) had been arranged.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
No conspiracy behind Oswald appears in this section. What it has is a small-town hidden order in three layers, each changing what Jake does:
1. **The town's "veil" (ch. 6).** People go quiet when asked questions, records vanish, posters warn against strangers. It blocks the ordinary routes (paper records, talking to children) and leaves only people who *choose* to talk.
2. **A private watcher with his own case.** Turcotte has suspected Dunning of murder for twenty years. He shadows Jake, sends Frati to sound him out, and on the night stands between Jake and the target: a second investigator whose aim is the opposite of the hero's.
3. **"The past pushes back," an antagonist with no face.** Its tools are small and deniable: the flooded records, the match the fan blows out, the flu, the dropped gun, the sandbox, air gone "syrupy" as he runs. King keeps it unprovable, so the reader can never pin a will on it, which is why it scares. The irony: the flu meant to stop Jake also brings on Turcotte's heart attack, and Turcotte is the one who kills Dunning.

## How the lore gets in
- **The Derry murders (ch. 6 §3):** bartender's gossip over a beer, about 1,500 words. It runs into a child killed by his stepfather with a hammer, an echo of Jake's case, so it does not stall.
- **Frank's history (ch. 7 §8):** Frati at the bar, about 1,200 words, while Jake watches Frank in the mirror.
- **Derry's wrongness (ch. 8 §2):** a catalogue of omens ending at the pipe in the old ironworks, about 700 words. The one place the story goes still; King keeps it short.
- **Turcotte's sister (ch. 8 §§8–9):** a confession at knifepoint, about 2,000 words, broken by watch checks. It cannot stall the story because it *is* what is in the way.

## Mechanism notes
- **A, the gate.** Here the gate is not "rule out the bigger explanation" but **knowledge loses its value once you use it.** Jake may not act early, because any warning throws away a known time and place (ch. 7 §5). The gate governs *when* he may act, not *what* he must prove. For SDF: James's findings could be worth something only while the organisation doesn't know he has them, so every use costs him.
- **B, the canary, turned on the hero.** Turcotte uses Frati as a canary on Jake: feed him gossip, watch the reaction (ch. 7–8). For SDF: the friendly colleague who hands James a document is testing him.
- **D, lock and key, twice.** The children hold back the address until Jake gives them a truth, a warning that Tugga is in danger (ch. 6). Turcotte demands the same, and Jake tells him the version he already believes, minus the time travel (ch. 8 §9). The cost: the right key opens the wrong door. Turcotte believes him and *chooses to wait*.
- **A fifth: the witness's record is wrong in the details.** The essay is true in outline and false in detail (sledgehammer, different words), and trusting it costs Tugga's life. For SDF: a document James relies on is right in its claim and wrong in a detail that matters.
- **Paying for being wrong (the SDF gap).** Jake misreads Frati, trusts the essay's hammer, and trusts the flu to deal with Turcotte. Each mistake costs blood, and the partial win (he promises Doris he will do better next time) sets up a second try.

## Quotes
1. "As it was, I knew where and when. If I warned him, all bets were off." (ch. 7) The gate as a rule about when.
2. "no one seems to know why they were or who authorized it." (ch. 6) The missing record, with no one to blame.
3. "The past does not want to be changed. The past is obdurate." (ch. 8) The antagonist's refrain, during the flu.
4. "Certainly I had no idea that a man named Bill Turcotte had put Frati up to it." (ch. 8) The late line that reveals an arranged scene.
