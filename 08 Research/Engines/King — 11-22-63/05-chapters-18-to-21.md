## Why it grips
The reader always knows the date. Al's notes give a hard deadline (April 10, then November 22), so eight months of waiting read as a countdown, not a drift. The waiting is filled with scenes where looking is itself dangerous: the lamp toppling as Jake plants the bug (ch. 18 §11), de Mohrenschildt jabbing a finger at the house across the street, which is Jake's (ch. 19 §2), Oswald walking straight toward him on Oak Lawn (ch. 21 §7).

The scene that does the most work is the bug failing (ch. 21 §5). It tightens step by step: Haiti, then Walker's ambitions, then de Mohrenschildt's line that killing Walker would be a public service. Oswald begins "If you really believe—" and the line goes dead. The one sentence that would open the gate is the one the reader never hears.

The pressure also comes from his passivity. An old woman on a walker orders him to stop Oswald beating Marina and calls him a coward (ch. 20 §5); he cannot even suggest the police, in case that changes the future. Each new fact raises the price of acting: Marina is pregnant (ch. 20 §9), so the target is now a father of two.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The possibility of a conspiracy is the only thing stopping Jake. He says he could simply wait for the Walker attempt and kill Oswald afterwards; he is bugging the flat only because de Mohrenschildt might be more than he looks (ch. 18 §11). That suspicion decides where he lives, what he buys and whom he follows (de Mohrenschildt, finding nothing, ch. 19 §6).

The gate is stated exactly in ch. 20 §9 and ch. 21 §7: he must see Oswald alone at the Walker shooting. He admits it is a stand-in. Acting alone once does not prove acting alone twice, but it is the best test he has. So the gate is a smaller, earlier crime used to test the big one, and King says openly that it can never be fully passed: the man at the bus stop might be a co-conspirator, and there is no way to know.

The conspiracy also brings people into the room. De Mohrenschildt's arrival is staged as an entrance, and every Oswald scene after it carries the question of whether this charmer is steering him.

## How the lore gets in
- **Al's notes** (document): one or two laconic lines at a time; action resumes at once. The notes now visibly go wrong (the Y, where the rifle will be hidden), so the guidebook becomes a risk. No stall.
- **De Mohrenschildt's Cuba speech** (conversation overheard on the bug, about 2,000 words, ch. 19 §2). Long but never still: Jake is listening for one name, Walker, and the speech ends in a shirt-ripping show aimed at his window.
- **Kennedy's quarantine speech** (TV in a bar, about a page, ch. 19 §7). Within a paragraph Jake is phoning Sadie, getting no answer, and driving to Jodie.
- **Two flyers** (objects): Hands Off Cuba, bought from a waitress for five dollars (ch. 19 §6); the Hidell flyer in his mailbox, given complete with misspellings (ch. 20 §8). They come through ordinary channels.
- **Hargis–Walker broadcast** (TV, about 1,200 words, ch. 20 §8). The nearest thing to a stall; it shows the target, and Jake ends it by switching the set off.

## Mechanism notes
- **A, gate.** The clearest case in the reading list. Jake must know one thing before he may act: that Oswald is alone on April 10. The gate has a fixed date, a stand-in test (Walker for Kennedy) and an admitted weakness. Every attempt to pass it early fails or backfires: the overheard talk makes things worse, following de Mohrenschildt shows nothing, the bug dies at the key moment. For SDF: give James one dated, observable stand-in event he must witness before he may act, and let what he learns beforehand raise his doubt instead of settling it.
- **The past pushes back, with no face.** It works through equipment (the lamp, the bug's buzz, the car throwing a rod), wrong notes, near-meetings, and "harmonics": a white-over-red Fury three times, the same Colt, a banker who looks like the Lisbon Falls banker. Jake even wonders whether Sadie is a trap the past has laid (ch. 21 §9). The opponent is how the world is built, and it wins by making the hero hesitate. That fits an organisation that discredits rather than prevents.
- **B, canary (reversed).** Jake gives Sadie predictions she can check (Scali, Stevenson at the UN) to earn her belief. A true fact planted to test trust.
- **C, front.** De Mohrenschildt sees fronts everywhere (the NAACP, the segregationists), and Jake's question is whether de Mohrenschildt is one himself, the tempter who makes the offer and stands aside (ch. 21 §10).
- **D, lock and key.** Sadie offers trust instead of demanding answers (ch. 20 §1), and gets one true thing, his name, Jake, before she agrees to marry him (ch. 21 §10).
- **Fifth: the certain paranoid as foil.** Clayton writes that statistical analysis does not lie; Jake answers that there is always a window of uncertainty (ch. 19 §9). The investigator is defined by not being sure. SDF could give James a conspiracy-minded figure who is certain, so James's doubt shows by contrast.

## Flags
- Real people are suspected of complicity: de Mohrenschildt as possible handler, the bus-stop stranger as possible accomplice. King never confirms either; the gate exists because of that doubt. Under SDF's rules, suspicion like this may fall only on invented people.
- De Mohrenschildt makes defamatory claims about Billy James Hargis and repeats talk of CIA plots against Castro. This is a character talking, not something the novel establishes. Do not borrow it.
- Oswald's beatings of Marina are shown in detail and watched by a passive hero. The tone is far from SDF's comedy; take the structure (a witness who judges the hero's inaction), not the material.
