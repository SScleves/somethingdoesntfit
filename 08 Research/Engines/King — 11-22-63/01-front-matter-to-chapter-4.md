# Stephen King, 11/22/63 — front matter to chapter 4 (prologue, Part 1 chapters 1–4)

> Coverage: front matter, unnumbered prologue, Part 1 "Watershed Moment" chapters 1–4 (about 32,000 words), read in full, 11 Nov 2026. Edition: Scribner, first hardcover edition November 2011 (eBook ISBN 978-1-4516-2730-5).

## Why it grips
King gives the experience before the explanation. Al refuses to explain the pantry (ch. 1 §4), so Jake and the reader go through with no rules; the rules come afterwards (ch. 2 §7) as answers to questions the reader already holds. Each lecture is earned by a scene.

Al's body is the clock. Every long speech in ch. 3 is broken by coughing fits, bloody napkins and a box of maxi pads, and each fit reminds the reader the speaker may not finish. The lore is long but the scene is a deathbed, so it never sits still.

The best-built scene ends ch. 3. Al tells the Poulin rescue as a success, asks "happily ever after?", Jake agrees; Al points to an object he planted in advance (a printout in the notebook's back cover); Jake reads it and works out the twist himself before Al confirms. The reversal lands on the hero, not the world.

The prologue promises that "every terrible thing" flowed from Jake's tears over Harry's theme; Harry is parked for three chapters and returns in ch. 4 as the next mission. The comedy never drops (catburger rumours, Al's baffled answer to the grandfather paradox, a gnome's Texas flag that turns out to be a clue).

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The conspiracy's job is to be the thing that must be ruled out. Jake asks what happens if he stops Oswald and someone shoots from the knoll (ch. 3 §4). Al is "close to positive" it was Oswald alone and explains why theories exist (Manchester's scale: the waif does not weigh enough against the president), but close is not enough. He gives a threshold in numbers (he wanted ninety-eight percent), a deciding event (seeing Oswald fire at General Walker, April 1963), and the price of error: kill Oswald, Kennedy dies anyway, and a man in his sixties may not get another five years to retry.

So the possibility of a conspiracy blocks action. Al stood within choking distance of Oswald in Fort Worth and did nothing (wife and baby present, prison, doubt). It also fills the waiting: the gate cannot open before April 1963, so the hero must live in the past until then. The energy here is not whodunit; it is that acting before you are sure wastes years you cannot get back.

Jake then copies the logic at smaller scale: no Kennedy until he has seen a bigger change play out (ch. 4 §6). The gate is stacked: test case, then Walker, then Dallas.

## How the lore gets in
- **Rabbit-hole rules** (ch. 1 §4–ch. 2 §7): first instructions without reasons (hand over your phone, take old money, give the wino half a rock), then the trip as scene (about 4,000 words), then about 1,500 words of rules in dialogue. No stall: each rule answers something just seen.
- **Watershed lecture and Kennedy pitch** (ch. 3 §3–4, about 4,500 words): argument, Jake objecting. It holds because Jake guesses the target before Al names it, the Oswald meeting is told as a remembered scene with dialogue, and the coughing marks time.
- **Poulin test case** (ch. 3 §6, about 2,800 words): a story within the story with its own obstacles, ending on an object.
- **The Oswald Book** (ch. 3 §4): a notebook holding the timeline and evidence. King hands the bulk of the lore over as an object to "cram" off-page and puts none of it here.
- **The tin box** (ch. 4 §7): cash, ID, typed credit cards, a sports-results sheet; each object carries one rule of 1958 in a line of dialogue.

## Mechanism notes
- **A, the gate.** The clearest version on the reading list so far. It has a threshold, a deciding event, a date before which it cannot open, and a cost of opening it wrongly (years of a finite life, since a do-over restarts in 1958 and every reset erases the good with the bad). For SDF: James's gate could be one named event he must witness or one named document he must hold that rules out the bigger explanation, and being wrong costs a resource he cannot get back (PROPOSAL; the map writer names the v8.2 chapter).
- **The past pushes back.** A faceless antagonist working through ordinary failures (two flat tyres, a bridge down, a tree across the road) that escalate on repeat (a thrown rod the second time, ch. 3 §6). Every obstacle has a mundane explanation and can be denied, which fits SDF's rule that the organisation discredits rather than prevents. Al's image: a nylon stocking that gives, snaps back, and can be torn. His phrase "Something doesn't want it to be changed" is close to the SDF title.
- **B, a canary in miniature.** Before trusting the hole, Al carves his name and "2007" into a tree in 1958, then drives to Sebago to see whether it survived (ch. 3 §6). He plants a mark and checks it.
- **A fifth mechanism: the page that changed, and you changed it.** The Poulin printout is two printings of one newspaper page, wheelchair in one and not the other, and the difference is the hero's own fault. That is SDF's premise turned on James: an innocent act of his (a library request, a query at work) triggers a re-edit. It gives Part Two a cost of being wrong with no break-in and no pressure on anyone (PROPOSAL).
- **D, lock and key.** Absent.

## Quotes
1. "Because being ninety-five percent sure isn’t a hundred." (ch. 3 §4) — the gate as a number.
2. "I knew I might not get another chance to make it right if I killed Oswald and someone else shot the president anyway." (ch. 3 §4) — the cost of opening it wrongly.
3. "Something doesn’t want it to be changed, I’m pretty sure of that. But it can be." (ch. 3 §6) — the faceless antagonist, named by what it does.
4. "That’s right, buddy. You bought yourself a dime root beer this afternoon. You also put Carolyn Poulin back in a wheelchair." (ch. 3 §6) — the reveal turned on the hero.

## Flags
- Real people: Oswald as killer and Walker as target are on the historical record; Al dismisses theories blaming the Mafia, KGB, Castro or Johnson. No real person is made complicit beyond the record, and SDF must not do so either.
- An altered real publication: King invents changed versions of the *Lisbon Weekly Enterprise* (a real local paper [from memory, not from the text]). Under SDF's rule (no invented edit on a real book), the printout device transfers only to an invented book or paper.
- Frank Anicetti, a real shopkeeper [from memory, not from the text], appears as a character. SDF invents its people.
