## Why it grips
The reader is waiting for the shooting, and King makes the wait the subject. The gate is stated only near the end (ch. 17 §8); everything before it is the life Jake builds while waiting, and that life is the stake. Each step into Jodie (the dance, the Candlewood, the Jamboree) raises what leaving will cost.

The withheld item is Jake's secret, kept from Sadie. The reader knows it, so the tension is how it leaks: small, funny slips, a Rolling Stones lyric in the car, future slang at the checkout. The blow-up (ch. 16 §11) is built as Sadie's list of every slip she has saved, each one recognised by the reader. Then King flips it: mid-fight Jake thumbs two parts of her name on a job application and reads DORIS DUN. She is exposing him, and suddenly he sees she is in danger; he looks maddest just when he is most right.

Pressure falls on the people around him: Vince dies, Bobbi Jill is scarred, Ivy's husband is crushed. Jake fears he caused it (ch. 15 §14); the book never answers.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Here the gate is stated outright (ch. 17 §8): Jake may kill Oswald only once he knows whether de Mohrenschildt took part in the Walker shooting (10 April 1963). If Oswald acted alone then, Al's notes put a second gunman in November near zero.

It demands surveillance, and every practical step aims at one future conversation: bugs, key, rental, night-watchman cover, binoculars, microphone. It blocks a normal life: no contract, no play at first, no truth for Sadie, because the test is a year off and he must be free and unknown when it comes. It costs Sadie, his job and his name (he comes to hate "George"). It puts in the room Ivy (sells the key), Silent Mike (sells the tools), Ellie and Deke (cover for him, then gently expose him).

The past pushing back has no face, and here it works mostly as harmony. Ivy shares Al's surname; Sadie's name echoes Doris Dunning, making her husband a likely Frank Dunning; the Derry skip-rope chant turns up on Mercedes Street. The "Jimla" haunts Jake's dream and a child's nightmare, then turns out to be a billboard for Jim LaDue that tells him what to buy next. The echoes warn and hand him the next tool. The actual obstruction is ordinary: a state immunization clause, slashed tyres, a bug he cannot safely wire up.

## How the lore gets in
- **Al's rule for the gate** (ch. 17 §8): about 450 words, a dream conversation with dead Al, then a paragraph on de Mohrenschildt and two lines from Al's notes. Within half a page there is something to watch (the skip-rope girls); binoculars are bought next day. No stall: the lecture is an argument (Jake asks if Al believes it; Al says no) and ends on a decision rule.
- **Sadie's marriage, the broom** (ch. 15 §11): about 1,300 words of confession in the dark. A nightmare and Vince's death follow at once. No stall: every detail (alphabetised books, hand-washing) later becomes evidence that Clayton is dangerous.
- **Spy-gear lore** (ch. 14 §2, ch. 15 §6, ch. 17 §12–14) comes as shopping and grumbling (Silent Mike's pitch, Coach's anger at the sideline mic): comedy, and objects Jake can buy.
- **The Jamboree's minstrel history** (ch. 16 §5): texture.
- **Identity evidence as objects**: Ivy's duplicate key, the Harrah's application, the *Tiger Tails* yearbook page 89 (lent by Ellie, with forceps marks at Clayton's temples), the *Time* cover of Jackie Kennedy that keeps Jake in his seat at the airport.

## Mechanism notes
- **A, gate.** King's gate has four parts SDF can copy. (1) One named question: was de Mohrenschildt involved. (2) A dated test in the future that can be observed: the Walker shooting. (3) A "good enough" threshold written by someone else: a rule, not certainty. (4) A long hold of about ten months in story time, filled with preparation that costs something. The waiting holds two things: equipment (getting ready to listen) and a life (what listening will cost). For James: a stated rule from someone he trusts ("if X made the edit alone, you may go public"), a date when it can be checked, and a life he must hide meanwhile.
- **The cost of being wrong comes from keeping the secret, not from the enemy.** Sadie leaves because Jake will not explain; Ellie exposes him through routine paperwork. The organisation never has to act. This is the cost SDF's Part Two lacks, and it needs no villain.
- **D, lock and key, partly.** Each helper wants one particular thing: Ivy wants money, Mike wants to be admired as a craftsman, Ellie wants an explanation. Ellie and Deke open doors (the Candlewood, the yearbook) because they choose to, which fits SDF's rule.
- **A fifth idea, the echo as clue-giver.** The faceless force rhymes events, and the rhyme carries information (DORIS DUN; JIMLA to microphone): uncanny and funny at once.

## Quotes
- "the only thing keeping me from killing Oswald as soon as he moved in across the street." (ch. 17 §8). The gate stated as the only barrier.
- "GOOD ENOUGH TO TAKE THE SON OF A BITCH OUT." (ch. 17 §8). A threshold, not certainty.
- "The past might be obdurate but the future was delicate, a house of cards" (ch. 16 §10). The double constraint on acting early.
- "“It’s too early,” I said." (ch. 17 §10). The gate's timetable overrides the love story.

## Flags
- Real people are named as possible conspirators (de Mohrenschildt, Walker, Oswald). SDF's rule is that people and companies are invented, so copy the shape, not the naming.
- Jake threatens to tell Ivy's husband about the money if she forgets to call (ch. 14 §3) and pays for the key. That is pressure and buying access, and SDF forbids James both.
- Jake plants a bug in a rented house (ch. 16 §2). SDF does not allow breaking in or covert entry.
