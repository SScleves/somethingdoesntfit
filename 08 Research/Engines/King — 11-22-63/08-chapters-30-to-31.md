## Why it grips
- **The win is the reversal.** The reader has waited all book for Dallas to come out right, and chapter 30 takes it apart within a few pages. The doubt comes before any explanation: the cab driver's earthquake (30.3), with Jake sure Kennedy is dead before she says what happened. King then takes Jake back to a childhood guilt he never settled (did he shut the gate on his dog Rags?), so the news hits as guilt before the reader has any facts.
- **Withholding to the end.** Whether Jake *will* save Sadie stays open until the coda. The dated diary entries (Final Notes) circle the question, "I don’t know, I don’t know" comes back like a chorus, and each entry ends in a small act. The scene that does the most work is the burned postcard (10/7/58): one object holding one sentence of warning, and destroying it is how he gives her up.
- **Proof by private knowledge.** Both recognition scenes (Harry's family photo, 31.4; Sadie and the chaperone question, coda 8) are built alike: Jake says one small true thing only an insider could know, and the other person's face changes. No plot machinery.
- **Still funny in the apocalypse.** "Charlie Fucking Chaplin" (30.7), the plaster Jesus edging off the mantelpiece (31.2), Hillary as president.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The Oswald question was the gate, and here it has closed without comment: stopping one man *was* enough, so Al's lone-gunman bet was right. King's Afterword then says it outright: "ninety-eight percent, maybe even ninety-nine." What the gate was worth shows in what it did *not* protect against. Being sure of the target guaranteed nothing about what would follow. Jake picked the right man and still wrecked the world.

Two moves matter. First, the conspiracy **changes hands**: in the new 2011, George Amberson is the mystery, "Savior, or part of the plot?" (31.3), with conventions of theorists. The man who investigated the conspiracy becomes the thing people theorise about. Second, the hidden order finally gets a face, and it is a pitiable one. The Card Men are men who "watch. Sometimes we warn." (30.5). Holding many strings wears out their minds, and Zack Lang ends up on his knees begging (31.6). They cannot stop Jake, only tell him and plead. So with no one to beat, the last obstacle is Jake's own choice.

## How the lore gets in
- **Zack Lang's explanation (30.5), about 1,800 words, a conversation.** Every rule (bubble, strings, residue, harmonics) is tied to a gauge you can see: the card in his hat flickers from green to yellow as he talks, and he smokes something medicinal to bring it back. He uses Jake's own past as evidence (the same meat bought over and over; Kyle, the Yellow Card Man). Within a page Jake is walking through the hole. No stall, because the lecture ends in an order: "go back and see".
- **Harry's alternate history (31.2–3), about 1,500 words.** Jake declines to give it in full ("forget about it"). It comes in compressed paragraphs, broken up by a tremor, a ripping sound overhead and Harry's own losses. The family photo scene follows straight after.
- **Final Notes, about 2,600 words, a diary.** The rules come back as a guitar-string metaphor, and Jake argues with them. The diary is an object (legal pads in a buried lockbox, "Maybe it will be you"). It stalls on purpose, to show his indecision.
- **The Dallas Morning News article (coda 2), about 250 words, a document.** The biggest reveal of the ending (Sadie lived) is an archive page in the hero's hands, with no one explaining it.
- **Afterword, about 2,500 words, outside the story.** Real-world lore (Karen Carlin, Ruby, the sweater) puts the assassination down to chance.

## Mechanism notes
- **A (gate), the payoff.** When the gate opens, being sure of the target does not make acting safe. King sets up a *second* gate at once, and this one Jake cannot pass: to save Sadie (the postcard) he would need to know the consequences, which he never can, so he does not act (10/7/58). The first gate's success costs the world; the second gate costs Sadie. For SDF: a gate gets its weight from a cost that comes *after* it is passed. James ruling out the bigger explanation should not make his act safe. It should only make the act his own.
- **The faceless antagonist gets a face late.** "The past pushes back" turns out to be self-protection (the turtle-shell line; "Want has nothing to do with it", 31.6). After 29 chapters with no face, it gets one: a suffering employee who can only warn. That is very close to SDF's organisation, which discredits rather than prevents.
- **D (lock and key).** Harry (31.4) and Sadie (coda 8) each open up because of one specific private truth (Ellen as a carrot-top, the 1961 chaperone). No force is used, which fits SDF's rule that a door opens only when a person chooses to open it.
- **C (front), weakly.** Each answer opens a deeper layer: the obduracy is protection, the hole is one bubble among a few, the guards are men from some unnamed time.
- **Fifth: the investigator becomes the theory.** Amberson becomes a conspiracy figure in the world he changed (31.3). Useful if James's own traces become the next thing someone finds.

## Quotes
- "Savior, or part of the plot? People held actual conventions to discuss it" (ch. 31.3): the hero turns into the conspiracy.
- "Want has nothing to do with it. You have to go back one last time." (ch. 31.6): the antagonist's whole case, with no threat.
- "The past is obdurate for the same reason a turtle’s shell is obdurate: because the living flesh inside is tender and defenseless." (Final Notes): the rule is explained only once it costs him.
- "The Sadie Hawkins dance, back in 1961. Who did you get to chaperone with you when Coach Borman’s mother broke her hip?" (coda 8): a private fact used as a key.
