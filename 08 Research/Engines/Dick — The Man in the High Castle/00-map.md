# Philip K. Dick, *The Man in the High Castle* — the map

> **Coverage:** converted English text with no edition data in the file. Read in full through three section notes (`01` chapters 1–6, `02` chapters 7–12, `03` chapters 13–15; about 79,600 words), 15 Nov 2026. Tier 3. Anything from outside the notes is marked *[from memory, not from the text]*.
> **Brief:** the leak. You have proposed that fragments of other realities, past lives or an earlier version of society reach into SDF's world. This novel is built on five such fragments. The map says how each one is delivered, which of them move the plot, and what the idea does to THE BOOK v8.2.
> **Standing-rule note:** real people are named as rulers and perpetrators in an invented history (`01`–`03`). An invented novel rewrites real history. A man travels under a false identity to kill; another under a cover name to warn. The police get a signed statement from a shopkeeper by pressure (`02`, ch. 7). Two men are shot and one has his neck cut (`02`, `03`). Characters think and speak in slurs. Take the shapes, none of that.

---

## 1. What it is

Germany and Japan won the war. In Japanese-run San Francisco an antiques dealer (Childan) learns his stock may be forged; a fired metalworker (Frink) starts making new jewelry; a trade official (Tagomi) hosts a "Swedish" visitor who carries a warning about a German first strike. In the Rockies, Juliana takes up with a truck driver who is reading a banned novel in which the Allies won. She works out he was sent to kill its author, stops him, and drives to the author's house to ask how the book was written. He says the oracle chose everything and will not say what that means.

What makes it work: every thread is stopped on a fact that someone has already acted on and cannot check. And the other world never arrives as an explanation; it arrives as a thing in the hands of a person who has something else to lose that day.

## 3. How the lore gets in

- **Childan's ride to a meeting** (ch. 2, about 1,100 words; `01`). The state of the world as one man's self-justifying chatter, two minutes before a client.
- **The succession briefing** (ch. 6, about 900 words; `01`). The one true lecture. Dick cuts it with Tagomi's collapse and gives the rest in a phone call.
- **The banned novel, retold** (ch. 5 and ch. 10, 700 to 900 words, both in cars; `01`, `02`). Each retelling is an argument: the forger disputes every point, the driver is angry at the praise of the New Deal. You learn the listener, and the plot comes with him.
- **The first-strike plan** (ch. 12, about 800 words; `02`). A negotiation between three men who are each hiding something; the general supplies the missing parts, so the plan arrives as a test of the speaker.
- **The author on his method** (ch. 15, about 2,400 words; `03`). Held up by a party going silent and a coin toss done in public.

**Where it stalls:** the novel read aloud in ch. 8 and 10, a page or two at a time. The note calls the stall deliberate; the interruptions are the relief (`02`). **Where it doesn't:** the first-strike plan, because the SD are one phone call behind and a man who may not believe it is weighing every sentence. This confirms ADDENDUM 05 on lore (someone in the room can lose by it) and adds that a retelling holds when the listener disagrees.

## 4. The leak

Five fragments of a world that isn't this one. They do different work.

**The book within the book.** It is never shown whole, and each time it is in the hands of someone with a reason of his own. A secretary takes it off the forger's shelf (ch. 5). A truck driver reads it aloud, furious at its version of Italy (ch. 6). Childan hears of it at dinner and decides to buy it as a duty (ch. 7). The German consul reads it in a locked office, is moved, and nearly orders a murder (ch. 8). What each scene gives you is the reader. It pushes twice: the consul's order, drafted and burned, and the plan to visit the author (ch. 10, paid in ch. 13). Elsewhere it colors. It never touches the German plot (`03`).

**The forged Colt.** The gun is fake, the lab proves it, and the man who said so is a fake too. This is the hardest push in the book and nothing in it is mystical: Childan goes to his supplier, the supplier calls the forger, the forger pays the wrong men (ch. 4–6). The lesson is about paper. The forger puts two lighters on a table, one of which was in Roosevelt's pocket, and says only the certificate tells them apart (ch. 5). History lives in the document.

**The pin with no historicity.** Made by two men who are not artists. Childan's client says it has a worth that owes nothing to any past (ch. 11). It pushes: Childan is responsible for an object he cannot price. It is the Colt reversed, a thing with no paper at all, which paper therefore cannot expose.

**The oracle.** Quoted line by line in the hands of whoever throws. It colors: it never tells anyone what he could not reach by judgment, and the reader cannot check it (`01`). Frink's reading says the money will not come, and it comes. It pushes once, when it sends Juliana to Cheyenne (ch. 13).

**Tagomi's glimpse.** He has killed two men and wants the silver piece to tell him something. He shakes it, curses it, tastes it. Nothing. When he has given up he is in another San Francisco for a few minutes (ch. 14). Nobody else sees it, and his own explanation is that his senses slipped. It pushes one decision: back at his desk, asked to hand Frink over, he signs the release. *[From memory, not from the text: the piece is Frink's work, and neither man learns what he did for the other.]*

**The rule under all five.** A leak here is never information the plot needs. It pushes when it changes what one person will sign, pay, drive to or burn, and colors when it only tells him something. And the discredit comes from the receiver: Tagomi blames his senses, the consul calls it cheap popular fiction, the author will not stand behind his own book. Nobody has to suppress anything.

**Why the ending stays open.** Juliana gets a yes from the oracle in front of witnesses, and the one man with the standing to accept it says he is not sure of anything (ch. 15). The reader holds a proof no character will take. The note names the price: it keeps the dread and loses a payoff, because nothing in the German plot depended on the answer (`03`). ADDENDUM 05 lists the open ending under "does not work," and this book agrees. What transfers is the refusal by the person in authority. v8.2 has it in The final conversation, after a win that happened on paper.

**Against A–D.** *A:* supported in small. Childan must know whether the gun is real before he acts; he passes, and it gets worse (ch. 4), which is ADDENDUM 05's "cost after the gate." *B:* inverted; the caller plants a false story and is never explained (ch. 4). *C:* the forgery trade inside an iron foundry; a cover identity that exists, the general says, to force the other side through formalities (ch. 12). *D:* Juliana gets past the author's deflection with a hexagram they both know without the chart (ch. 15); Baynes reaches his contact with a clerk's name (ch. 10).

**New, not in ADDENDUM 05's list:**
- **Paper against object.** The certificate makes the antique. A thing that arrives with no paper cannot be reclassified; only its holder can be.
- **The wrong payee.** A true fact, the wrong culprit, and money moves on the mistake (ch. 5–6). Near King's "right in its claim, wrong in a detail," but here a third party acts on the error and an innocent man collects.
- **The enemy as reader.** The man whose job is to remove the fragment reads it, and it works on him (ch. 8).

The note's "object that works only when you stop pushing" is already yours: the fourth night of The night he stops being afraid.

## 5. Transfer to SDF

**What the leak idea already has on the page.** More than you may think. **Does it repair REVIEW 01, findings 1.1 to 1.3?**
- **1.1 (the edits mean three things): yes.** Read *given* as "received from before" and *arose* as "you made it up yourself." Then the edit is the discredit, done to a sentence: it reclassifies a leak as an invention. *Transmitted* to *arisen*, *shared* to *coincident*, *remembered* to *imagined* (v6 The floor) are one move. History, instruction and "the passing" become one thing without a new speech.
- **1.2 (the hidden thing is on sale): mostly.** A leak happens in people, so it cannot be deleted from the world, only refiled. That fits the review's own fix (they make it exotic or funny) and gives the forty-one a plain job: the dry, sober books where an author reported a leak as received.
- **1.3 (the motive): only if you decide what the leaks contain.** A leak by itself gives the organization no reason to care. It gets one if what people recall includes the organization: the man with a list. Then every leak is a witness statement and the motive is self-interest. PLACEHOLDER: yours. Kalder still needs his line.

**Where it breaks the plan or a rule.**
1. **A third leak to James makes him a seer.** The brief forbids him out-deducing the book. He has two (the funeral, the floor). Any more must land in other people and reach him as their words or paper.
2. **An object from before a reset breaks three things:** the real-world rule (Urfa: "the book never says he found it"), the book that claims nothing, and REVIEW 01 1.4, which found the reset already cut. Past societies can leak only as invented sentences in invented books, which is what the forty-one are.
3. **"All forty-one are one edit" does not survive The List as written.** Entries 3, 7 and 17 soften a date or an uncertainty; they are not verbs of receiving. Rewrite them or narrow the claim.
4. **The count counts words said, per v7 The payload.** A leak is counted only when someone says it. Keep that and "counted the night you asked" stands.
5. **The win is paper.** Sheets are diffs. A leak may not appear on a sheet as a claim.
6. **This book's own flaw.** If nothing in Part Three depends on the leak, you have Dick's ending. Proposals 1 and 3 are there to prevent that.

## 6. Do not take

- **The open ending.** A proof nobody accepts, with no thread depending on it. Keep the refusal; keep your vote.
- **The oracle as a steering device.** A text that tells characters what to do and that the reader cannot check. James checks things.
- **Real people in an invented history, and a fragment that works as a vision on demand.** The first breaks your rule. The second fails inside the novel itself until Tagomi stops trying.
