## Why it grips
- **Four threads, each stopped on a question.** Childan does not know why the buyer lied; W-M thinks Frink did something he did not do; Tagomi thinks Baynes is a spy but not for whom; Juliana senses something wrong in Joe (ch. 6).
- **Dramatic irony runs on a misreading.** The strongest chain is ch. 4 to 5. Childan's catastrophe is a stranger's lie. W-M, hearing about it, makes the wrong inference about who is blackmailing him, and the money flows to Frink and McCarthy by accident. Frink's oracle said the money would not come, and it did.
- **The most efficient scene is the Syokaku phone call (ch. 4).** One call to a newspaper reference desk turns a fifteen-thousand-dollar sale into an unexplained impostor in three lines. The facts do not resolve.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The secret machinery (Baynes's cover, Yatabe's trip, the cipher) is only a pressure in the background: it makes Tagomi waste a morning and makes Baynes stop talking about the molds (ch. 6). The thing that actually shoves people is **the forgery trade**, a secret everyone knows and no one says. It makes Childan nearly end his life (ch. 4), makes W-M pay two thousand dollars he need not pay (ch. 5), and starts Edfrank (ch. 6). The conspiracy here is a market that survives because nobody tests it ("everyone was happy", ch. 4). Closer to SDF's funded nonsense than to its nightly count.

## How the lore gets in
- **Childan's pedecab ride (ch. 2), about 1,100 words, inner monologue.** Africa, the Mediterranean project and the Slavs, as his self-justifying chatter. It does not stall, because he has a meeting in two minutes.
- **Frank's morning (ch. 1) and Juliana's evening (ch. 3, 6).** Backstory as dread, small talk and a radio broadcast.
- **The Foreign Office lecture (ch. 6), about 900 words.** Five portraits of the Nazi contenders, cut by Tagomi's collapse and followed by a phone call that summarises the rest. The one real lecture, broken by a body reaction.

## Mechanism notes
**How each leak is delivered, in this section:**

1. **The Grasshopper Lies Heavy, a book within the book.** It reaches the reader twice, each time in the hands of someone who does not own it for the reason the reader expects. First as an item on W-M's bookshelf, taken down by a secretary, Rita, who tells him to read it, and whom he dismisses (ch. 5). She retells the plot in a car for about 900 words, with Churchill, Rommel, Stalingrad, Tugwell and no Pearl Harbor, and W-M argues with each point. Then it appears as Joe's thumbed, greasy copy, read to Juliana in her apartment (ch. 6), with one quoted passage of an empire that never fell. Joe is enraged by its version of Italy. What each character does because of it: Rita leaves W-M in disgust; Joe is shown to be tense and defensive; Juliana picks up the author's name and the address "The High Castle" (ch. 6) and says he is brave. Cost: the book is forbidden to everyone on the East Coast, and Juliana fears Joe could be shot for owning it. Pushes or colors? Weakly pushes: it gives Juliana a name to find and a reason for tension with Joe.

2. **Historicity (objects with or without it).** W-M's two Zippo lighters, one of which was in Roosevelt's pocket, and the Smithsonian certificate (ch. 5). The delivery is a demonstration on a coffee table by a man who wants to prove it is a swindle, and the scene turns when the young woman asks to see the paper. The object that carries history is indistinguishable from the one that does not, and only the document separates them. Cost: the evening ends, because the girl is saddened (ch. 5). The lighters only color; the Colt forgery (ch. 4) pushes hard, since the whole chain starts from whether Childan believes the stranger.

3. **The oracle.** Frink in ch. 1 (Hexagrams 15 and 44), ch. 4 (11 changing to 26, with a doom line), ch. 6 (47, line nine in the fifth place); Tagomi in ch. 2 and 6. Delivered as a book in the hands of the one who casts, with the text quoted line by line, then interpreted at length. What the character does: Frink takes McCarthy's offer after reading a doom line as a general warning and the judgment as personal (ch. 4); Tagomi reads a Hexagram 46 comment on a strong, brusque man as confirming his doubt about Baynes (ch. 2). Cost: Frink spends half an hour on one line (ch. 6) and still reads it wrong when the check arrives. It colors choices rather than forcing them. The point that matters: **the oracle never tells anyone anything they could not reach by judgment, and the reader cannot check it.**

4. **A glimpse of another world.** None as an object in this section. Tagomi's attack at the embassy (ch. 6) is the nearest, but it is a collapse into dread, with no vision.

**A (gate).** Childan's ch. 4 gate is internal: he must find out whether the gun is real before he can decide whether to ruin himself or Calvin. He passes it, learns the answer, and it makes things worse.

**B (canary).** The Syokaku stranger (ch. 4) acts like an inverted canary: he plants a false story (a ship sunk in 1945) and watches how Childan responds. Whether he was a rival, a trap or a prank is never settled here, which is the useful part.

**C (front).** The forgery trade hides inside W-M's iron foundry (ch. 4).

**Fifth: a clue that is both right and a lie.** The caller is right about the gun and wrong about everything else. For SDF: let a source be accurate and fraudulent at the same time.

## Quotes
- "the carrier Syokaku is at the bottom of the Philippine Sea. It was sunk by an American submarine in 1945." (ch. 4): the stranger's identity collapses in one phone call.
- "The paper proves its worth, not the object itself!" (ch. 5): the historicity argument, said by the man who profits from it.
- "There is evil! It’s actual like cement." (ch. 6): a lecture is cut by a body's reaction.
- "The Grasshopper was not merely classified; it was forbidden, and to everyone." (ch. 6): the cost of owning the book.

## Flags
- Real people appear in an alternate history as perpetrators (Goring, Goebbels, Heydrich, Rommel, Speer, Bormann and others, ch. 5–6). SDF must not do this; keep to invented people.
- The Grasshopper rewrites real history inside the fiction (Churchill, Roosevelt, Stalingrad, ch. 5). That is a novel inside a novel, not an invented edit of a real book; SDF's rule still forbids an invented edit on a real book.
