## Why it grips
- **The reader is ahead of the hero's hope.** Belbo's file (ch. 110) already says They caught him. Paris is the reader waiting to see *how*. Eco withholds the method, not the outcome.
- **The rite is a farce that turns lethal halfway (ch. 113).** The cast are the clowns from earlier chapters, in titles a paragraph long. They fight among themselves: Olcott outvotes Aglie, Aglie exposes a "spirit" as quoting a book "I wrote it myself; anyone can read it for sixty francs!", Pierre takes the throne, the mediums die of their own ectoplasm. Then, with no change of tone, the knife comes out. These people are ridiculous *and* they kill, and that is what frightens.
- **The victim wins by the sense of the ridiculous.** With the wire round his neck, Belbo relaxes once he sees how silly the room is; his last words are a Piedmontese obscenity. Garamond, mid-stampede: "Gentlemen, please! Manners!" The book stays funny through its worst scene.
- **The investigator catches the disease (ch. 115–116).** Casaubon walks Paris reading every shop window, street name and the Eiffel Tower as proof of the Plan. The reader sees he is wrong and sees why. The cost of belief is shown on the page as a mind that no longer works.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
This is mechanism B's payoff: the editors invented the Plan as a game, others believed it, and the believers come for the inventors. Three things happen.

1. **Denial becomes proof.** Belbo goes to Paris to confess; "His words were too undramatic, too simple" (ch. 110). Chapter 118 spells out why: refusal makes the secret look bigger. Once a canary is believed, its author cannot take it back. This is the strongest engine in the section.
2. **The fake Plan gives the believers an identity.** Aglie tells the assembly the prisoner knows who they are better than they do (ch. 113). Ardenti, whose "message" started everything, kneels and admits he never understood it: the original source has been overtaken by the version built on top of it.
3. **It puts the wrong people in the room.** Garamond, whose vanity press fed on cranks, is now a robed dignitary.

On whether anything was real, Eco closes every door but one. The Provins message was a laundry list (Lia, recalled in ch. 118); the museum shows no trace. But two people are dead, Garamond is missing from Milan, and Casaubon concludes "something did happen", like Amparo, who knew something possessed her even if not the god (ch. 118). His rule for trust: Gudrun, who never understood the game, is the only reliable witness. The Plan is false; the believers are real, and he waits for them.

## How the lore gets in
- **Diotallevi's deathbed lecture (ch. 110), about 1,500 words, conversation.** Torah as body, metathesis as metastasis. No stall: the speaker is dying as he talks, so the lecture *is* the cost scene.
- **The museum walk (ch. 112), about 1,800 words, interior monologue.** Each machine read as a Templar instrument. It moves because he must cross dark rooms before midnight.
- **Anathemas and titles (ch. 113), about 700 words, ceremony.** A catalogue of orders read out while Lorenza stands drugged and Belbo has not yet appeared. A joke and a countdown at once.
- **The three rules of the game and the empty secret (ch. 118), about 2,500 words, notes on a plane.** The thesis arrives only after the climax, as a man explaining to himself what he saw. It stalls on purpose; the action is over.
- **Belbo's juvenilia (ch. 119), about 3,000 words, a found manuscript.** The last reveal is an object, and it is not about the conspiracy: a boy holding one trumpet note at a partisan funeral, the one real moment of Belbo's life, which he never recognised.

## Mechanism notes
- **B (canary): the cost.** Eco's canary works too well. Believers adopt the planted material and act on it, and the inventor's denial counts as confirmation (ch. 110, 118). For SDF: if a stranger believes James's invented title, James telling him "I made it up" should *deepen* the belief. The cost falls on James's honesty, not only his safety. Place: after **On land**, where the invented book exists and Kalder says he has read it. PROPOSAL.
- **B and the clowns.** Eco's clowns self-organise; an invented Plan is enough to gather them. SDF's organisation *funds* its clowns. A twist to test: James's canary is picked up first by a funded clown, so he cannot tell whether the bite came from the organisation or its decoy. PROPOSAL.
- **The outsider as witness (ch. 118).** Someone who has never heard the theory (the widow in Zwolle in **The other ten**) is reliable because she cannot be contaminated.
- **D (lock and key), literally.** A house key in a pocket (ch. 118) opens the cupboard with the final text. It opens a private memory, not the secret, and it is an object in the hero's hands.
- **Fifth: the investigator infected (ch. 115–116).** James, a monitoring engineer, could misread an ordinary alert as a signal: a cheap, funny, visible cost of being wrong.
- **A and C:** no gate here; the front shows only weakly (the Tres calls every other order fake, ch. 113).

## Quotes
- "But if you invent a plan and others carry it out, it’s as if the Plan exists. At that point it does exist." (ch. 118): the canary's payoff.
- "the more he vowed he didn’t possess it, the more convinced They were that he did possess it" (ch. 118): denial as fuel.
- "He knows even who we are; in fact, we learned who we are through him." (ch. 113): the invented plot gives the believers their identity.
- "Gudrun was never part of our story—she wouldn’t have understood it—so she is the only one left who tells the truth." (ch. 118): the uncontaminated witness.

## Flags
- Real organisations (Golden Dawn, Ordo Templi Orientis, the Priory of Sion) are named in the anathema (ch. 113) as rivals, not killers; Kelley, Khunrath and Saint-Germain are summoned as spirits. SDF should not borrow the naming.
- Real Paris bookshops (Editions Rosicruciennes, Librairie la Vouivre, Arcane 22) are read as parts of the Plan (ch. 115). SDF keeps companies invented.
- A fictional character claims authorship of a real text, the *Très Sainte Trinosophie* (ch. 113): close to the forbidden invented edit on a real book.
- The ch. 111 epigraph cites Cazotte "from a page suppressed in later editions": a real-book variant, on-theme for SDF, but SDF must not invent such variants for real books.
