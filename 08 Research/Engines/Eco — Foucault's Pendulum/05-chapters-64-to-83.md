## Why it grips
- **Three voices, one of them a heckler.** Each lump of lore answers an objection: "honey and pepper" (ch. 68), "Make beer?" (ch. 82), "But suppose it's overcast?" (ch. 82). The objection forces the next invention, so the lore arrives as a solved problem, never as a lecture.
- **Puzzles with a deadline and a stake.** "I'll give you two weeks" (ch. 68); "It took seven days to make the world" (ch. 81). Each lore chapter is framed as a delivery.
- **The narrator keeps telling us it went wrong.** "If I had only stopped there" (ch. 77) and the line about waiting under the Pendulum turn the game into a countdown toward something we know is bad but cannot name.
- **A real life alongside.** The scene that does the most work is ch. 78–80. Casaubon has a presentiment, Lia sends him to work, and Salon pulls him into the den. While Salon talks about tunnels, Casaubon invents new lies, then gets home to a note on the kitchen table. The Plan's first cost is a missed hour, and it is a private one.
- **Still funny.** The car as the Tree of the Sefirot (ch. 66), Minnie Mouse as an axiom (ch. 65).

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Here the Plan mostly acts on its makers. It takes their attention: Casaubon at Tomar, Belbo forgetting it is fake, Casaubon plotting while his son is born.

The leak starts at one point, the Tres test (ch. 75–76). They doubt Aglie knows every sect, so they add a fake one and say it came from the Diabolicals' manuscripts. But the name is not wholly theirs. Casaubon takes it from a question the inspector De Angelis once asked him (ch. 75). So the bait is half-real, and Aglie's reaction reads two ways: either he has caught an invention or he has recognised a real word. He is the first outsider to touch the Plan, and he does it calmly. He asks which manuscript, asks again, says it does not matter, and checks his watch. The three lie about the source and can no longer take it back.

Salon (ch. 78–79) is the clown beside it. He believes his own anti-Semitic tunnel plot. But his father worked for the tsarist secret police under a man whose name echoes the colonel's visitor Rakosky. Casaubon refuses the link: "No more coincidences." The clown and the possible real danger are the same man, and the hero chooses not to see it.

## How the lore gets in
- **Abulafia printouts (ch. 65, 71, 82), a few lines each, objects.** Read like a dice roll, so the next idea seems to come from outside.
- **Provins message (ch. 67), about 800 words, document plus dialogue.** A real place has already done the persuading, and Lia is on the phone looking up bannock cakes.
- **Popelicans (ch. 68), about 1,200 words, monologue with interruptions.** It is told to win a bet and gets a laugh every few lines.
- **Rosicrucian manifestoes (ch. 70), about 1,500 words, close reading.** The nearest it comes to stalling, since Casaubon reads alone with no one to object. It is saved by ending on a deduction.
- **"The Cabinet of Dr. Dee" (ch. 73), about 3,000 words, a pastiche inside the novel.** Lore turned into melodrama, and it shows Belbo writing his own story inside the Plan.
- **Chronology of sects (ch. 75), about 1,700 words, a list.** A dead stop on purpose, and it pays off at once as the vehicle for the trap.
- **Aglie on Masonry (ch. 76), about 2,500 words, conversation.** He slides into the first person, as if he had been there. It ends on "Which manuscript?"
- **Telluric secret and map (ch. 81–83), about 3,000 words.** Built through objections: which Pendulum, which hour, which map.

## Mechanism notes
- **B (canary), clearest case so far (ch. 75–76).** How it works: hide one invented item among many real ones, credit it to someone else's material, and watch who asks about the source. The tell is not belief. It is a second question and a quick exit. The price is not paid in this section; the frame only announces it (ch. 77). For SDF: James's invented book for Kalder in **On land** is the same move, already on the page. Eco adds three things. (1) Bury the item in a long, genuine list, such as a catalogue or bibliography, so that noticing it is itself the tell. (2) Lie about the source, so James cannot withdraw it without exposing himself. (3) Build the bait from a word James once overheard, so he cannot tell whether a reaction means his invention was caught or the real word was recognised. Cost: the lie about the source becomes his debt.
- **The inventor believes first** (Casaubon ch. 67, Belbo ch. 68–69). An SDF canary could catch James himself, if he starts to treat his own planted edit as evidence.
- **The clown holds one real thread** (Salon, ch. 79). One of SDF's funded cranks could carry a true connection, so that James's habit of dismissing them costs him.
- **C (front), weakly.** Masonry is "A facade. The secret is elsewhere" (ch. 76).
- **Fifth: the job supplies the fuel.** Garamond's commission (ch. 64) is why they read the Diabolicals at all. Like James, they receive documents through work, but they turn them into something they make, and the making is what exposes them.

## Quotes
- "the idea is not to discover the Templars’ secret, but to construct it." (ch. 67): the game said out loud, just as he stops laughing.
- "Let’s add a sect that doesn’t exist. Founded recently." (ch. 75): the canary planted as a test of one man.
- "It’s just that I never heard of it before. You really can’t tell me who mentioned it?" (ch. 76): the tell is the second question.
- "Serves you right, I said to myself that other evening. Now here you are, waiting for what will happen under Foucault’s Pendulum." (ch. 77): the cost announced before it is paid.

## Flags
- Real people named as complicit: Dee plans murders and Bacon has Dee killed (ch. 73); Postel and Fludd as grand masters; Galileo's cardinals as Templar adepts (ch. 82).
- Real texts read as coded (the Fama, Shakespeare), and Bacon inserts a line into the sonnets (ch. 73). That is an invented edit on a real book.
- Salon voices the Protocols (ch. 79). The book frames this as a believer's hatred, but no SDF clown should borrow it.
