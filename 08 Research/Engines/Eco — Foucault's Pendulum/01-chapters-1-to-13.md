# Umberto Eco, Foucault's Pendulum — section 01 (front matter, chapters 1–13)

> Coverage: front matter (contents, epigraphs) and chapters 1–13, Keter, Hokhmah and the start of Binah (about 31,000 words), read in full, 11 Nov 2026. Edition: William Weaver's English translation, converted text with OCR errors (the title page reads "Foucalt’s"); no publisher data in the file.

## Why it grips
- **The ending comes first.** By chapter 3 the reader knows the Plan was invented, that Belbo now thinks it is real, and that someone has taken him. Everything from chapter 7 on is a comedy in a bar, read under that phone call. The School of Comparative Irrelevance (ch. 12) and the Templar routine (ch. 13) are funny, and each joke is also evidence of how the disaster was built.
- **Three time layers, each with a clock.** The hill "now" (dogs, the moon, "Was that really the end", ch. 2 and 6), the periscope two nights ago, and the Milan years. The periscope makes remembering plausible and puts a deadline (midnight) on the flashback.
- **The password scene (ch. 4–6) is the best-built sequence.** It is a lock with a timer of its own (two thousand years by brute force, ch. 4). Casaubon tries the Sefirot, 36, 666, the 720 names of God, the list read backwards, SOPHIA. Every guess is lore used as a tool, and all of it fails. The answer, typed in anger, is "NO" (ch. 6), and the narrator withholds why: "Now I know."
- **The reader is infected first.** Chapter 1 is written from inside the delusion ("the Only Fixed Point"); the deflation comes later.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Here we see only what the Plan has done, never how: it has leaked, been believed, and come for its makers. **Belbo, its author, believes it first**: Casaubon says Belbo was obsessed "beyond the confines of the game" (ch. 3). Then "They" believe it, and think the inventors hold what the invention describes: "They think I have the map, they tricked me, made me come to Paris" (ch. 3). The invented conspiracy produces an appointment (the Conservatoire, midnight, Saint John's Eve). The believers set a time and place; the inventors must turn up. That turns a game into a plot.

Casaubon takes the blame early, in an aside to Lia (ch. 7): the inventors supplied a depth that others wanted. That is the canary's cost from the other side: the stranger who believes is not the only one counted, because the person who planted the false thing gets counted as well.

The Diabolicals are not on stage yet. Their place is prepared in two ways. First, Belbo's typology (ch. 10): cretins, fools, morons, lunatics, and the lunatic "sooner or later" brings up the Templars. Second, the cranks come in as **manuscripts left at a publisher's door** (ch. 10, 12): "A lunatic, but with a human face." Casaubon dismisses the crank's logic in one line (the Puss in Boots argument, ch. 12). So the clowns are first a professional joke, a category editors sort, and that joke is what the Plan will later be built from. In chapter 12 Casaubon names the risk himself.

## How the lore gets in
- **The Conservatoire as occult text (ch. 1–2), about 6,000 words, a walk through a museum.** Carried by a practical task: each car or case is judged first as a hiding place, then read as a symbol. It does not stall, because the clock ("they closed at five-thirty") and the guards keep pressing.
- **Cabala and permutation (ch. 5), about 2,000 words, a remembered office argument with the BASIC program printed in full.** It is set inside the password hunt, so the lore marks out the search space and the reader uses it at once. It stalls only for as long as one argument lasts.
- **Credulity versus incredulity (ch. 7), about 1,500 words, an essay.** Slow, but it holds the thesis and the guilty aside to Lia.
- **Belbo's files (ch. 3, 8, 11): "Abu", "A bevy of fair women", "Seven Seas Jim".** Documents that carry character, not conspiracy; on plot terms they stall, kept alive only because they are the evidence the narrator broke into.
- **Templar history (ch. 13), about 5,000 words, a barroom lecture.** It is interrupted by jokes, a petition girl, and Dolores joining the table. The key move is that Belbo and Diotallevi turn the Templar prohibitions into an invented scandal ("From prohibitions you can tell what people normally do"): the method of the Plan, tried as a game. It slows the story, and the narrator admits he was "dreaming aloud". It works as seduction: Dolores is won over, and the reader with her.

## Mechanism notes
- **B (canary), seen from the receiving end.** Eco shows the canary coming back to bite. The invented Plan does not just catch a believer. It names its inventors as the people who know. For SDF: if James plants an invented title or edit (On land already has the invented book Kalder claims to have read), the cost could be that the plant comes back to him. Someone, or the organisation, now takes James for a man who knows what he made up. The cost is also a door: people come to him.
- **Who believes first.** The inventor, not the mark. A risk for James: after enough plants, he is no longer sure which edits are his.
- **Clown beside the question.** The publisher's slush pile (ch. 10, 12) is the place where cranks gather and can be sorted. Close to SDF's organisation, which funds the nonsense the real question must be picked out of.
- **D (lock and key).** The password opens by understanding the person, not the lore (ch. 6).
- **Fifth: open with the cost.** The bill is shown before the game, so every later joke reads as a fuse.

## Quotes
- "The Plan. The Plan is real. I know, don’t say it. They’re after me." (ch. 3): the inventor is the first believer, and the first hunted.
- "If two things don’t fit, but you believe both of them, thinking that somewhere, hidden, there must be a third thing that connects them" (ch. 7): Eco's definition of credulity, close to SDF's title.
- "I made Them believe there was a depth, a depth that They, in their weakness, desired." (ch. 7): the cost of the canary falls on the person who planted it.
- "If the public gets wind of this, people will show up with manuscripts." (ch. 12): an invented scheme draws cranks, shown as a joke.

## Flags
- Belbo's "Seven Seas Jim" file (ch. 11) has an editor fantasising that he rewrote Hamlet ("William S."). It is plainly a comic daydream, but SDF must not borrow it as an invented edit on a real book.
- In chapter 1, the narrator in his delusion suggests that Lavoisier was "killed to silence what his fraud revealed" and reads the Convention's plan as Bacon's House of Solomon. Real people in a conspiracy reading, framed as illness; SDF should not do this.
