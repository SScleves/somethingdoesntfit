## Why it grips
- **Code before meaning.** Ch. 1 opens on a coded call that Hale fumbles; the reader gets "raincoat" before its meaning and the ankh before any explanation.
- **The past answers the present, one reflex at a time.** Chapters alternate between 1963 and the past, and each past strand answers a question the present has just raised. Who is the old man in Green Park? The next block is the 1929 rooftop visit. Why does Hale obey? The Haslemere knife (ch. 2). Why does "Bueno Año" hit him so hard? Ch. 4 starts Elena's story but never names her as the woman.
- **The briefing happens during a crime.** In ch. 3 Theodora mimes a drill, so Hale bugs the table in Number 10 while the old man explains Philby's six years of disgrace, with the ivory fan rattling to cover the noise. A long stretch of lore runs alongside an illegal act in the hero's hands.
- **He withholds something himself.** Hale keeps the slips from Theodora (ch. 3). It is his first break with "the Crown's good servant", so the present strand now holds a secret of his own as well as the service's.
- **The hero's competence is what reveals the leak.** In ch. 4, with Moscow off the air, he practises deciphering on discarded pad pages and finds the one message that gives their address.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Powers makes the real Philby record the engine. Each documented fact stays in place, and the novel adds a hidden reason for it: the 1955 exoneration statement Macmillan read, Philby's broke and drunken years, the Beirut piecework, the KGB approach in '58, the 1960 Indian citizenship attempt, Golitsyn and Flora Solomon, the coming immunity offer (ch. 3). Each is now SOE "painting a proper picture" to make Philby attractive to Moscow. Nothing the reader may know is contradicted; it is re-explained. The ARCOS raid becomes a template the secret world rehearses again to give Hale a police record (ch. 2). The 1940 bomb that burned SIS files reaches Hale through Philby's voice as a question: was the fire a pretext for a deeper service to take the files away? (ch. 4).

The conspiracy also writes the hero's own file. The Registry now shows that he never left the service (ch. 3). He is given fabricated Kuwait crimes, Cassagnac is shot with his gun, and a policeman is shot to cover it. The service discredits its own man so that the enemy will recruit him. That is SDF's organisation exactly (discredit, don't prevent), but used on the protagonist. It forces every next step: he cannot go home, cannot confirm his orders, and cannot tell the woman the truth.

## How the lore gets in
- **The 1929 rooftop visit (ch. 1), about 1,700 words, a scene seen by a child.** The Chief asks him to kill a fly by will alone, tells him to "remember your dreams", says "you're on our rolls". Nothing is explained; the instructions pay off later (the dreams, ch. 2). No stall.
- **The Number 10 briefing (ch. 3), about 6,000 words, a conversation.** The Philby history is split between three speakers who dislike each other; Zhlobin's defection (about 900 words) is a story with a punchline. It never stalls, for three reasons: the bug-planting, Hale's private shock over the slips, and the reveal at the end that he has been framed.
- **The Norfolk wireless course (ch. 4), about 900 words, training.** Heaviside Layer, *les parasites*, one-time pads. It reads like a manual, but every term is fired in the same chapter: the parasites become the supernatural rhythm, and pad reuse gives away the address.
- **Elena on the rhythms (ch. 4), about 300 words, a hurried instruction at a door, in danger.** Maly, the walk of "nothing right here", "don't look up". It comes as tradecraft, not as magic.

## Mechanism notes
- **A (gate), turned inside out.** Theodora gives a "confirmation-in-advance": the script cannot be checked, and if it is abominable it is genuine (ch. 3). The hero is barred from ruling things out.
- **B (canary).** The Chipping Campden request (ch. 3) is a canary aimed upward: an insecure request whose acceptance would reveal the plan.
- **C (front), strongly.** Comintern turns out to be GRU, which turns out to be "not entirely" the Red Army (ch. 4). SIS turns out to be the surviving SOE, which turns out to be Declare. Nasser "is of course incidental to the main Soviet purpose" (ch. 3). Each solved layer is the cover story for the next.
- **D (lock and key).** "Bless me" is a password that opens a door with a loaded gun behind it (ch. 4). His mother's sentence gets him onto the ship (ch. 4).
- **Fifth: the organisation keeps the hero's file.** His Registry record and his crimes are rewritten to suit the operation (ch. 3).

## Quotes
- "this had been the concealed war that, ironically, facilitated its own concealment simply by being beyond the capacity of most people to believe." (ch. 1): SDF's clown-beside-every-question, as a rule of the world.
- "So when we needed a story last night, they just re-enacted the ARCOS raid" (ch. 2): a real event used again as a template.
- "make it appear to be just one more of an established ongoing series" (ch. 3): how to hide an invented event among real scandals.
- "In fact our Registry books now indicate that you never left the force, that you’ve been taking your full pay all along" (ch. 3): the record rewritten around the hero.

## Flags
- Real people are made complicit in an invented operation: Macmillan authorises Declare and Theodora's lethal ultimatum; Dick White knows of it; the SOE burns Kurdish villages and kills Turkish soldiers (ch. 3); Lawrence's death is hinted to be murder by the service (ch. 3). SDF must not do this.
- Philby, Golitsyn, Flora Solomon, Profumo are real [from memory, not from the text]; Powers keeps their documented acts and invents motives. SDF cannot give real people invented guilt.
