# Tim Powers, *Declare* — the map

> **Coverage:** English, converted text, no edition data. Read in full through six section notes (`01`–`06`, about 203,000 words: Prologue to Epilogue, Afterword, Author's Note with footnotes), 11 Nov 2026; map 12 Nov 2026. Anything from outside the notes is marked *[from memory, not from the text]*.
> **Brief:** secret history that never contradicts a documented fact. Powers keeps every recorded fact and date and invents only the hidden cause of real oddities. Set that against SDF's rule: real anomalies may stay real; edits, people and companies are invented. Also: how the two timelines force each other, the front (C) built from true facts, records made for the enemy to read, and the hero writing the leak himself and leaving it with a solicitor.
> **Standing-rule note:** Powers's method *is* naming real people as complicit. Philby, Burgess, Macmillan, Menzies, Lenin, Stalin and others are written into the occult plot, real deaths become hinted murders, and a real company becomes a front (Flags, `01`–`06`). Powers puts an invented cause *inside* a real event (Philby's head injury becomes a gunshot, `04`). SDF may only put an invented person *next to* a real event and never say what caused it, which is what Urfa, 1994 already does ("The book never says he found it"). Every proposal below uses invented people and documents only. Hartsik's claim that copyists changed 1 Kings 3 (`05`) invents a variant of a real text. In SDF only the old man may claim something like that.

## 1. What it is

Andrew Hale, an Oxford lecturer, gets a coded call in 1963 and is pulled back into Operation Declare. Declare is a secret remnant of the SOE that has fought a supernatural war with the Soviets since the 1920s. The chapters alternate between 1963 and the past: Paris 1941, Berlin 1945, the failed Ararat raid of 1948. Declare frames Hale for murder so that the Soviets will recruit him. In Beirut he meets Kim Philby and Elena, the Soviet agent he loved, and in the end he climbs Ararat again with Philby. Every documented Philby fact stays true; the novel supplies only the hidden reason.

What makes it work is that the reader already knows the public record, so the suspense is about what forces each event, not what happens. And each piece of lore costs Hale something at the moment he gets it: a cover, a friend, a stone, an oath.

## 3. How the lore gets in

- **The Number 10 briefing** (ch. 3, about 6,000 words, `01`). Three speakers who dislike each other split the Philby history between them while Hale bugs the table. It ends with the news that he has been framed. It never stalls, because a task is happening in his hands. In SDF, keep the task and drop the crime.
- **Ishmael's history** (ch. 7, about 1,800 words, `02`). The lecture is also an interrogation. A parrot heckles, a lie is caught, and it ends in an oath.
- **The archive montage** (ch. 9, about 1,300 words, `03`). **Where it stalls.** Powers rescues it three ways: it is short and funny, Hale has a duty to sign the files off honestly, and it ends on a chain of words instead of an explanation.
- **Philby's confession** (ch. 11, about 4,000 words, `04`). Every disclosure pays for his escape, and the birds circle closer as he talks.
- **Hartsik in the jail** (ch. 16, about 4,000 words, `05`). **Where it doesn't stall**, though it is the book's biggest dump. It is held to objects (lead balls, a derringer), Farid interrupts it three times, and Philby is waiting in the hall.
- **The Author's Note** (`06`). Powers lists the real "wobbles" with their sources: the method stated outright, after the story.

**How the timelines force each other.** The present quotes the past before the past shows it. "Kiss the bride quick, Andrew" (ch. 8) comes two chapters before the wedding (ch. 10). The covenant phrase is spoken in 1963 and muttered and then shouted in 1945 (`03`). Chapter 7 jumps twenty-two years and hands over a list of results, five dead men and Cassagnac shot "yesterday", that the past strand then has to explain (`02`). The poker hand stops in 1948 and is called in 1963 (`05`).

## 4. The mechanism we came for

**Setup and rule.** "I could not change or disregard any of the recorded facts, nor rearrange any days of the calendar." Powers collects the record's oddities and asks "what momentous but unrecorded fact could explain them all" (Author's Note, `06`). The record works as a timetable. "Why in God's name did the man have to be in Berlin on this particular day?" turns a known date into a plot point (ch. 9). Real books are quoted accurately and reread as cover stories (`04`, `06`). This is SDF's rule, minus the real people.

**First use.** Philby's exoneration in 1955, his Beirut drinking and the immunity offer become the SOE "painting a proper picture" (ch. 3). The ARCOS raid is staged again to give Hale a police record (ch. 2).

**Escalation: records made for the enemy to read.** This is the part to copy. Declare doesn't only explain records; it writes them. It invents Hale's Kuwait crimes and makes his Registry file show he never left (ch. 3). In 1948 it passes off a real agent as invented, with a crime that "can't be disproved" because "the illegal kept no records" (ch. 13). In 1963 Hale has to match those papers with his body, down to two punches and a coffee stain (ch. 16). The danger is wherever the record doesn't fit him. The double's transcript has one line that was not "the sort of thing he would say", and Mammalian picks on it (`05`).

**Cost.** The papers come back. The 1948 forgery makes the Soviets trust him and then doubt him, and it turns Elena against him (`04`). The old failure returns as one fact, "You mistook the mold for the bullets", and the new plan is built on it (`05`).

**Payoff.** Hale writes the leak himself: a book called *Declare*, left with a Belgian solicitor and released if his yearly Christmas card stops arriving. Then he offers the conspiracy what it needs, inside a cover story Whitehall will accept (`06`). The last beat is one any reader can check: Philby was buried, and the USSR fell in 1991 (Afterword).

**Against A–D.**
- **A (gate):** supported, with new versions. It can be passed by refusing (ch. 8), by an embarrassing honest report ("Magic, that is, sir", ch. 10), or by leverage (Epilogue). It can also run inverted: "If you hate it, it's the genuine instructions" (ch. 7).
- **B (canary):** supported, and it runs both ways. Ishmael trusts a vain lie more (ch. 7). Philby mutters the covenant phrase to see who flinches (ch. 9).
- **C (front):** this *contradicts* Addendum 05's verdict. Hale wears fronts ("Garner", the defector, the journalist) and has to live up to them (ch. 16, Epilogue), so C becomes something the hero does. See 5.3.
- **D (lock and key):** ordinary ("Bless me", "Segne mich"). Nothing to build.

**New:** a planted record the hero has to match, and the phrase that isn't his (ch. 13, 16). **Sharpened:** Eco's frame, now a file improved in order to discredit him (ch. 3). **New:** ending on a checkable fact. **Confirmed:** Orwell 1 (ch. 9), King 2 (ch. 16). On PLACEHOLDER #7, Powers's habit of letting one strand quote what the other later pays off argues for planting the remembering line once in the opening, as a joke.

## 6. Do not take

- Real people with invented guilt or invented causes, real deaths recast as murders (`01`–`06`). It is Powers's whole method, and SDF can't touch it.
- The solicitor as leverage (Epilogue). A dead-man's switch is a threat. Monday already gives James the same protection, by accident, which is truer to him.
- Framing the hero for murder, the killings on the page, the "verify" culture (ch. 3, 18). SDF's organisation kills rarely, and when it does it has failed.
