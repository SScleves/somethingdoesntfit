## Why it grips
- **The method comes before its use.** The Ceniza brothers lecture on how a missing leaf is faked, step by step, with the tools. Two chapters later Corso uses their vocabulary ("gemelos", worn type) at a table. The lecture is paid off in a scene.
- **The discovery is a seven-errors puzzle.** Corso sets two plates side by side and feels "infantil", as when he played at spotting seven errors. The answer comes in a sentence ("Entonces lo vio"), then the detail (a hand), then a pause at the window.
- **Pressure follows the puzzle at once.** A tail on the road, then a death and a fire, come right after the table.
- **Comedy with a body count.** Pinto's measles-stricken children and La Ponte's betrayals keep the book light while a man dies.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The pursuers do not stop Corso; they leave signs and remove evidence. The cigar butt in two flats is a literary signature. The cost lands on others: Fargas dies, copy Two burns, La Ponte gets silent night calls, Liana's anger ends in violence. The girl who shadows him never says who sends her; she says only that she looks after him. Corso's own order to Pinto to steal copy Two means he cannot be sure whether the fire came from his side. So the hidden side moves him by clearing the field, not by threatening him.

## How the lore gets in
- **The Ceniza lecture (about 3,500 words, a conversation).** It ends on a plot fact: seven plates credit A. TORCH. as inventor and engraver, two only as engraver, with the inventor given as "L. F.". Plot inside a trade lecture, so no stall.
- **Fargas's monologue (about 4,500 words, a scene).** The family's collapse and his ritual of selling one book at a time. The cognac, the violin and the painted Abraham keep it a scene, but it is the slowest stretch.
- **The comparison table (about 1,000 words, Corso's own notes).** The reveal comes from a document he makes, not one he is handed.
- **The burned book (about 800 words, a physical scene).** He works out from the ash how long it burned; again an object gives the news.

## Mechanism notes
- **How he compares.** He brings his copy (Uno) to the other (Dos), is lent a side room, and rules three strips on a sheet, each headed by a number and a name. Page by page he notes any difference, then checks each plate line by line with a 7x lens. He records: gatherings (twenty of eight), plate positions identical (I between pp. 16 and 17, through IX between 144 and 145), the same erratum on p. 72 and the same broken s on p. 87. That means the pair was printed at once under one press, "gemelos" in the Cenizas' word. Then the five differences: the hermit's keys in the left hand or the right (plate II), a labyrinth with or without an exit (III), the hourglass empty above or full above (V), chess squares white or black (VII), a halo on the executioner or none (VIII). He also records the engraver marks. Later he sets them out as a collation table and a list of five words (hand, exit, sand, board, aura). He reads the pattern as a plan by the printer, not as a fake.
- **D (lock and key).** Fargas will not sell: he sells one book to one buyer, a "compatriota", twice a year. His lock is a ritual. Corso's key is shared recognition (a toast, "hermano de culto"), and only the pair on the table moves him: seeing both copies together excites him, so he lets Corso study them. Nothing leaves the house. When Corso offers money from Borja, Fargas becomes suspicious and the offer is dropped. This owner wanted a witness to a rare event, not money.
- **C (front).** The Dumas strand supplies the danger: Liana's attack, the cigar butts, the calls. Corso reads each as a Dumas scene (Milady, Rochefort, Montecristo). The Ceniza lecture and the plate table point the other way: the puzzle is in the book. The Dumas strand keeps drawing attention to people, the book to the copies.
- **Who follows and why.** The scarred man with the Montecristo cigar reappears on the Sintra road and asks for a light. The girl is new: she walks into the train and the hotel, and she is in Fargas's garden before Corso. Neither says why.
- **What each discovery costs.** The comparison costs Fargas his life, or at least coincides with it. Corso loses copy Two. His table is now the only record of copy Two.
- **Real and invented kept apart.** Fargas's shelves hold real books (Vasari, Agricola, Kircher); only the Torchia copies are invented. Corso borrows a real method, Eco's collation tables on the Hanau Pantheon [from memory, not from the text: Eco's Hanau study is real].

## Quotes
- "El truco consiste en localizar un gemelo." (Pedro Ceniza, "Sobre apócrifos e infiltrados"): faking and comparing both start from a second copy. (The trick is to locate a twin.)
- "Página tras página, empezó a anotar cualquier diferencia entre el Uno y el Dos, por mínima que fuese" ("El número Uno y el número Dos"): comparison as routine. (Page by page, he noted every difference between One and Two, however small.)
- "nadie se ocupó nunca de concretar en qué mano sostenía las llaves" ("Postuma necat"): bibliographies never recorded the detail that differs. (nobody ever bothered to say in which hand he held the keys)
- "Un tercio del misterio, exactamente el 33,33 por ciento -periódica pura- había volado." ("Postuma necat"): the cost of the discovery. (A third of the mystery, exactly 33.33 percent, had gone.)

## Flags
- Real figures appear only as references (Dumas, Eco, Doyle's characters). No real person is complicit.
- The hero orders a theft (Corso to Pinto, "Postuma necat"). SDF's hero cannot hire crime or break in.
