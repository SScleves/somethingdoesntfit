## Why it grips
- **The opening scene points at the book, not the body.** A policeman remarks on a page marked in red pencil; the reader's first clue is a printed page.
- **A hero who sells his time and gives nothing away.** Corso invents a Swiss client to close a sale (ch. 3), waits until the widow's nails drum (ch. 2), and lies about where an old sword came from. Every scene is a bargain in which both sides hold something back, so the reader reads the gap between what is said and what is wanted.
- **The turn is built on a refusal.** In "Gente de toga" Corso walks toward the door; Borja speaks after three paces and the tone changes. That is the scene that does the most work: the client's fear is the first evidence that the book matters.
- **Comedy between beats.** 5,775 palindromic tram tickets, Makarova's bar and La Ponte's cowardice keep the pressure bearable.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
Nothing here is yet a conspiracy. What moves the hero is a commission with a hidden aim: Borja says his copy is false and refuses to say why, so Corso must test a claim he cannot check. The Dumas manuscript adds a dead seller and phone calls. The pursuit shows that someone else wants the book or the hero gone. It does not say who or why, and it makes the client a suspect too, since Borja promises to have an authentic copy "como sea".

## How the lore gets in
- **Borja's library (ch. 3), about 2,000 words, conversation with objects.** Corso holds the real copy while Borja recites the Delomelanicon, Bruno and Torchia. Corso interrupts, doubts and asks for proof, so the talk keeps moving.
- **The dossier at the terrace table (ch. 3 end), about 1,500 words, a document.** It ends when a car engine sounds off stage.
- **The typed plate list (ch. 4), about 800 words, Corso's own file.** Nine captions, one figure each. This is the material to compare later.
- **The Dumas bibliography (ch. 4), about 900 words.** A screen of titles; the story stalls here.
- **Balkan's café talk (ch. 5), about 2,500 words, a lecture** on d'Artagnan and Rochefort, ended by Corso's question about Torchia.

## Mechanism notes
- **How he compares.** In this part, one copy against its descriptions. He thumbs the page edges to hear the paper, looks at each plate, sets his copy beside a photocopied bibliography entry and writes "verify" in the margin. Then he types the title page, collation (160 pages, nine plates numbered 1 to VIIII, last page blank, no colophon, 299 x 215 mm) and one line per plate into a computer file. The first discrepancy is recorded as a margin note: Mateu says the Coy copy lacks plate 9; this copy has it. The odd numerals IIII and VIIII are typed in so they can be checked.
- **D (lock and key).** Borja's lock is a keypad door and a vitrine key on a gold chain. His key is Corso: a man with no cause, paid in advance. Corso's counter-key is the book itself, which Borja lets him carry as "garantía", the one thing he needs to compare. The other two owners are only named (a private collection, a public foundation); Corso does not yet know what either wants.
- **C (front).** The Dumas chapter and the Torchia book are set side by side from the first page: a Porthos line in a dead man's open book, then the dead seller, the widow and the phone calls on the Dumas side, the real work on the Torchia side. Corso himself calls both "puntas de iceberg" (opening of "Remember"). The Dumas strand carries the plot-like events (death, calls, widow); the real task sits in a library.
- **Who follows and why.** A scarred man appears three times (Makarova's bar, a chauffeur's cap outside Liana's house, a car at the Toledo cloister wall). Corso cannot say who he is, and fits him to Rochefort, then finds the image came from a film. The false pattern is his own reading of threat through literature. First cost: a crushed hand.
- **Real and invented kept apart.** The real Aldine Hypnerotomachia (1545) sits on Borja's table beside the invented book, and real figures (Bruno, Bacon, Innocent VI) date the invention. [From memory, not from the text: the Delomelanicon, Torchia, Terral-Coy and Mateu's entry are invented; Bruno's "nine secret doors" and the papyrus citations are not real.]

## Quotes
- "Investigue Las Nueve Puertas como si de un crimen se tratara." (Borja, "Gente de toga"): the book becomes a case. (Investigate The Nine Doors as if it were a crime.)
- "En realidad no hay dos iguales, porque ya el nacimiento los distingue con detalles." (Corso, "Gente de toga"): differences are not proof of forgery. (In fact no two are alike, because birth already marks them with details.)
- "Falto de lámina 9. Aquello era incorrecto, comprobó Corso." ("Gente de toga"): the first recorded discrepancy. (Lacking plate 9. That was wrong, Corso checked.)
- "Con privilegio y licencia de los superiores. Aquello era imposible." ("El hombre de la cicatriz"): a detail in the copy that does not fit history. (With the privilege and licence of the superiors. That was impossible.)

## Flags
- The invented book is given false support from real figures: Bruno is made to quote it, Bacon and Innocent VI are tied to it, and real texts (Corpus Hermeticum, papyri) are cited. SDF must not do this; its invented edits sit in invented printings only.
- Real names appear as texture (Dumas, Maquet, Courtilz). No real person is complicit.
