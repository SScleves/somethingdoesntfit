# Arturo Pérez-Reverte, *The Club Dumas* (*El club Dumas*) — the map

> **Coverage:** the Spanish original, from a converted text with no edition data; chapters have titles and epigraphs but no numbers, so cites are by chapter title. Read in full through four section notes (`01`–`04`, about 113,000 words, cut by word count, not at chapter breaks), 15 Nov 2026. Anything from outside the notes is marked *[from memory, not from the text]*.
> **Brief:** a plot made of comparing copies of one invented book, run by a hunter with no authority. What the routine is, why the pattern needs a third copy, what each owner wants, what each look costs, how the second plot works as a front, and how the invented book is kept apart from real history.
> **Standing-rule note:** Corso hires a theft (`02`), gets an owner out of the room by showing her a photograph of her past (`03`), and beats two men (`04`). Real figures are made to vouch for the invented book (Bruno, Bacon, popes, Himmler's circle; `01`, `03`), real people sit on the club's guest list (`04`), and a real novel is given an invented manuscript find (`04`). Take the shapes, none of that.

---

## 1. What it is

Lucas Corso finds rare books for clients who pay. He takes two jobs at once: checking a manuscript chapter of *The Three Musketeers* whose seller has just hanged himself, and comparing a collector's copy of a 1666 book of nine engravings, *The Nine Doors*, with the only two other copies, in Sintra and Paris. Each time he compares, the owner dies and the copy burns. People dressed as Dumas characters follow and rob him. At the end the Dumas chase turns out to be a literary society's game, unconnected, and the killer is his own client, who wanted the plates for a ritual that fails because one plate is a forgery.

What makes it work: the evidence is a table Corso rules himself, so every reveal is something the reader watched him write down. And the loud plot is the wrong one, so the reader makes the same reading error he does and is corrected with him.

## 3. How the lore gets in

- **Borja's library** ("Gente de toga", about 2,000 words, `01`): talk over objects; Corso keeps asking for proof, and it ends on his walk to the door.
- **The binders' lecture** ("Sobre apócrifos", about 3,500 words, `02`): how to fake a leaf, with tools, ending on a plot fact (two plates credit a second hand). **It does not stall**, because two chapters later Corso uses its vocabulary at a table. The method is taught before it is needed.
- **The plate list and the two comparison tables** (about 800, 1,000 and 1,200 words, `01`–`03`): his own notes. The reveal is a document he makes.
- **The baroness** ("El número Tres", about 7,000 words, `03`): the longest lore in the book. It holds because Corso is hiding what he sees on the page in front of him while she explains what it means.
- **Balkan's account** ("Corso y Richelieu", about 5,500 words, `04`): told on a walk through a castle to a man who is losing his theory as he listens.
- **Where it stalls:** the Dumas bibliography ("El hombre de la cicatriz", about 900 words, `01`), a screen of titles nobody in the room can lose by, and Fargas's family history (about 4,500 words, `02`), which the notes call the slowest stretch. Addendum 05's rule holds: lore runs when someone present can lose by it.

## 4. Comparing copies as a plot

**Setup.** One client, one claim Corso cannot check (the copy is false), and the copy itself lent to him as a guarantee, so he carries half of every pair (`01`).

**The routine.** First, one copy against its descriptions: he listens to the paper, types the collation and one line per plate into a file, and writes the first discrepancy as a margin note (`01`). Then, at each owner's table, he rules a sheet into strips, one per copy, and goes page by page noting anything, however small. He records what is the *same* before what differs: the gatherings, where each plate sits, a shared erratum and a shared broken letter. That proves the two were printed together, so any difference left is deliberate and not wear. Only then the plates, line by line under a lens, and a list of five plain words, one per difference (`02`). A second strip tracks the engraver's credit on each plate, a detail the binders mentioned in passing.

**Why the third.** Two copies give a list. It reads as "one of these is the fake," which is what the client said. The third does three things. No plate varies the same way twice, so nobody is correcting anything. Crossing the two strips shows that every plate that differs also carries the second credit, so the differences are signed. And the count closes: eight such plates across three copies, where the book has nine (`03`). That missing one is the ending, and it was planted in part one as a bibliographer's error about plate nine (`01`, `04`). Two is a difference. Three is a total you can be short on.

**What each owner wants.** Borja wants a man with no cause, paid in advance. Fargas will not sell and turns suspicious at the mention of money; what moves him is seeing the two copies side by side, and nothing leaves his house (`02`). The baroness wants a pupil and will not leave him alone with the book, and here Corso uses the photograph, the one key in the book that is a threat (`03`). Balkan and, at the end, Borja open their own doors because they want an audience (`04`). Money never opens a door in this book. A second copy does.

**What each look costs.** After every comparison the copy is destroyed and the owner dies, and the part that differed is taken before the fire (`02`, `03`). So Corso's table becomes the only record of each copy, and then the table is stolen too. He also cannot rule out his own side: he ordered a theft the night Two burned (`02`).

**The front.** The Dumas plot carries everything that looks like plot: the suicide, the widow, the scarred man, the cigar ends, a note in Richelieu's hand. The real job sits in libraries. Corso joins the two himself, and Balkan's defense is that nobody ever told him they were joined (`04`). It meets Addendum 05's four conditions for C: he acts on the layer (he goes to Meung), it breaks on a person and an object, the club is real at its own rank, and there is a floor (the client).

**Real and invented.** The invented book is described by invented reference works, which can be wrong, and the first clue is that one is. It sits on tables beside real books, and the method Corso uses is a real bibliographer's (`01`, `02`). Where it breaks your rule is in the dating: real thinkers are made to quote it (`01`, `03`).

**Against A–D.** A: nearly absent. The only gate is step 4 (one copy cannot be faulted alone). B: absent. C: supported, with an addition: **a front nobody built for him**, a real, unrelated story with its own cause. D: supported, and it sharpens *Libra*'s limit: a key gets Corso a look, never a copy. **New mechanisms:** (1) the hunter's own table as the only surviving record; (2) the cost he may have caused himself. The removal of only the differing part belongs under Addendum 05's "make it useless."

## 6. Do not take

- Corso's means: the hired theft, the photograph, the fists (`02`, `03`, `04`). In SDF an owner chooses, or James goes home.
- Real names holding up the invention: thinkers quoting the invented book, a real novel's manuscript "found," real guests at the club (`01`, `03`, `04`).
- The body per comparison. Your organization discredits and kills rarely; a copy withdrawn or an owner made quiet does the same work.

## 7. Quote bank

1. "Investigue Las Nueve Puertas como si de un crimen se tratara." ("Gente de toga y gente de espada") *Investigate The Nine Doors as if it were a crime.* The book becomes the case.
2. "En realidad no hay dos iguales, porque ya el nacimiento los distingue con detalles." ("Gente de toga y gente de espada") *No two are alike; birth already marks them.* A difference alone proves nothing.
3. "El truco consiste en localizar un gemelo." ("Sobre apócrifos e infiltrados") *The trick is to locate a twin.* Faking and checking start from the same thing.
4. "Página tras página, empezó a anotar cualquier diferencia entre el Uno y el Dos, por mínima que fuese" ("El número Uno y el número Dos") *Page by page he noted every difference between One and Two, however small.* The routine.
5. "nadie se ocupó nunca de concretar en qué mano sostenía las llaves" ("Postuma necat") *Nobody ever bothered to say which hand held the keys.* The detail that differs is the one no record keeps.
6. "Un tercio del misterio, exactamente el 33,33 por ciento -periódica pura- había volado." ("Postuma necat") *A third of the mystery, exactly 33.33 percent, had gone.* The cost of a look, as arithmetic.
7. "Se llevaron los grabados. Los nueve" ("El número Tres") *They took the engravings. All nine.* What the other side removes is the part that differed.
8. "Nadie le dijo en ningún momento que las cosas ocurriesen como usted creía." ("Corso y Richelieu") *Nobody ever told you things happened as you believed.* The front he built himself.
