## Why it grips
- **The reversal is a reading error.** Corso confronts the "mastermind" in a castle and learns that half his theory (Rochefort, Milady, Richelieu) was a game played at him. In "Corso y Richelieu" the villain says he never lied; Corso filled in the gaps. The reader has done the same for three parts.
- **The second reversal is the real culprit, a client.** Borja turns up for the last scene, undramatic: the door opens on the fifth knock, he is unshaven and wants to be left alone. The scene that does the most work is the ritual room, where every plate difference from parts 2 and 3 lies on the floor, so the reader sees the comparison material used for the opposite purpose.
- **The ending turns on one detail from the first part.** Plate IX was the only plate identical in all three copies, and Mateu had claimed one copy lacked it. Corso's last thought, "Ceniza sculpsit", pays off both.
- **Comedy to the end.** La Ponte bangs his head on the window and Corso asks whether there is any gin.

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The conspiracy is sorted into two plots. The Dumas plot is a society, a game and a feud that Balkan ran for fun and spite; its cost was a hanged man and some bruises. The Torchia plot is one rich collector with a ritual, and its cost was two murders and Corso's use as scapegoat (Corso works out that Borja hired him to confirm the three-copy theory and to carry the police attention). Because the Dumas plot was a false pattern, it sat in front of the real one and ate Corso's attention. The reveal costs Corso his client, his pay and the idea of himself as a reader who got it right.

## How the lore gets in
- **Balkan's exposition (about 5,500 words, a walk and a terrace talk).** The Club Dumas has 67 members, each holding one chapter of the manuscript as a share, voted in by a board Balkan chairs. Taillefer broke the rule by selling chapter 42; Liana holds chapter 37. It is delivered mid-action (a lit corridor, a drinks table), then Corso's counter-account takes one paragraph.
- **Borja's ritual (about 4,500 words, a scene).** Magic square, nine plates, nine words, drugged drink. The lore is done with props and with Corso's refusal ("quiero mi dinero"), so it never stalls.
- **The car conversation with Irene (about 2,500 words).** She explains that she is the devil (a fallen angel, alone). Slow, but it carries the book's emotional close.

## Mechanism notes
- **How the copies are finally used.** Borja has torn the plates from the copies of Fargas and Ungern and from his own, and sets plates on the floor in a pattern. Corso sees them there: plate VI with the hanged man by the right foot, two plates VII (white board and black board), plate I with three towers. He reads the nine words Borja wrote on the back of plate I, then burns that plate and breaks the mirror. So the differences recorded across three copies were not for authentication; they were the inputs to a procedure, and the proof of that is physical.
- **The last comparison.** Corso concludes the final plate is a forgery by the Ceniza brothers, made to fill the missing plate Mateu mentions. That is why Borja's sums fail. It is a result reached by thinking back over the table, not by new inspection.
- **D (lock and key).** Borja's door has no lock to defeat: it opens after the bell and two knocks, and Borja lets Corso in because he brings the book. Corso refuses to step into the circle until the end. Balkan's door (the castle) opens because he wants an audience.
- **C (front).** The strongest case in the book. Balkan's Club Dumas supplied the cigars, the safe-conduct note in Richelieu's hand (Balkan wrote it) and the quotation game. Balkan insists he told no lies and Corso built the false plot himself. The reader sees a pattern that invites reading while the real crime sits behind it.
- **Who follows and why.** Rochefort (an actor called Laszlo Nicolavic) and Liana wanted the chapter back for the club; Irene guards the book; Borja, the one who kills, never follows. Corso concludes there are two "authors".
- **What each discovery costs.** Learning the real culprit costs him the fee. Every earlier discovery cost another person: the plate differences cost Fargas and Ungern their lives.
- **Real and invented kept apart.** Balkan claims to have bought the whole real manuscript of Les Trois Mousquetaires from Le Siècle's papers [from memory, not from the text: no such manuscript is known]. Its provenance is invented around a real book and real people (Maquet, Baudry).

## Quotes
- "Esta historia tiene dos autores" (Corso, "Corso y Richelieu"): the front is named. (This story has two authors.)
- "Nadie le dijo en ningún momento que las cosas ocurriesen como usted creía." (Balkan, "Corso y Richelieu"): the front was left for him to fill. (Nobody ever told you things happened as you believed.)
- "Alquilé sus servicios sin confiarle mis razones, ni mis planes" (Borja, "Un recurso de novela gótica"): the client hired him blind. (I hired your services without telling you my reasons or my plans.)
- "en los tres ejemplares, la última lámina era falsa. Ceniza sculpsit." (close): the comparison's real result. (in all three copies, the last plate was false. Ceniza engraved it.)

## Flags
- Real figures in the Club Dumas guest list: the Duchess of Alba, a Spanish Interior Minister, a former Argentine president, the Moroccan crown prince, a Nobel winner. SDF must not name real people at a real conspiracy table.
- Balkan's claim to have found the real Dumas manuscript is an invented provenance for a real book. SDF must not invent a find for a real book.
- Corso beats Rochefort down a stair and Borja; SDF's hero cannot use force or pressure.
