# George Orwell, Nineteen Eighty-Four — section 04 (Part Three, chapters IV–VI, and the Appendix "The Principles of Newspeak")

> Coverage: Part Three, ch. IV to "THE END", plus the Appendix (about 12,500 words), read in full, 11 Nov 2026. Edition: Penguin, converted text, chunk file 04; no further edition data in the file.

**Scope warning.** The forbidden book's payoff as the state's trap (O'Brien saying he helped write Goldstein's book) is **not in this section**. It comes earlier in Part Three [from memory, not from the text]. This section holds what the trap leads to.

## Why it grips
- **The reader is waiting for one thing, and Orwell holds it back.** Since Part Two the reader has known Room 101 holds "the worst thing in the world" [earlier setup from memory, not from the text]. Ch. V shows the cage in stages: something made of wire, then a cage with a mask on the front, then rats. O'Brien explains how it works ("I have pressed the first lever") before he uses it. The reader works out what the mask is for at the same moment Winston does.
- **The key is made from the hero's own material.** O'Brien names a recurring dream of Winston's, a wall of blackness with something behind it. The threat was set up long before, from Winston's own head.
- **The hero's secret plan is beaten before he uses it.** In ch. IV Winston makes a careful plan: hide the hatred from himself, then let it out in the ten seconds before the bullet. A chapter later Room 101 leaves nothing to hide.
- **The last chapter is a café and a chess problem, not an execution.** Ch. VI moves between the telescreen, the board and a memory of his mother he calls "a false memory". The final defeat happens in his feelings, set off by a news bulletin.
- **It is still funny.** The bootlace quota "overfulfilled by ninety-eight per cent", and the Newspeak sub-committee arguing about commas (ch. VI).

## How the conspiracy moves the story here
The hidden order stops hiding and becomes routine. In ch. IV the reader gets the full extent of the watching in one sentence of Winston's thoughts (the dust speck, the sound-tracks, the photographs). What the state wants from him also changes. O'Brien says "Intellectually there is very little wrong with you", so the test is now about feeling, which no amount of thinking can pass.

After Room 101 the conspiracy **lets go of him, and that is part of how it works**. He is free to meet Julia because "nobody would take any interest in them". He has a paid sinecure and a reserved table, and nobody sits near him. The state does not need to stop him any more. A broken man walking around is its proof. This is very close to SDF's organisation, which discredits rather than prevents. Orwell's state breaks him first, then leaves him in public.

His sinecure puts him on a sub-committee of the Eleventh Edition of the Newspeak Dictionary. That is the same edition the Appendix describes. The man who rewrote records now works, uselessly, on the dictionary that will make his old thoughts unthinkable.

## How the lore gets in
- **The surveillance reveal (ch. IV), one paragraph, Winston thinking back.** No stall: it sits inside his re-education on the slate.
- **O'Brien on Room 101 (ch. V), about 600 words, a lecture given while the threat moves closer.** Every paragraph of explanation goes with the cage moving nearer ("less than a metre", "a couple of hand-spans"). The lecture and the threat are the same thing, so nothing stalls.
- **The Appendix, about 4,000 words, a dry scholarly essay after "THE END".** Here the story deliberately stops. It works because it is written in the past tense from a later time: Newspeak "was" the official language, Oldspeak is "Standard English, as we should call it",. The voice can explain the machine calmly because it stands outside it. Readers often take this to mean that Oceania did not last [an interpretation; the text never says so]. Inside this section, what survives is the plain language itself.

## Mechanism notes
- **The forbidden book as trap (asked for, mostly outside the section).** The trap is closed in an earlier chapter [from memory, not from the text]. What this section adds is the *after*. The state that left the seats findable then puts the person who sat in one back on the street, harmless and well fed, and that person proves the trap works. For SDF: if the organisation leaves things findable on purpose, its best result is not a dead James. It is a James who still goes to work, with everyone politely keeping their distance.
- **B (canary), turned round.** The dust speck (ch. IV) was Winston's own tell, and the watchers *carefully replaced* it. The defence against a canary is to put it back. For SDF: James sets a small tell, finds it undisturbed, and later learns it was restored.
- **A (gate), as feeling, not proof.** O'Brien's last gate cannot be passed by being right. SDF can borrow the shape (a gate that tests what James will do, not what he can prove).
- **D (lock and key).** Room 101 is a key cut for one person from his own dream. The craft point that carries over is that the key should come from material the reader has already seen.
- **The Appendix as a model ending for SDF.** SDF ends with the old head defeated and talking. Orwell gives a second ending in a voice that comes after the regime, and that voice explains the regime calmly. A dossier or later document could do the same for SDF. And the Appendix's account of "ideological translation" of the classics, with the originals then destroyed, is the clearest statement in this section of SDF's own premise: a book changed between printings.

## Quotes
- "Even the speck of whitish dust on the cover of his diary they had carefully replaced." (Part Three, ch. IV): the watcher restores the hero's tell.
- "Intellectually there is very little wrong with you. It is only emotionally that you have failed to make progress." (Part Three, ch. IV): the gate changes from proof to feeling.
- "It was something to do with the question of whether commas should be placed inside brackets, or outside." (Part Three, ch. VI): the comedy holds up at the bleakest point.
- "their original writings, with all else that survived of the literature of the past, would be destroyed." (Appendix): editing a book between printings, carried to the end.

## Flags
- **Invented edits on real books.** The Appendix has Shakespeare, Milton, Swift, Byron, Dickens and the Declaration of Independence being "translated" and the originals destroyed. SDF must never do this to a real book. Its edits have to be on invented books.
- Room 101 is force and pressure. SDF's rules forbid James using it and keep the organisation's violence rare.
