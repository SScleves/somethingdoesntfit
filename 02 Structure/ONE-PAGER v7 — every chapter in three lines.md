# ONE-PAGER v7 — every chapter, plainly
### Fable, 18 October 2026. Each chapter in reading order: what happens, what it establishes, and one line on intent. No teasing. Interludes (L) and villain chapters (V) are slotted where they sit. Full beats in `TREATMENT v7`.

**Tracks:** J = James, first person · K = Keepers now · K-past = Keepers before · J-past = James before · L = interlude, real events only · V = villain alone, third person.

## MOVEMENT I — NOTHING FITS

**0 · Cold open** (J)
We are dropped into the climax, eight months ahead of the story: James sits at his mother-in-law's kitchen table across from Bram, a sixty-year-old man in a jumper who represents the organisation, with Ana beside him and the old dog under the table. Everything James has found over the book is laid out on the tablecloth as objects. Bram is making him an offer of "seats" (a place of safety for his family) and the two men are talking around the truth in code words because, by the organisation's rule, if Bram confirms anything to Ana she becomes part of it and liable. James tells the reader that one of the two men at the table was afraid, and that a sheet of numbers on the table said which one. Then: *Eight months earlier.*
*Intent:* open with the stakes already high and the question "what is this room and how did he get here" driving the first half.

**1 · The funeral** (J, Santi's, unchanged)
Ana's grandmother is buried on a beautiful spring day. James, who works in IT monitoring systems and cannot stop asking "how do we know?", stands behind his grieving wife and hears everyone say "she's in a better place." When Ana needs him to confirm it, he says yes without believing it, and this small lie is the thing that starts the book. During the service he has a flash of a different place: cold mud, a hand cracked at the knuckles reaching for his. He files it as lack of sleep. On the drive home he realises nobody even pretends there is a heaven for his old dog, Buddy, and that the comforting words at the grave were never knowledge. The search begins.
*Intent:* establish the narrator's defect (checking everything), the marriage, the dogs, and the single unanswered question under the whole book.

**2 · The good life** (J, Santi's, unchanged)
Ordinary life after the funeral: Ana's grief that visits rather than moves in, the unfinished knitting on the shelf, the two dogs. James's job is explained: he watches other people's systems die and asks the dull questions nobody asks. His colleague Frank sends a link about AI tools that will replace monitoring engineers ("enjoy it while it lasts") and James lets slip "based on what?" when everyone says new jobs always come. His hobby is explained: hunting for things that don't fit, old books as depositions that can't be edited, and the Record, his archive of sentences that changed between printings. Golf is his sanctuary. One night he finds fourteen tabs open about near-death experiences and realises the search has started without his permission.
*Intent:* warmth before it breaks; the tools James will use later are all introduced as habits.

**3 · Reading with a pen** (J, Santi's, unchanged)
James reads seriously about death for the first time: hospital accounts, near-death reports, and the explanations that work harder than the mysteries they explain. Ana finds his notebook and assumes it's about her grandmother; he lets her believe it. He types a question into a forum asking if anyone has had an NDE, and deletes it, because he is not ready to be seen believing. Buddy stops walking the front nine. At the Sunday market Beard Man, the book dealer, has two crates from a dead professor's house; James buys eleven books and is given a twelfth, including a thin one with no title, and shelves them without ceremony.
*Intent:* plant the crate, the thin book, the persona's fear of being seen, and the dog's decline.

**4 · The sentence** (J, Santi's, unchanged)
Reading the professor's copy of a dry 1926 book on flood legends, James realises a sentence on page 118 is different from the copy he already owns. Both are "first edition," but his is a later printing. Someone reopened the printing plates, changed one clause (from "transmitted to the early peoples" to "arisen among the first peoples"), and left the typos and a blemish untouched so nobody would notice. Playing fetch on the kitchen floor at 4 a.m., he understands what the change does: the original says somebody *handed* the knowledge over; the edit makes it appear by itself. The author vanished in Turkey in 1929; the edit was made in 1931. He asks the question that drives everything: if it was worth doing here, where else?
*Intent:* the book's central image, the hand edited out of a sentence, and the method (check, don't believe).

**L1 · Down House, Kent. January 1860** (L)
A true story told as a scene: Charles Darwin, sick at his desk, correcting the galleys of the second edition of *On the Origin of Species*, adds the words "by the Creator" to the last sentence under public pressure, and will write to a friend years later that he regrets having "truckled to public opinion." The chapter shows that the world's most famous book changed between printings, quietly, under pressure, and that the author himself did it. It ends with where the letter is today.
*Intent:* the first "lore" breath; it tells the reader that what James found on page 118 is a known kind of thing, and makes it more frightening, not less.

**5 · Utrecht. A phone call. 1993** (K-past)
Thirty years earlier, a physics department head called Koster rings a pleasant man at a foundation to report his friend Verhoef's unpublished paper, which showed two people in separate shielded rooms responding to each other. The foundation man calls it "recall," tells Koster not to suppress the paper but to reclassify it as "instrumentation" so the right people never read it, and confirms Koster's reward: places of safety ("seats") for himself, his wife and children, but not his mother, because "the allocation is the allocation." Koster then goes and is kind to his friend for two hours. A closing line tells us the department was restructured the next year.
*Intent:* show the reader, before James knows, how the organisation works: decent people, a phone call, renaming a category, a budget. This is Verhoef's paper that becomes entry 26 in the professor's list.

**6 · Leave it** (J)
At work James reads the full list of scheduled jobs, as he always does, and finds one with no owner, no ticket, no history, called `rcl-agg`, which runs every night at 02:14 for nine seconds and sends forty kilobytes to an address that exists only for those nine seconds. He raises it in the daily stand-up. His manager Pieter has an answer ready before he's finished: "diagnostic artefact from the migration, leave it." James did that migration himself; the job wasn't in it. At home, a letter arrives from an antiquarian dealer he'd written to about the 1931 printing: the first time he sees his own name, "Dear Mr Klippe," written by someone connected to the book, with a postscript advising him to "be plain about one's reasons." That night he opens a low-priority ticket asking who owns the job.
*Intent:* the second thread (the job) appears, and the first lie is told to his face by someone who was ready for the question.

**7 · The payload** (J, new)
James does what an engineer does: at 02:14 he captures the forty kilobytes. It is a list of 112 identifiers, which he works out are internal employee IDs of his own company, no names. He checks for his own ID. It isn't there. The next night, after he has asked about the job in stand-up, he captures it again: 113 entries, and the new one is his. He understands that the job counts people, and that asking about it put him on the list, though he does not yet know what the list is for.
*Intent:* James acts rather than being told; the reader learns the job is about *people who ask*.

**8 · The control case** (J)
James tries to kill his own finding, properly. He buys another copy of the 1931 printing (it has the same change: it's the whole print run, not a one-off). He checks nine other books from the professor's crate against other copies, page by page, over three weekends: nothing, which is the control. Then he opens the thin untitled book. It is a notebook in two hands: the professor's grandfather's, then the professor's. Forty-one entries, each a book, a page, a printing, a before and an after. Entry 11 is his own sentence, found and recorded by a man dead fifty years. The last page, in a shaking hand, holds a library reference number (a shelfmark), a date three weeks after the professor's own funeral, and one word: *Ask.* The professor had asked to see something, been given an appointment, and died before it.
*Intent:* James is not the first; the list exists; the appointment he will later keep is planted.

**9 · The week it vanished** (J)
Beard Man tells him how the professor died: in a blue chair, found because the bin wasn't put out; his daughter sells curtains near the station; a polite man in a van bought the furniture. Then, day by day: his ticket is closed as a duplicate of a ticket number that never existed; the job disappears from the list and from every backup going back years, which should be impossible; Frank has no memory of the stand-up; Pieter has no memory of the job. Only James's screenshot says it was ever there. He checks who holds the keys to the backups: four people, one of them Pieter. He says nothing to Pieter, and never will.
*Intent:* the turn of Movement I. Somebody reached into the plate while he was watching, and his manager is one of the hands.

**V1 · A pension visit. Zwolle** (V, new)
Kalder, the organisation's handler, whom we have not yet met in James's story, visits a retired man on the quarter's list who has "started saying strange things about his father." He spends a kind afternoon with him, learns the man loves his old car and the canal road, borrows the car to move a box, and returns it washed. Three days later the man drives into the canal. We never see what he did to the car. We see him wash it.
*Intent:* the monster, shown before James ever meets him as "Dave." The reader now knows what a visit is.

**10 · Groningen** (J, new)
James drives to the dealer who wrote to him. The old man assumes James is from the organisation ("be plain: who sent you?") and then, realising he isn't, explains that after he sold the professor the 1931 printing in 2011 he was visited by polite people who bought three things and asked him to telephone if anyone ever asked for that title again. He has telephoned about James, three weeks ago, before James even opened the ticket. He also mentions that Verhoef's grandson bought from him in 2015. He is frightened and sorry.
*Intent:* the lowest tier of the machine has a face: an ordinary shopkeeper with a phone number. James learns he was reported before he knew there was anything to report.

## MOVEMENT II — THE MACHINE

**11 · Deventer. A Thursday in March** (V / K-past)
The professor's death, from the handler's side. The old man had written to the foundation asking someone to come and talk about his notebook, so Kalder came. Tea, the blue chair, the notebook under the professor's hand. The professor says it plainly: forty-one books, always the same edit, a hand taken out, and he wants, before he dies, to have asked someone. Kalder says nothing, makes him comfortable, photographs every page of the notebook while he sleeps, and reports from the car: "I'd rather wait. A body's a fingerprint." Eleven days later the professor is dead in his chair. When Kalder comes back in June to buy the furniture, the books have already gone to the market: the one kind mistake, which is how James got the crate.
*Intent:* the reader learns who answers "Ask," and that the crate reaching James was an accident the organisation is now correcting.

**L2 · Gondar, Ethiopia. 1770** (L)
True story: the Scottish traveller James Bruce, at the Ethiopian court, is given the Book of Enoch, a text lost to Europe for a thousand years in which heavenly beings *teach* humans metalwork and astronomy and are punished for it. He carries it home. London mocks his travels as lies for the rest of his life. The chapter ends with where his manuscripts are today.
*Intent:* a real example of the pattern in the professor's list (knowledge *given*, and the messenger laughed at), told as a story, no commentary.

**12 · Frank's kitchen** (J)
Frank invites James round to debunk things for him. James kills a viral video linking earthquakes to full moons in a minute (no baseline, cherry-picked dates: "a sock drawer"). He kills the "phantom time" theory (that three centuries of the Middle Ages were invented) in four minutes with tree rings and eclipse records, then asks why a theory that dies on tree rings gets a documentary: because ridicule aimed at bad theories trains people to dismiss good ones. Frank shows his own two-column list of claims, "still standing" and "dead," and his trump card: the real 1953 government panel that recommended debunking UFO reports through mass media. James reads the real text and says what it implies: a standard of proof that was taught until nobody remembered it was taught. Frank can't put the memo in either column: "the memo's the table." On the stairs Frank half-remembers the orphan job for one second.
*Intent:* the book's rule for conspiracies, shown: noise is killed on the page, and the one real document is a method, not a secret.

**13 · The math** (J)
James goes to the professor's daughter, Els, at her curtain shop. She refuses him; everyone who comes about her father is polite and she's had enough. He goes back a week later with the truth and the dealer's letter, and she gives him a folder she found under the lining paper of her mother's dresser: three photocopied pages headed *Preferred Formulations, fourth revision*. Two columns, "deprecated" and "preferred": *was given to → arose among; taught by → developed by; remembered → imagined*. A page on dates (always prefer the later, the shallower, the simpler-first). And a page headed *When a reader notices*: answer courteously, let him publish in a managed venue, and if he persists, review him; "contradiction confirms; the preferred response to a correct reader is agreement, followed by reclassification." James does the arithmetic of how many people a thing like this needs (not a cabal; about one in five hundred, asked to do one small thing once) and starts withdrawing from everyone. Ana catches him lying about why he's up at four; he says "reading"; she is quiet for a day. A recruiter's email arrives from a consultancy with a joke about his golf that could only come from the club website; the consultancy's only director is called Kalder; the website captions him "J. Klippe and companion, every Wednesday at seven."
*Intent:* the style guide is the organisation's method in writing; James learns it is a *style*, not a lie; the handler's name enters on paper.

**14 · The seventh tee** (J)
On his Wednesday morning round a man on the sixth green waves him through: fifties, good coat, bad golfer, funny about it. He asks how old Buddy is before he asks James's name, calls the dog "foreman," calls James "Jim," chips in from an impossible lie and says "I'm never going to be able to explain that to anyone." Over coffee he's in "continuity," the business of keeping a copy of things, and hands James a real did-you-know about the term "unidentified anomalous phenomena." He says he'll bring a flask next week. In a paragraph James takes the job at the finance house the recruiter offered, mostly because somebody made him laugh. He drives home happy and doesn't ask why.
*Intent:* the reader, who has the name Kalder from the email and the canal from V1, watches James make a friend.

**15 · A car park. Thursday** (K)
Kalder reports by phone to a superior from a car park. He says James "has stopped asking." The voice corrects him: stopped asking out loud, which is worse, and moves the review from the end of the year to the end of the quarter because of that verb. Kalder is told to get "near enough to know what he loves." He answers: the old dog, the wife, being right about a thing nobody else checked. He has bought a flask, because he told James he was a flask man and wanted it to have been true.
*Intent:* show that the date moves on handlers' sentences, and that Kalder's cover is becoming a habit.

**16 · Leiden** (J)
A stranger messages James on the forum: the polite correction he got on his post used a phrase her old supervisor showed her on a photocopy in 2009. She is Noor, the professor's last doctoral student, who left the university when its special collections were "restructured" and now repairs paper. In her kitchen she confirms the style guide is a template, identifies entry 26 as Verhoef's 1993 paper (changed from "shared" to "coincident," the only entry about the present, not the past), and explains why she is alive: she has never published a word; the organisation stops people being *heard*, not talking. She shows him a real 1999 French military report whose last chapter describes, as a thought experiment, how to manage a population through sacred texts, films and "suitable sects," and notes that its authors feared only being seen up close. On the doorstep she tells him he's counting the wrong column: not how many run the machine, but how many have noticed and stayed quiet. And: "Paper doesn't pay for itself. Find the money." The shelfmark in the professor's book is the depot's format; the professor had asked and been given a date.
*Intent:* James is not alone; the second column; the pointer toward the money and the depot.

**L3 · Portsmouth, New Hampshire. September 1961** (L)
True story: Betty and Barney Hill drive home from Montreal and arrive with two hours missing. The scuffed shoes, the torn dress, the dreams, the hypnosis sessions in 1964, the star map, and the way the story was retold by newspapers, a book and a film until the couple became a national joke. Told straight, with the real dates, with no verdict on what happened.
*Intent:* a real, documented case of ordinary people being handled by ridicule; the gorilla mask before James meets it.

**17 · Meerwijk** (J)
James starts at Meerwijk, a finance house four centuries old that has never thrown anything away. Saskia, his new boss, tells him nobody above her reads anything; he is "the person who reads it." After a month of reading every system, he finds the same job, same minute, same forty kilobytes, and this time with a name: a ledger line paid monthly for thirty-one years to a foundation at a post box, described as *continuity reconciliation (recall index)*. He captures the payload here too: 2,300 identifiers, Meerwijk's own staff and clients. The house pays the foundation per flagged person; the fee is a headcount. The sum has stepped up four times in thirty-one years, in clean multiples: 1996, 2008, 2019, and this July, the week he opened his ticket at his old firm. He tells nobody, including Ana, and knows that's wrong.
*Intent:* the money is found, the fee is explained, and James sees his own asking recorded as a step in a budget.

**18 · Enjoy it while it lasts** (J)
The AI monitoring tools Frank joked about arrive at Meerwijk, installed by a consultancy. James reads all four hundred pages of their default settings and finds, on page 212, that the tool is told never to report jobs that look exactly like the one he found: no owner, nightly, small payloads to unresolved addresses, classified as "noise." The ruleset is "Rev. 4" and has a review date at the end of the quarter. He asks for the ledger's history and is refused; the next day his own access is narrowed by exactly the one category he needs. On the sixth green Dave advises him to "ask the person who'd be embarrassed," the name on the consultancy's invoice. He emails; a "Julian K." fixes it in eleven minutes and signs off with the same phrase. James notices and files it as coincidence.
*Intent:* the blind spot is built into the tools everyone buys; the handler helps him, by email, to get closer.

**19 · A room with a long table** (K)
Bram, the organisation's "one who talks," and Kalder. Bram shows the weekly sheet for James: a left column, "reporting," which the nightly job produces by counting who is asking, posting and visiting; and a right column, "hands," which no machine produces. The right column is filled by handlers who have themselves crossed, because only a person who has had the experience can see it in someone else; the organisation has six such handlers, and Kalder is one. The index counts people whose fear of death is leaving them. A frightened person can be given a seat; a person who has stopped being afraid cannot be housed, bought or ridiculed, and the review exists for populations where too many of them appear. Bram wants to know the week James "gets to the floor." He adds that the wife will find the schedule and should be allowed to.
*Intent:* the reader learns what the count counts, how the machine measures it, and that the machine's only instrument for the thing it fears is people like Kalder.

**V3 · Leiden, nine years ago** (V)
Kalder, with a clipboard, overseeing the transfer of the restructured library's collection, spends three weeks learning everyone's names. On the last day Noor makes him tea and says nothing for twenty minutes, and the thing goes through him in her kitchen. He drives to Zwolle, sits in the car, files his own crossing, watches the review move forward because of it, and asks for more names. His seat is a promotion he wanted.
*Intent:* the reader learns Kalder has hands and chose to hunt with them; James will not learn this until the table.

## MOVEMENT III — THE SHIFT

**20 · The gorilla mask** (J)
Ana insists on a barbecue so she can meet "Wednesday golf man." Frank tells the story of the 1943 test pilot in a gorilla mask (the truth with something absurd attached so the witness discredits himself). James discovers his twelve-year-old forum persona has been edited: three sentences added in a hundred, a line about bloodlines, and his deleted question about NDEs posted under his name with four hundred replies; his correction is seen by eleven people. Dave is wonderful with everyone, listens to Ana talk about her grandmother, and finishes one of James's did-you-knows with a private phrase James has only ever written in the Record. Ana: "He's kind. Keep him." James asks Dave whether he minds Buddy not coming near him; Dave says nobody has ever asked him that. Buddy stays away from Dave for four hours. James decides to stop trying to be believed.
*Intent:* the mask applied to James; the friendship at its warmest with the reader knowing the canal; the one exchange Kalder will later cite.

**21 · On land** (J)
After eighteen holes, the pub, with Ana. Dave mentions "that professor's place near Deventer," a town James has never said or written anywhere he could read. James tests him with a wrong town; Dave accepts the correction and nothing leaves his face. He makes a threat as a joke about how fast nice houses burn, "good street, dogs," and leaves: "Good game, Jim. Let's not play another." James feels sad, not frightened: he'd had a friend for nine weeks. In the car he can't tell Ana without putting her inside it, holds up the tinfoil hat, and for the first time in eleven years the joke doesn't land. Ana: "One evening you're going to tell me, and it's going to be too late to be the evening I wanted."
*Intent:* the handler reveals himself to James; the marriage's jokes stop.

**22 · The schedule** (J)
James signs out of the archive a document the foundation produces and the house administers: the quarter's field schedule. It lists records being moved "for conservation" and a column headed *Visit* with eleven addresses, dated, accelerating toward the end of the quarter: Noor's street, the dealer's shop, the curtain shop, and his own house, eleven days away, with the word *companion*, the club website's word for Buddy, in the notes. Ana reads it upside down on the kitchen table and asks why their address is on it. He tells her the truth: it's the dog, and he thinks it's the man from the pub. She uses his name for the first time, says she is leaving the address and not him, takes Mora because Mora bites, leaves Buddy because he can't do her mother's stairs, and makes him promise to ring at nine every night.
*Intent:* the review is on paper with their address on it; Ana leaves because of danger, not distance.

**L4 · The Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem. September 1991** (L)
True story, with one invented graduate student as witness: the Dead Sea Scrolls kept by a small editorial team for forty years under the word "conservation," and the month in 1991 when scholars reconstructed texts from a concordance and a California library released its photographs, breaking the monopoly without a fight. Ends with where the announcement was printed.
*Intent:* "for conservation" is a lock; locks break by copies, not by force. The reader has just read the schedule.

**V4 · A street. Night** (V)
The visit, on the scheduled night. Kalder walks the street twice. No Doberman. The old dog watches him from the window; he says "hello, foreman" to the glass. Eleven minutes in the car. Then the back lane, the fourth gate, the water bowl inside it. He does what he does. In the morning his report reads: *subject not approached; the old dog is not a problem.*
*Intent:* the book stops being coy: he poisons the dog. The reader knows; James never will for certain.

**23 · The vet** (J)
At two in the morning Buddy is on the floor with his legs going. The emergency vet is kind and unhurried: "not tonight." James sleeps on the kitchen floor next to the dog; they watch each other. He tells Ana the honest version: the visit date was yesterday, nobody came, and he will never know. He sends Noor a barbecue photo to ask about the forum crank and she rings him: the man by the heater is Julian Kalder, who oversaw the library transfer nine years ago and brought a flask. It takes James a full minute to connect the consultancy, the email, the van, Deventer. He goes to Leiden to make Noor a public witness; she refuses, because needing to be believed is what gets people reviewed, and instead gives him a shelfmark: the item the professor was trying to see, the same number as the last page of the thin book. Nobody has ever simply asked for it. Two days later she messages "there's a van on my street," then her number dies and her account is deleted.
*Intent:* James finally checks a person; his method costs Noor; the appointment is now in his hand.

**V5 · Leiden. Friday** (V)
Noor's visit from Kalder's side. In one afternoon, with a clipboard and a kind voice, he dismantles her life without touching her: her freelance clients cancel, her mother's care-home place is "under review," her phone contract and forum account close, her bank runs a "security check." He uses her first name. She makes him tea again. She never raises her voice.
*Intent:* what "going quiet" costs, shown; the chapter that makes her parcel at the end land.

**24 · A corridor. He is sixteen** (J-past)
Third person. James at sixteen in a hospital corridor with a coin for the vending machine while his father dies. He has eleven questions about the machines and asks none. A doctor says "leave it for now" about paperwork and he learns there is a category of thing that is handled and goes away under someone's arm. A nurse says "he's in a better place" and the boy thinks *based on what* and then *don't*, and puts the question where there's nowhere to put it. He takes his father's hand only after it can't mean anything. The coin goes in the machine and nothing comes out.
*Intent:* the origin of the fear, the deflection, and the phrase "leave it," without commentary.

**25 · The floor** (J)
Alone with Buddy for four nights, James sits on the kitchen floor and does nothing, because he has run out of paper to check. On the fourth night he stops trying, and the thing happens, sober and without light: he understands the hand from the funeral is one he has seen in flashes all his life and filed; he sees a sequence of short, ordinary lives, each cut off by someone's administration; and the fear he has carried since the corridor is simply gone. He checks the kitchen afterward like a system: oven clock 02:14, fridge humming, not glowing. He tells the dog he thinks he has a soul and apologises for it. He understands the forty-one edits are one edit: every one removes the thing that passes between people, which is the one thing that can't be gated because it isn't information. The professor's pencil note in *Blindsight*, "Not nobody. Not you," was him arguing with a book that said nobody is home. He rings Ana at nine: "I'm okay." She hears it. "Bring the hat."
*Intent:* the crossing, flat and domestic; the theme stated once, by James, and then left.

**L5 · Nuremberg. Dawn, 14 April 1561** (L)
True story: a printer interviews townspeople who saw shapes in the sky at dawn and cuts a woodcut broadsheet, choosing words for things he has no words for. No verdict on what they saw. Ends with where the broadsheet is today.
*Intent:* the outer world doing what James just did on the floor: reporting something with the wrong vocabulary and being filed.

**26 · The long table, again** (K)
Kalder reports the crossing to Bram: the week, the hour, the light left on, "he said soul to the dog." Bram asks how he knows, and makes him say it: nine years ago, Leiden, his own crossing, filed, the seat taken. Kalder says "James has hands," using the first name for the first time. Bram writes one word, brings the review forward to two weeks, and sends Kalder to the depot on a records transfer, away from James, while Bram himself goes to make the offer "somewhere with the wife." Bram also tells him the dog was handled on the organisation's order regardless of his report.
*Intent:* the date moves because James crossed; Kalder learns his own word didn't count.

## MOVEMENT IV — THE INSTRUMENT

**27 · Twenty minutes** (J)
At Ana's mother's house James puts every object on the tablecloth and explains each one in order, labelling what he can't prove, ending with his hand flat on the cloth for the thing that isn't an object: the floor. Ana counts six "I don't know"s and says she has never seen him without the heavy thing he's been carrying. She doesn't believe any of it and she believes him. She refuses to hide: they will all stay there, and if someone comes to the door she will open it, because she is the only one of them who is "not in."
*Intent:* the marriage repaired on honesty; the plan for the table is Ana's.

**28 · The other ten** (J, new)
James checks the other ten addresses on the field schedule against public records. Three are "resolved" this quarter, each plausibly: an archivist after a short illness, a man in Zwolle who drove into a canal, a child moved to another school. Four have gone quiet online the way Noor did. He drives to Zwolle and sits with the widow, who says people have been very kind, and that a man came about the pension and brought a flask. He tells her nothing.
*Intent:* the review is not a future event; it is running, one address at a time, and it looks like weather. The stakes become concrete.

**29 · The band** (J)
At 3 a.m. at Meerwijk James asks the engineer's question: a thermostat that only cools is a freezer, so what does the machine do when too few people are asking? He reads the foundation's grants, which the house administers, against the index: a documentary company in 2003, a publisher of footnoted nonsense in 2011, a retreat centre in 2014, a streaming channel, a festival, the library restructuring in 2017. Every grant to noise fell in a year the index was low. The machine funds the sock drawer to keep the number in a band, so that the people who look properly always have a gorilla standing next to them. A man who has stopped being afraid isn't warm or cold; he isn't on the dial.
*Intent:* the mechanism completed by James himself, from money, not from a confession.

**L6 · A river town, before paper** (L, optional)
Invented: two men in a real ancient city agree the first list of words that are to be said one way and not another. The reader recognises the same machine, far back. Keep only if the organisation should feel older than printing.

**30 · Leiden. A printer's shop. 1931** (K-past)
A visitor in a good coat asks a careful printer to reset one line on page 118 of a dead man's book and leave the misprints and the flaw in the margin, because a clean plate is a confession and a dirty one is just a printer. He offers no threat, only the information that a foundation will be placing work in Leiden for forty years. The printer sets the line himself at night. The foundation placed work for forty-one years. The card with the two versions of the line was thrown away by a grandson in 1994.
*Intent:* the origin of James's sentence, and the method Kalder will use on the last sheet.

**31 · The depot** (J, new)
James writes to the foundation's public depot, by post, under his own name, asking to see one item by shelfmark, citing the public catalogue. They say yes, because refusal is the only answer that confirms anything. He drives to a legitimate building with a car park and a sign, is given a badge, and is brought a notebook on a tray: the foundation's own first revision from 1931, handwritten, in the same five-field format the professor's grandfather used; its first entry is page 118. The professor died three weeks before seeing this. Kalder walks in carrying a transfer box. Across the reading-room table, in whispers, he tells James that Bram is coming on Sunday to the mother-in-law's house, to bring the old dog, and that the review is two weeks away because Kalder himself moved it with one true line. It is a threat dressed as a favour: a frightened man can be seated. He does not use his badge for the lower floor. James returns the notebook and thanks the woman at the desk.
*Intent:* James acts, the "Ask" thread fires, the organisation's whole history is one list, and the two men meet without masks.

**32 · The table** (J)
The cold open, reached. Bram arrives for tea; Ana answers the door. He will confirm nothing. James walks him through the objects and Bram admits nobody has ever got "the band." Bram tells the story he believes: every time enough people stop being afraid, they build something together, the frightened majority burns it and them, and the next religion and the next war are built on the story of who gave the fire; so the organisation removes the giver from every story and keeps the number of the unafraid inside a range where they can be laughed at instead of followed; "it's not cruelty, it's capacity." He offers five seats; companions are not seated. He slides over the two-column sheet: James's code, and in the same class, a code dated nine years back that James matches to the handler reference on the schedule. The reveal that hurts: the first person he's met who has had the experience is the one who was sent to count him, and it didn't make him good. Ana asks Bram directly whether any of it is true; Bram says he cannot answer in this room; James tells her "don't," and she understands the rule. She says she is not in, that she doesn't believe it, and that she believes her husband, and squeezes his hand. Bram says he will report the refusal accurately, that he has never seen what happens to a man who just sits there, and leaves.
*Intent:* the offer refused by a man who doesn't need rescuing; the villain's crossing revealed as an object; Ana's line, once.

**V6 · The depot. Monday** (V)
Kalder checks in the transfer, which includes the master weekly sheet. Beside James's code, in Bram's hand: *hands confirmed*, and Sunday's note, *offer declined, not afraid, review as scheduled*. With the same black pen, spacing held, nothing else on the page touched, he changes the one word to *reporting ceased*, the category Noor has been in for nine years. He does it to save himself: the handler of the most recent confirmed crossing gets reviewed; the handler whose subject went quiet gets moved on. The Monday reading is down by one. The review is quietly returned to the end of the year, then to a date nobody at Bram's level is told.
*Intent:* the plate reached into one last time, by the man who learned the method, for his own reasons. The reader knows; James never will.

**33 · Leave it** (J)
Nothing happens. The date passes. They move home. Saskia tells him the committee has read his forty-page report and decided to "leave it," a direct quote. The ledger sum steps down for the first time in thirty-one years. James will never know whether that was because he went quiet or because someone changed a word; ninety-nine per cent, the last one never comes. He understands the machine has filed him as noise, with the first lie anyone told him to his face. Frank makes a third column for his list. A parcel arrives for Ana from Leiden: a four-hundred-year-old page, repaired so you have to hold it to the light to find the join, no note; Noor is quiet, and okay. On the bench by the sixth green one Wednesday, a full flask of hot coffee and nobody in sight; Buddy gets out of the cart and sits beside it.
*Intent:* the ending as extension, not victory; the handler can still reach the sixth green at seven in the morning.

**V7 · A market. Sunday** (V)
A market stall, two crates of books on the cobbles, a man in a good coat kneeling and going through them spine by spine, the way James does. Beard Man, mid-sentence: "Chap took twelve." The man thanks him and buys a condensed Hemingway for fifty cents. The next name.
*Intent:* the monster's last image: it never ends, it just moves on.

**34 · Coda** (J)
Four years later Buddy dies at home on his blankets at a quarter past two, with both of them on the floor. Nobody says "he's in a better place." They bury him in the garden by the back gate and James says the only true thing he has: he doesn't know where the dog is, he knows where he's been, and it was enough. A puppy, Ida, arrives in the summer and in the autumn sits at the bottom of the stairs watching James go up, exactly as Buddy did, a thing she never saw. He files it under coincidence and asks the reader to hold it. Ana wakes one morning and says she dreamed his hands were different, cracked at the knuckles, and that she knew them from somewhere. Frank sends the new revision of the monitoring defaults: page 212 has been removed, no note. Ana looks at the garden and says "He's in the garden," the one claim about the dead anyone in the house has been able to verify. "Check."
*Intent:* grief with a container; the almost-recognition, possible and not promised; the last word.
