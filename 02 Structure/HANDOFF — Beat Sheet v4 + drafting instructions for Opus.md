## 1. The book in one paragraph (so you never lose it)

James Klippe works in IT — his tools are eyes; the thing that's dying is never his. At his wife's grandmother's funeral he says *yes* at eighty-five per cent to a question nobody checks, and the search begins. In a dead professor's crate he finds a 1926 book whose second impression was silently corrected two years after the author vanished — and a handwritten list of forty more. He verifies them the way he'd verify anything: slowly, with controls, publishing his failures. The edits all push one way — *nothing was given* — and they cluster after every comfort peak, accelerating. Somebody isn't hiding history; they're maintaining it, with a style guide, and the same three names sit on the board that chooses which pages survive and the board that chooses which people do. They don't kill him. They correct him, ridicule him, try to buy him, and finally send a charming man to play golf with him. His method fails four times on the page. His marriage nearly does. The one system he never instrumented was himself, and when he finally does — sober, on a kitchen chair, with an old dog watching — the flashes he's been misfiling since he was six turn out to be the same list. He walks into the archive by asking. He splits the guards from the keepers with a roster. He stays inside, as an absence, so the thing he built at four in the morning stays armed. Years later a dog dies and nobody says *he's in a better place.* That's the revolution.

**Genre contract (Santi's, exact):** *tight like Watts or Weir; conclusions by testing; nothing fed to the reader to move the plot; everything real in it can be looked up.* And Santi's own gloss, which is the real key to James: what makes him dig is never how outrageous a thing is — it's **unanswered questions and coincidences.** Bush in Dallas. Ruth Paine's family. Why archaeology found it easier to redefine a whole category of human behaviour than to move a date. Why Enoch survived in Ethiopia and nowhere else. He doesn't care about the magic bullet. He cares about the four things touching the same node at the same timestamp.

## 4. Voice — the rules Opus drafts under

Santi's chapters are the calibration. Here is what they do, stated as rules, with the failure modes I fell into in 5–18 so you don't repeat them.

**4.1 The register recipe.** Serious observation → concrete detail → a joke that's actually a confession. The joke *delivers* the seriousness; it never interrupts it.
> *"I stood there in my one dark suit and did the job you do at funerals when the grief isn't yours. You hold the coat. You keep the water bottle… I was good at it, I think. I had no idea what I was doing."* (ch. 1)

**4.2 One landed line per scene. Two per chapter, maximum.** Then flat connective sentences on purpose: *I checked the page again. Nothing. I went to bed.* The narrator is not allowed to notice how good his sentences are. **My drafts overshoot this** — Gemini's critique ("over-signposting") is correct. Specifically:
- *"I want that on the record"* — it's the Switch's tell and must stay rare. **Budget for ch. 19–33: four uses total.** It currently appears ~14 times in ch. 5–18; the retro pass will thin it.
- *"I wrote it in the file"* / *"I put it in the file"* — cap at once per chapter, and only when the file matters later.
- Do not end consecutive sections on an aphorism. One in three sections ends flat.

**4.3 Dialogue over monologue.** If a fact can enter through a conversation, it enters through a conversation. Ana and the dogs are the licensed listeners; now also the ally, Beard Man, Els, the Keeper, and Dave. Dialogue is formatted as dialogue. **The best chapters in the draft are the ones that are mostly talk** (10, 11, 13, 14) — Santi confirmed this ("explanations through dialogue"). Act 2B and 3 are drafted that way.

**4.4 No jargon in anyone's mouth.** Narration may use James's professional metaphors (queues, defaults, margins, what's normal). **Nobody speaks them.** Not James at a pub, not Dave, not the Keeper. Gemini wrote *"clear the cache"* and *"the system auto-saves"* into the reveal scene and Santi called it shit. He was right. Threats are made in plain English about houses, insurance, dogs, wives.

**4.5 IT analogies in narration — allowed, rationed, and Santi will prune.** He said so explicitly. One per section at most. Not every observation needs a monitoring frame; some of the best lines in ch. 1–4 have none (*"the folklore equivalent of a comma"*, *"a doorman who knows you don't belong but isn't paid enough to say so"*).

**4.6 Not Langdon.** Insights *happen to him* — the dogs answer a question he wasn't asking; the wife notices in nine seconds what he missed in eleven days. Every inference smaller than the last draft's. He is wrong first, on the page, before he is right.

**4.7 Hedging is the texture.** *I want to be accurate. I don't want to oversell it. That's the honest version.* Holly-Gibney register. Keep it; don't let it become tic — vary the phrasing.

**4.8 Swearing is punctuation.** *"Fuck. I don't hate my job at all. But fuck."* Sparing. Ana swears more freely than he does.

**4.9 Darkness budget.** One dark joke every few pages, and dark means *an implication he declines to examine*, never a label.

**4.10 The Faucet.** Public did-you-knows: fringe, real, light. Budget already spent by ch. 20 — **zero after the buy**, and Ana notices the silence. Reader-facing italic closers (ominous, never spoken) — use at most three more in the whole book.

**4.11 Ana is never offstage for a whole chapter without a written reason.** And — Gemini's one fully correct note — **her tolerance must tighten faster than it does in 10–14.** She accepts the sixty-per-cent version too gracefully. Retro: ch. 14's breakfast lie should cost him something visible; ch. 19 gets an Ana beat where she catches a second lie and doesn't let it go.

**4.12 The dogs are not props.** Buddy is a foreman; Mora is a helicopter that has crashed and made its peace with it. They are in the room. When something happens to Buddy (ch. 23) it happens to *him*, not to the plot.

**4.13 Tense and person.** First person past for James, always. Third person past for interludes and cutaways. Never present tense. Never a second POV inside James's chapters.

**4.14 Chapter shape.** 1,800–3,200 words. Short scenes with `---` between them. Ordinary life warm before it breaks. End on a hook or a flat true sentence — never on a speech.

**4.15 Names.** He never says his own name. It arrives from other mouths and paper. Ana: Mulder / Scully / *James* (rare; a slap). Frank: *James*, cheerfully. The Keeper: *James Klippe*, both names, once. Dave: *Jim* — nobody else calls him that, and it's the first thing that should have bothered him.

## 7. The interludes (real only; third person; ≤1,400 words; each ends with one italic line telling the reader where the object is today)

**INTERLUDE I — Down House, Kent. January 1860.** *Function: books change between printings, and the world's most famous one did it first.* What's documented and usable: *On the Origin of Species* published 24 Nov 1859; second edition 7 Jan 1860; the final sentence of the second edition adds *"by the Creator"* (*"having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one"*); Darwin was ill (his stomach, most of his life); in a letter to Hooker of 29 March 1863 he wrote that he had *"long regretted that I truckled to public opinion & used Pentateuchal term of creation."* Later editions (5th 1869, 6th 1872) altered his timescales under pressure from Kelvin's estimates of the earth's age, which Darwin called an *"odious spectre."* Morse Peckham's 1959 variorum shows the majority of sentences changed across the six editions. **Do not** invent a publisher's letter threatening bans (Gemini did; unverified). **Do not** put words in Darwin's mouth beyond the letters. Scene: a sick man at a desk in January, with galleys, adding three words he will regret for the rest of his life. Closing italic: *The letter to Hooker is in the Darwin Correspondence Project. Anyone can read it.* **Placement:** immediately after ch. 4's last line — *If it was worth doing here — where else?*

**INTERLUDE II — Gondar, Ethiopia. 1770.** *Function: the Bringers survived in one place, and a man carried them out and was laughed at.* Fable's replacement for Gemini's "Council of Rome 382 AD" (the Damasus decree attribution is contested; don't build on it). What's documented and usable: the Book of Enoch — the Watchers who *taught* metalwork, cosmetics, astronomy, and were bound for it — quoted in the Epistle of Jude, defended by Tertullian, rejected by Augustine (*City of God* XV), absent from Athanasius's 367 canon list, **lost to Europe for a thousand years, and preserved complete only in Ge'ez, in the Ethiopian Orthodox canon.** James Bruce, a Scottish traveller, reached Gondar in 1769–70, and brought back **three manuscripts** of the Ethiopic Enoch; one went to the Bodleian, one to the King's Library in Paris. Enoch was first translated into English by Richard Laurence in 1821. Bruce's *Travels* (1790) were widely disbelieved and mocked in London. Also documented: the Ethiopian calendar sits seven to eight years behind the Gregorian because of a different reckoning of the Annunciation. Scene: a Scot in a highland court receiving a book Europe thinks doesn't exist, and the reader knowing he'll be called a liar for it. Tresidder's mirror — the traveller who walks *out* with the text. Closing italic: *Bruce's manuscripts are in the Bodleian and the Bibliothèque nationale. The Ethiopian Church still reads Enoch in the liturgy.* **Placement:** after ch. 11 (the Bringers).

**INTERLUDE III — The Rockefeller Museum, Jerusalem. September 1991.** *Function: "conservation" as a lock, and how a monopoly on a text breaks.* What's documented and usable: scrolls found from 1947; Cave 4 fragments from 1952; an international editorial team of eight under Roland de Vaux with exclusive access; decades of non-publication; Geza Vermes in 1977 calling it *"the academic scandal par excellence of the twentieth century"*; the Biblical Archaeology Review campaign; in September 1991 Ben Zion Wacholder and Martin Abegg published texts reconstructed from a concordance; on 22 September 1991 the Huntington Library in California announced open access to its photographic set; the Israel Antiquities Authority then lifted restrictions. Among the Qumran texts: Aramaic fragments of Enoch and the Book of Giants. Scene: a private, invented graduate student (allowed — private person, real event) standing outside a locked room in 1978 or 1985, and then the morning in 1991 when the photographs go public. No invented quotes from the real scholars beyond the record. Closing italic: *The Huntington's announcement is in the New York Times of 22 September 1991.* **Placement:** after ch. 20 (the buy) — the reader has just watched someone offer James a key to a locked room.

**INTERLUDE IV — Nuremberg. Dawn, 14 April 1561.** *Function: folklore and the phenomenon are the same report in different vocabularies.* What's documented and usable: on 14 April 1561, at dawn, many residents of Nuremberg witnessed an aerial spectacle of globes, crosses and tubes; the printer Hans Glaser produced a woodcut broadsheet describing it; the broadsheet survives in the Wickiana collection of the Zentralbibliothek Zürich; a comparable broadsheet by Samuel Coccius describes an event over Basel on 7 August 1566. Modern readings range from atmospheric optics (sun dogs) to the unexplained; the book takes no side. **Do not** have Glaser compare chronicles (Gemini invented that). Scene: a printer interviewing witnesses and cutting a block, choosing words for shapes he has no words for. Closing italic: *The broadsheet is catalogued at the Zentralbibliothek Zürich. The Basel one is next to it.* **Placement:** after ch. 25 — INNER has just resolved; this is the outer world doing the same thing.

## 13. Calibration scene — the pub slip (ch. 22). Match this register; don't paste it.

> Santi: *"I prefer a very tense situation where Ana is also there. Gemini's suggestion wasn't bad — just that the dialogues are extremely bad."* So here is the same beat, drafted properly. Three rules it obeys: nobody says a word of jargon; the threat is about ordinary things; James's grief is real and Dave's is absent.

The pub near the course has a back terrace with heaters, and on a Saturday in October you can sit out until nine if you keep your coat on. Ana had come to meet us. That was her idea. She'd said, on the phone, *bring him, I want to see this person who's made you nice,* and I'd laughed, and here we were.

She was on her second glass and she was telling him about the toaster.

"— three weekends," she said. "Three. Every wire in the house. I'm standing there with a toaster in my hands like a woman in a hostage situation."

"Did he find anything?" Dave said.

"He found a *label*. Somebody had labelled a fuse wrong in nineteen ninety-something." She squeezed my arm. "He was so happy. It was the happiest I've ever seen him."

"That's a good day," Dave said, and he meant it, and he smiled at me over his glass. "Cautious man. There's nothing wrong with that. Old houses go up. You read about it — that retired professor's place near Deventer, was it? Last spring."

I want to be exact about what happened in my body, because I'd read about it a hundred times and it was nothing like the descriptions.

My heart did not race. It did one thing — a single hard knock, like somebody testing a door — and then it went very slow, and my hands got cold, and everything on the terrace got extremely clear and extremely far away, the heaters, the coasters, my wife's hand on my sleeve.

I had never said Deventer to him. I have never written Deventer anywhere. There is no fire, no professor's place, no *last spring* — Hendrik Maasen died in a blue chair with the bin not put out and his daughter sells curtains four streets from the station, and the only two people alive who know that I know it are her and me.

Ana was laughing at something else already.

I took a sip. It was a long sip and I made it look like a short one.

"Deventer?" I said. "I thought that was up near Zwolle."

The heaters ticked. Somebody inside dropped a tray.

"Zwolle," Dave said. "You might be right." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the coaster. "I'm bad with those towns."

And that was it. That was the whole thing. Nothing happened to his face. I've thought about this for a long time and the honest description is that nothing *left* it — the warmth was all still there, the crinkle, the tan, the man who'd shanked one into the trees on the second and laughed so hard I'd liked him before we reached the ball. All of it stayed exactly where it was. It just stopped being attached to anything.

He knew I'd caught it. I was ninety-nine per cent. And he was looking at me the way I look at a number that has just done something a number shouldn't, and I understood that he was at ninety-nine too, and that neither of us could get the last one, and that this was going to be the arrangement now.

"Who's this professor?" Ana said. "Is this a book thing?"

"It's a book thing," I said.

"It's a shame, is all I meant," Dave said to her, easily, and put his hand flat on the table between us. "Some people spend so long looking at old wreckage they forget how quickly a new house goes up. Especially the nice ones. Good street. Dogs."

Ana made a face. "God, Dave. That's a bit morbid for a Saturday."

"Home insurance," Dave said. "Boring subject. I'll stop." He looked at his watch, and then at me, and there was nothing in it — I want that understood, nothing, no regret, no *shame it had to be you* — and he stood and put a note under his glass.

"Good game today, Jim." He put his coat on. "Let's not play another."

He kissed Ana on the cheek. He'd done it at the barbecue too. She let him.

When he'd gone she said, "Well, that got weird," and finished her wine, and I said "yeah," and sat there on a terrace under a heater next to the person I love most in the world and felt — this is the part I didn't expect, and it's the part I'd like on the record — *sad*. Not frightened. That came in the car. Sad, the way you're sad when you find out a thing you enjoyed wasn't real, because I had enjoyed it. I hadn't had a friend in eleven years and I'd had one for six weeks and he'd been very good at it.

**Why this works and Gemini's didn't:** the slip is a *town*, not a plot summary; James tests it with a wrong town, not a quip; the threat names a street and dogs, not a cache; Ana's line is the only one that says what the scene is; and the last paragraph is James's, not the villain's. Opus: the scene must end on *him*, never on Dave's exit line.
