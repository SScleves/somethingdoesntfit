## PART 0 — WHAT THE STORY IS BUILT ON

**The idea in one paragraph.** A man who watches computer systems for a bank notices that an old book was quietly changed between printings. He pulls on that thread and finds an organisation that has been doing three things for a very long time: hiding what happens when we die, keeping the world from waking up too fast, and preparing a small number of people to survive whatever comes next. He can't fight them and he can't expose them, because nobody would believe him. He beats them by understanding how they actually work: thousands of ordinary people doing small jobs without knowing what the jobs are for. He gives those people the truth about what they did, in their own paperwork, one at a time. Some are ashamed, some are frightened, some are angry, and the organisation can't run without them. It has to stop, re-think, and start again somewhere else, which takes a generation. He buys the world time. Along the way he stops being afraid of dying, nearly loses his wife, nearly loses his dog, and finds out what the organisation has been deleting from every book: the instructions for the one thing that matters.

**The three truths James finds, in the order he finds them.**
1. *What the organisation did to the world.* They changed history (every book that said knowledge was *given* now says it *arose*). They killed people who noticed (a scientist in a car on a canal road, a professor in a blue chair, an engineer in a canal). They buried science (a 1993 paper filed under "instrumentation"). They sat on inventions and ancient knowledge (the archive at the depot). They fund nonsense when too few people are asking, so the ones who look properly always have a clown beside them. This is the thriller.
2. *What happens when we die, and what they took out of the books.* The deleted sentences aren't history. They're instructions: how to die without fear, how to keep what you are across the gap, how to find the people and the dog you loved. Fear is what scatters you back into the pool; love is what makes you an individual who can't be dissolved. The organisation keeps people afraid at the end and busy in the middle so that nobody persists. This is the philosophy, and it arrives in rooms with people, never as a lecture.
3. *What ordinary people did for the machine, and what they were promised.* Every manager who said "leave it," every clerk who catalogued, every executive who signed a seat contract, every dealer who telephoned: they were told a small believable reason and promised, in a word they never examined, that they'd be *saved*. They never asked from what. James shows each of them two things: what they actually contributed to, and that they are not on the list. This is how he wins.

**WHERE EVERY REVEAL GOES**

*The opening (the reading room, December; James alone; the old head talking; Anneke silent).* Hints, not explanations. The reader gets a conspiracy and a machine on page three; the villain later shrugs at both.
- The seats: "You found the seats. Everyone who gets this far finds the seats. A bank's disaster plan, names, dependants per allocation, no dogs. We leave them findable."
- The count: "And you were counted the night you asked. Everyone who asks is counted."
- The ledger open at page 118: one line struck out, one written beside it. The reader doesn't know what it is. Four chapters later James finds it on his kitchen floor.
- A generator under the floor. Aylin: "we were expecting you." Cut. *Eight months earlier.*

*Part One (the ordinary world, then two big things).* The method, found: one edited sentence. The job at work, found and erased. The thin book with forty-one edits and a date still to come. James on the machine's own list.

*Part Two (the old stuff; proof it has always been there; the people).* Method: the style guide, the 1931 printer, the 1953 panel. Religion and the old truths, through Noor, the shelf and Beard Man: the knowledge-bringers punished in every tradition; the old gods rewritten as demons; the warden who calls himself God; "within" turned into "through us"; the mechanism edited, never the message. The afterlife layer, lightly: the reports rhyme; the bucket theory; Pythagoras and the dog; the names as passwords; the window the size of a meal. The modern paperwork: the invoice, the seat contract, the drill, the seat list. James assumes the seats are to save the organisation, probably by freezing; he's wrong until Part Three. And all the people: the corporate day, the pub, the barbecue, the promise, the schedule, the gate, the vet, Noor quiet, the corridor, the floor, Twenty minutes, the other ten, the band, Frank loose.
Part Two ends with the opening continued: James at the reading-room table puts the recent, cool stuff down in front of the old man (page 212; the grants in the cold years; the hearings as vinegar; the forums farmed; the invoice stepping up the week he asked; the eleven addresses). The old man, delighted, confirms only the general method (stories were always the tool; each new medium frightened them and then became theirs) and makes the offer: five seats, no dogs. James says no.

*Part Three (the new, outrageous reveals, and the truth).* The dinner; the phone; the compartments; the sheets; the leak; the night before; Monday. Then the final conversation, the old man defeated and talking, Anneke beside him, in this order: (1) soft disclosure was never disclosure: Men in Black, I Origins, the moon as a soul collector, Inception, Dracula and Walt Disney; (2) Philip K. Dick, handed a true thing and spending his life disproving it; (3) the internet in the nineties, social media, AI as the crown jewel: doubt is cheaper than belief; (4) the bunkers are fuel; freezing is meaningless; (5) the Dalai Lama search confirmed, the ceremony that makes the real thing unthinkable; (6) they remember: the one thing they took out of every book and kept; (7) James's callback: remembering was for leaving the cycle, not staying; the old man agrees; they chose to stay; (8) why the truth can't come out: collapse; killers and savages; (9) the deal with the new management; the one last thing; the market; leave it; the book he's allowed to publish.

*Epilogue.* Buddy, the garden, "I'll see him soon," Ida at the stairs; the parked far-future scene.

**How the organisation works, plainly.** It is not a cabal in a room. It is a method and a few hundred professionals, standing on millions of semi-aware helpers. The professionals (people like Bram and Kalder) know some of it. The helpers know none of it; they know their one task and the word "continuity." The organisation's strength is that so few people need to know anything. Its weakness is the same fact: it depends on people who don't know, and the moment they do, they stop. James attacks the compartments, not the top.

**The machine, in plain terms.** Three parts. *Editing:* every text that says something was given to humanity is quietly changed between printings so that it arose by itself; typos left in so nobody checks. *Counting:* a nightly job in the systems of companies that pay for it lists everyone who reported something that week (asked about a job, posted a question, wrote to a dealer). That's the left column. Handlers who have had the experience themselves judge who has stopped being afraid: the right column. When too many people in a population stop being afraid, there's a "review": records moved "for conservation," and people visited at home, one address at a time, each ending plausible. *Pressure:* enough ordinary load that nobody has a spare hour. And a thermostat: when too few people ask, it funds documentaries, footnoted nonsense and retreats, so the real question always has a gorilla standing next to it.

**The seats.** Banks buy "recovery seats" at a disaster-recovery site: a contract naming who gets a desk, a bed and a badge if the building is gone. The organisation hides its own seats inside that contract. The seat list is an annex to the bank's continuity plan: names, "dependants per allocation," and the line *companion animals are not accommodated.* Kalder sells it.

**The building.** The foundation's archive (a reading room, a catalogue, Aylin at the desk) sits on top of the bank's recovery site (the lower floor, the seats). James has drilled there with a lanyard and a sandwich. He keeps the dead professor's appointment there in December.

**The people.**
- *James.* Monitoring engineer at a big financial house, eleven years. His hobby is a nose, not a method: always onto something, doesn't know what he's looking for, knows it when he sees it. Checks everything. Reads people's pauses. Funny about what frightens him. Lonely in a specific way: thinks all day what nobody says.
- *Ana.* Believes her grandmother is okay now, and the book never proves her wrong. James tells her everything once. She leaves the house when it's on a list, because he asks her to. She comes back and ends the game herself, with her mother's sleeping pills, improvising.
- *Buddy* (old) and *Mora* (the Doberman). Buddy is under every table. He gets poisoned and lives. He dies in the coda, buried right.
- *Frank.* The friend. Not in it. Skeptic who teases, then loosens. Files the last ticket with James.
- *Pieter.* James's manager. A helper. Told the orphan job is a security test. His name is on the seat list. One of the people James shows the truth to.
- *Kalder.* Handsome, funny, a very good golfer, great company, likes James. Sells the bank its continuity. A handler who crossed nine years ago and took his seat. A psychopath, loyal to himself. Poisons the dog to get into the house. Never changes. Used, burned, found.
- *Bram.* The one who talks, in the handler scenes. Sincere, decent, believes the story he tells, has never had the experience he counts.
- *The old head.* The bad one. Walks into the reading room on page three; defeated and talking at the end.
- *Anneke de Wit.* The bank's head of risk. In the reading room on page three, silent, an observer; reasonable at the end; takes the chair.
- *Noor.* The professor's last student, a paper conservator in Leiden. Quiet because the professor told her to be.
- *Beard Man.* The market dealer. Ninety per cent nonsense. One of his nonsense theories is the one that turns out to be right.
- *Els, Aylin, Anneke, the Groningen dealer, the widow in Zwolle, the brewery man.* The helpers James goes to, one by one.

**Rules for the writing.** American spelling. No percentages or writers' shorthand in the prose. Real documents quoted only in their real words. Real anomalies may be real; edits, people and companies are invented; no invented edit attached to a real book. Reveals arrive as objects in James's hands, never as speeches. The book stays funny to the end. Every chapter with a table has a dog under it.

## PART 1 — EVERY CHAPTER, EXPLAINED PLAINLY

### PART ONE — NOTHING FITS

**The reading room (the opening).** It is the second of December. James drives alone to a low building behind a car park with a sign on it, to keep an appointment that a dead man made. The woman at the desk, Aylin, tells him they were expecting him; not today, but they were expecting him. Upstairs in the reading room a tray is already on the table, and on it is a handwritten ledger from 1931, open at a page where one line has been struck out and another written beside it. An old man comes in and sits down, pleasant and unhurried. A woman takes the chair by the window and doesn't say a word. The old man talks about the archive for a while, and then, as if it were nothing, he says that everyone who gets this far finds the seats: a bank's disaster plan, a list of names, dependants per allocation, no dogs. They leave them findable. And James was counted the night he asked. Everyone who asks is counted. Through the floor, a generator starts up. The chapter cuts there. Eight months earlier.
*Why it's here:* the reader gets a hidden bunker inside a bank and a machine that counts questions on page three, and no explanation. Everything after this is how James got to that table.

**The funeral.** (Santi's chapter.) Ana's grandmother is buried on a beautiful day. Everyone in the receiving line says she is in a better place. When Ana needs James to say it too, he says yes without believing it, and that small lie is what starts the book. During the service he has a flash of somewhere else: cold mud, and a hand cracked at the knuckles reaching for his. He files it under lack of sleep. On the drive home he realises nobody even pretends there's a heaven for a dog, and that the comforting words at the grave were never knowledge. The search begins.

**The good life.** (Santi's chapter.) Ordinary life after the funeral: Ana's grief that visits rather than moves in, the unfinished knitting, the two dogs. James's job is explained: he watches other people's systems die and asks the dull questions. His hobby is explained as a nose rather than a method: he is always onto something, he doesn't know what he's looking for, and when he sees the real thing he knows it. Old books that change between printings are the one branch he checks carefully. Golf is his sanctuary. One new line tells us the ten thousand systems he watches belong to a bank that has kept records for four hundred years. He finds fourteen tabs open about dying and realises the search has started without his permission.

**Reading with a pen.** (Santi's chapter.) James reads seriously about death for the first time and finds that the reports rhyme and the explanations rhyme harder. Ana finds his notebook and assumes it's about her grandmother; he lets her. He types a question into a forum and deletes it. A new passage carries the loneliness of a man who thinks all day the things nobody says and suspects everyone else does too and was taught to stop asking. At the Sunday market Beard Man has a dead professor's crates and will only sell the whole lot. James takes forty-odd books home in two bags. A thin book with no title is among them. Beard Man, mid-sentence as always, tells him his nonsense theory about souls: puppies share one cup of water, and a dog that's been loved can't be poured back in the bucket. James files it under Beard Man.

**The sentence.** (Santi's chapter.) Going through the lot, James finds a copy of a dry 1926 book he already owns, and this one is a first impression, which thrills him, and the professor has annotated it, which scandalises him. Checking the professor's abuse against his own copy, he finds that a sentence on page 118 is different, and then two more further in, all the same kind of change: something that was given now arose by itself. The typos and a blemish were left untouched so nobody would check. Playing fetch on the kitchen floor at four in the morning, he understands what the edits do: the original has a hand in it. The author vanished in Turkey in 1929. The edits are from 1931. He asks the question that drives the rest of the book: if it was worth doing here, where else? The reader has seen that struck-out line before, on page three.

**Down House, 1860.** (A real story.) Charles Darwin, sick at his desk, correcting the second edition of his book, adds the words "by the Creator" to the last sentence under public pressure, and writes to a friend years later that he regrets it. The most famous book alive changed between printings, by its own author.

**A round with a stranger.** James plays his Wednesday round alone with the dogs, and we learn why he plays golf and never team sports, and what the dogs are to him. The starter pairs him with a man playing alone: handsome, easy, with a swing James would pay for, and the man likes James. He asks about Buddy before he asks James's name. He says he's "in continuity, insurance for things that can't be insured; if your building burns down, who does your payroll on Monday." James drives home giddy, like a schoolgirl: the cool guy likes me. Much later we learn this was Kalder taking a first measure.

**Utrecht, 1993.** (The organisation, thirty years earlier.) A physics department head rings a pleasant man at a foundation to report his friend's paper, which showed two people in separate shielded rooms responding to each other. We learn how he was approached: two lunches, a question about his children's schooling, a mention of colleagues who had declined the lunch and whose departments got smaller. The foundation man calls the paper "recall" and tells him to file it under instrumentation. His reward is places of safety for his wife and children, but not his mother: "it's a budgeting thing." He goes and is kind to his friend for two hours. The friend retires early and dies in 1995 in a car on a canal road.

**Leave it.** At work James reads the full list of scheduled jobs and finds one with no owner that runs every night at fourteen minutes past two and sends forty kilobytes to an address that exists for nine seconds. He raises it in stand-up. His manager Pieter is caught off guard for a visible moment, and then has the sentence: diagnostic artefact from the migration, leave it. James did that migration; the job wasn't in it. He reads the pause before the sentence the way he reads everything. A letter arrives from an antiquarian dealer, the first time he sees his own name written by someone connected to the book, with a postscript advising him to be plain about his reasons. He opens a ticket. Three weeks later Pieter tells him the continuity programme wants him: "they asked for the person who reads things." James says thank you and notices he didn't ask who they are.

**The payload.** James does what an engineer does and captures the forty kilobytes at fourteen minutes past two. It is a list of employee IDs with a two-word reason beside each one. His own isn't on it. The night after he asks about the job in stand-up, there is one more entry, and it's him: asked (job). The job counts people who ask. Asking put him on it.

**The control case.** James tries to kill his own finding properly. He buys another copy of the 1931 printing (same three edits, so it's the whole run), which is expensive; he lies to Ana about the price and feels bad. He checks nine other books from the lot against other copies: nothing. The itch that stays is the professor's shelf, which has a pattern of subject, not of edits: VALIS, Blindsight, Ubik, a Canticle, a Borges filed with the science fiction; these were a man's conclusions. Then he opens the thin book: a notebook in two hands, the grandfather's and the professor's, forty-one edits in forty-one books. His sentence is number eleven. The last page holds a library reference number, a date, and the word Ask. The date is the second of December, still to come. The professor died in March with an appointment in his diary.

**The week it vanished.** Beard Man tells him how the professor died: in a blue chair, found because the bin wasn't put out; his daughter sells curtains; a polite man in a van bought the furniture. Then, day by day: James's ticket is closed as a duplicate of a ticket that never existed; the job disappears from the list and from every backup going back years, which should be impossible; Frank honestly doesn't remember the stand-up; Pieter dishonestly doesn't remember the job. Only James's screenshot says it was ever there. He checks who holds the backup keys: four people, and one is Pieter. He says nothing.

**A pension visit, Zwolle.** (The villain alone.) A man we aren't shown clearly visits a retired engineer who has started saying strange things about his father. He spends a kind afternoon, learns the man loves his old car and the canal road, borrows the car to move a box, and returns it washed. Three days later the man drives into the canal.

**Groningen.** James drives to the dealer who wrote to him. The old man assumes James is from the organisation and asks who sent him. Then he explains: after selling the professor the 1931 printing he was visited by polite people who asked him to telephone if anyone ever asked for that title. He telephoned about James three weeks ago, before the ticket. James explains to him what the phone call actually does, and the old man says he won't make another. The first helper, turned by accident, with one question.

*Part One ends with two things in James's hands: a dead man's list of forty-one edits with a date still to come, and the proof that his own bank counted him the night he asked and then erased the counting.*

### PART TWO — THE MACHINE HAS ALWAYS BEEN THERE

**Deventer, March.** (The villain alone.) The professor wrote to the organisation's post box asking someone to come and talk about his notebook, which broke his own rule. Somebody came. Tea, the blue chair, the notebook under the old man's hand. The visitor photographs every page while the professor sleeps and reports from the car: better to wait; a body is a fingerprint. Eleven days later the bin isn't put out. By the time the visitor returns for the furniture, the books have already gone to the market, whole lot. That is the one mistake.

**Croton, 530 BC.** (A real story.) Pythagoras stops a man beating a dog and tells him to stop: it's the soul of a friend; he knew the voice. The oldest recorded version of the thing this book ends on.

**Frank's kitchen.** Frank invites James round to debunk things for him, teasing, friendly: flat earth, a video linking earthquakes to full moons, the theory that three centuries of the Middle Ages were invented. James kills each one in minutes and then asks the better question: a theory that dies on tree rings doesn't need a documentary, so why is there one? Frank shows him his own list of claims, standing and dead, and his trump card: the real 1953 panel that recommended debunking through mass media. James reads what it implies: a standard of proof that was taught until nobody remembered it was taught. Frank can't put the memo in either column. "The memo's the table." On the stairs Frank half-remembers the orphan job for one second and the beer takes it back.

**The math.** James goes to the professor's daughter, Els, at her curtain shop. She refuses him; everyone who comes about her father is polite and she's had enough. On his way out he says one thing (still to be chosen) that stays with her. He lies to Ana about where he was. A week later Els rings: why did you say that, my father said that. She gives him a folder from under the lining paper of her mother's dresser: three photocopied pages headed Preferred Formulations, with deprecated words on the left and preferred words on the right, and a page called When a Reader Notices that says a correct reader is never to be contradicted, only agreed with and then reclassified. James does the arithmetic of how many helpers a thing like this needs, and what each would have to be told, and realises none of them would need to know the rest. He withdraws from everyone. At breakfast Ana catches the join between what he tells her and what he doesn't. He says "reading." She is quiet for a day.

**The corporate day.** The bank's golf day, which never happens, and James is excited. He is paired with two colleagues whose first words are a crass line about the cart girl. Then he sees the stranger from the spring on the practice green with a vendor's lanyard, and is honestly glad. The man does corporate relationships, lobbying mostly; he sells the bank its continuity, the recovery seats; boring. He knows the organiser, and they're paired. On the first tee he hits a 320-yard draw.

**A car park.** (The organisation.) A handler reports by phone that the subject has stopped asking. The voice corrects him: stopped asking out loud, which is worse, and moves the review date closer because of that verb. The handler is told to get near enough to know what the man loves. Nothing in the chapter identifies the golfer. (Still to decide: keep it this way, cut it, or let the reader know.)

**Leiden.** A stranger messages James on the forum: the polite correction he received used a phrase her old supervisor showed her on a photocopy in 2009. She is Noor, the professor's last student, now a paper conservator. In her kitchen she confirms the style guide is a template, identifies entry 26 as the 1993 paper, and gives James the professor's rule in his own words: I'm fairly sure I'm already being watched; until we're a hundred per cent, never publish, never ask, always hide; if anything happens to me, know I was right. She shows him a real 1999 report by French generals whose last chapter imagines managing a population through sacred texts, films and suitable sects, and notes its authors feared only being seen up close. On the doorstep she tells him he's counting the wrong column: not how many run it, how many have noticed and stayed quiet. And: paper doesn't pay for itself; find the money. The reference number in the thin book is the depot's format. The appointment is real.

**Mexico City, 1994.** (A real story.) The scientist who published the two-rooms experiment disappears without trace. Facts only.

**The drill.** James's first week on the continuity programme is a business-continuity test: forty named staff bussed to the recovery site, lanyards, a sandwich, a desk with his name on it, number forty. Pieter is twelve. Upstairs, behind a glass door he isn't badged for, is a reading room: the foundation's archive. He notes the reference-number format on a spine and doesn't connect it. Kalder is in the car park talking to the site manager, and waves.

**The money.** On the programme's invoices James finds a monthly line to the foundation at a post box, described as continuity reconciliation (recall index), billed per flagged person, and stepping up in clean multiples over thirty-one years; the last step was the week he opened his ticket. A second contract runs through Kalder's consultancy: work-area recovery, seats, annual. And the annex he now has the role to open: the seat list. Pieter is on it. Anneke, the head of risk, is on it. A brewery executive is on it. Frank isn't. James isn't. Dependants per allocation. Companion animals are not accommodated. James reads it as the organisation saving itself, probably by freezing, and is wrong about that until Part Three. He closes it and goes to get Buddy from the car.

**Enjoy it while it lasts.** The AI monitoring tools Frank joked about arrive. James reads all four hundred pages of their default settings and finds, on page 212, a rule that tells the tool never to report jobs exactly like the one he found. The ruleset has a review date at the end of the quarter. His access is narrowed by exactly that one shape. On the next round Kalder tells him to ask the person who'd be embarrassed, the name on the invoice. He emails; a Julian K. fixes it in eleven minutes and signs off with the same phrase. James notices and files it.

**A room with a long table.** (The organisation.) Bram, the one who talks, explains to the handler what the count counts. The nightly job lists everyone who reported something; that's the left column. The right column, the one that matters, is filled only by handlers who have had the experience themselves, because only they can see it in someone else. The index counts people whose fear of dying is leaving them. Bram wants to know the week James gets there. The wife will find the schedule; let her.

**Leiden, nine years ago.** (The villain alone.) Kalder, with a clipboard, overseeing the transfer of a restructured library, spends three weeks learning everyone's names. On the last day Noor makes him tea and says nothing for twenty minutes and the thing goes through him. He drives to Zwolle, files his own crossing, watches the review date move because of it, and asks for more names. The seat is a promotion he wanted.

**On land.** (The first coded scene, Ana present.) After eighteen holes, the pub, with Ana telling the toaster story. Mid-story, Kalder uses a word James read in the professor's margin that week and had to look up. James hears it and thinks: if he reads this and knows I read it, why has he never once said so. So he sets a trap the way he'd set one at work: he invents a book that doesn't exist and mentions it. Kalder nods and says he knows it. Only a man pretending would. James shows nothing. Ana laughs at something else. No threats. The evening carries on, and over the following weeks the two men play along, Kalder testing him in small clever ways, James passing without knowing if he's passing, not sure it was even a slip, sure of what it costs if it was. The friendship continues.

**The gorilla mask.** Ana insists on a barbecue to meet "golf man." Frank tells the story of the 1943 test pilot in a gorilla mask. James discovers his twelve-year-old forum persona has been edited, with bloodlines and his deleted question added; his correction is seen by eleven people. Kalder is wonderful with everyone, listens to Ana about her grandmother, finishes one of James's private lines, and says "Deventer," a town James has never written down; filed. Ana: he's kind, keep him. James asks Kalder whether he minds Buddy not coming near him; nobody has ever asked him that. Buddy stays away for four hours. That night James starts building something at four in the morning and doesn't describe it.

**The promise.** On the kitchen floor at four in the morning, more frightened than he has ever been, James tells Ana: one day I'm going to need you to believe me; I'll tell you, and you'll know what I mean, and that day I need you to run. She says, run where, I've got a Doberman; and then, in the other voice, okay. (Still to decide: exactly where this sits.)

**The schedule.** James asks Ana to go to her mother's for a while and won't say why; she refuses. Then, from the programme's records, the foundation's quarterly field schedule: records moved for conservation, a column headed Visit, eleven addresses, and theirs, with a date eleven days off and the word "companion" in the notes. Ana reads it upside down. James tells her the truth: it's the dog, and he thinks it's the man from the pub. She uses his name for the first time. She says she's leaving the address, not him. He lets her go, with Mora, knowing a house with one old dog in it is a target, because the alternative is her name on a list. Nine o'clock every night.

**Washington, 1953.** (A real story.) Five scientists, one weekend, and a recommendation to debunk through mass media. Told as a story in a room, with real words only.

**A street, night.** (The villain alone.) Kalder walks the street twice. No Doberman. The old dog stands at the window like a two-year-old guard dog and watches him. Eleven minutes in the car. The back lane, the fourth gate, the water bowl. He does what he does. In the morning his report reads: the old dog is not a problem.

**The vet.** At two in the morning Buddy is on the floor with his legs going. The emergency vet is kind and unhurried: not tonight. James comes home at six to a house with nothing touched and the thin book gone. He sleeps on the kitchen floor beside the dog. At nine he tells Ana the honest version. He sends Noor a barbecue photo to ask about the forum crank, and she rings him: the man by the heater is Kalder; he oversaw the library transfer nine years ago; he brought a flask. By now James thought he knew. This is the nail. He goes to Leiden to make Noor a witness and she refuses, because that's the rule and it's what killed the professor; instead she gives him a shelfmark, the same number as the thin book's last page, and tells him nobody has ever simply asked for it. Two days later she messages that there's a van on her street. Then nothing.

**Leiden, Friday.** (The villain alone.) Kalder makes Noor quiet in one afternoon with a clipboard and a kind voice: her clients, her mother's care-home place, her accounts. She makes him tea again.

**A corridor, sixteen.** (James's past, in the third person.) His father is dying in a hospital. He has eleven questions and asks none. A doctor says "leave it for now" about paperwork. A nurse says "he's in a better place." The boy thinks, based on what, and then thinks, don't. He takes his father's hand only when it can't mean anything. He puts a coin in the vending machine and nothing comes out and he leaves it.

**The night he stops being afraid.** Alone with Buddy, James sits on the kitchen floor for four nights and does nothing. On the fourth he stops trying, and the thing happens, sober and without light: the hand from the funeral is one he has seen all his life and filed; the lives he sees are short and ordinary and cut off by somebody's administration; and the fear he has carried since the corridor is gone. He checks the kitchen afterward like a system: the oven clock says 02:14, nothing is glowing. He tells the dog he thinks he's got a soul and apologises. He understands the forty-one edits are one edit: they all take out the thing that passes between people. He rings Ana: I'm okay. She hears it.

**Urfa, 1994.** (A real story.) A German archaeologist climbs a hill the farmers call Potbelly and finds worked stone, and the date of civilisation moves by six thousand years. The hill is fifteen kilometres from where Tresidder walked east in 1929. The book never says he found it.
