# Story blocks — the library

Every conspiracy, case, document, theory, method and scene seed gathered for the book: 639 blocks from 20 sources. The same list lives in the Atlas site, where each block can be dragged onto a chapter.

Reality tags: **Documented** is real and on the record. **Contested** has real evidence on both sides. **Claim** is a claim with thin or no evidence. **Invented** is fiction made for the novel.

## MindBook brief (110)

### Vallée's thermostat with two principles
*Theory · Claim · Vallée, UFOs: The Psychic Solution*

Jacques Vallée argued that the sightings do not show successive visits from space and instead show a control system, and that the variable being controlled is human belief. He compared it to a house thermostat, which needs two opposite principles to work, one that heats and one that cools, and he pointed out that a child can climb on a chair and turn the dial. He left open whether the system is natural, artificial or governed by laws not yet discovered. In a story it describes a machine that holds belief inside a band, so it has to push belief up when it runs low as well as down when it runs high.

Might fit: The band: James finds the machine has a lower limit as well as an upper one

### Vallée's single reinforcement graph
*Document · Contested · Vallée, UFOs: The Psychic Solution; UFOs: The Psychic Solution, Jacques Vallée (1975)*

Vallée backs his control-system idea with one graph, Figure 3, a monthly index of UFO activity from 1947 to 1962 with the trend removed. He borrows from Ferster and Skinner the point that the strongest reinforcement schedule combines periodicity with unpredictability, and he asks, as a question, whether his graph shows such a schedule. He names two colleagues who suggested the idea and says he did not extend the series past 1962 because there was too much work for one man. A fictional machine could have the same flaw, with an index that someone built once and nobody checked.

Might fit: The math: James is the first person to ask what the trend removal did

### The phenomenon must mislead us
*Theory · Claim · Vallée, UFOs: The Psychic Solution*

Vallée writes that if the phenomenon is forcing humanity through a learning curve, it must mislead us, and that we may be ready to fall into a kind, benevolent trap. He reports the formula the ufonauts themselves use, "you should believe in us but not too much," and says genuine contact would ruin the experiment. In the same chapter he disclaims the jail reading, saying he does not mean a higher order has locked us in a space-bound prison and does not propose to redefine God.

### Campbell's recyclable awareness
*Theory · Claim · Campbell, My Big TOE*

In My Big TOE, physicist Tom Campbell describes "recyclable awareness" as a designed feature of the reality he calls a space-time trainer. Beings periodically start a new lifetime with their personal delusions and fears wiped but their accumulated wisdom kept, so growth can keep accelerating. A reset built by people would be a poor copy of this, wiping memory and keeping fear.

### Campbell's politics of reality
*Theory · Claim · Campbell, My Big TOE*

Campbell argues that reality has politics, made of ego, fear and belief traps enforced by cultural, scientific, political or religious authority. He writes that fear, control and conformity make ignorance their friend. His psi uncertainty principle says psychic ability stays limited because it would harm the growth of a low-quality, high-entropy consciousness. A machine that imitated this rule would limit awakening for everyone.

### COMETA's four phases of contact
*Document · Documented · COMETA report*

The political chapter of the COMETA report asks its authors to put themselves in the shoes of extraterrestrials and then sets out four phases of contact. First comes covert observation for a thousand years, "and why not?", then furtive appearances that could not go completely unnoticed. Next comes influence by providing sacred texts and accentuating the intellectual and moral tendencies of select individuals. Last, contact is made discreetly at the highest level of states and kept secret, and the contacted leaders then run information, disinformation and counterinformation campaigns.

### The London Controlling Section
*Case · Documented · Vallée, Messengers of Deception; Messengers of Deception, Jacques Vallée (1979)*

Turing's Ultra decoding machines stayed secret until 1974. Vallée uses this record to argue that governments can keep a secret for decades, and that deception work is protected even from friendly services.

### The 1952 flap
*Case · Documented · Peebles, Watch the Skies!; Watch the Skies!, Curtis Peebles (1994)*

Peebles describes the 1952 flap as a loop in which publicity, reporting channels and public sky-watching fed each other. Life ran "Have We Visitors from Space?" on April 7, 1952 and Air Force Letter 200-5 widened reporting, producing 1,501 sightings for the year and 536 in July alone. The two July weekends over Washington followed, and the Civil Aeronautics Administration found that a temperature inversion had been indicated in almost every instance.

### Scully's saucer book and the True article
*Case · Documented · Peebles, Watch the Skies!*

Frank Scully's book about crashed saucers was demolished by a single article in True magazine, which found that a piece of the supposed craft was a disk of "grade 2S aluminum" of the type used in pots and pans. Peebles notes that for the next twenty-five years any such tale was relegated to the far fringes. One well-aimed debunking can bury a whole category.

Might fit: The leak

### AFR 200-2 and JANAP-146
*Document · Documented · Peebles, Watch the Skies!*

Two Air Force rules shaped what witnesses could say about sightings. AFR 200-2 barred releasing information on a sighting until it was solved, and JANAP-146 put reports from pilots and others under Espionage Act penalties. The people best placed to report a sighting had the least freedom to talk about it afterward.

### MJ-12 forgery analysis
*Document · Documented · Peebles, Watch the Skies!; Santi's ledger: MJ-12 card (Peebles, Watch the Skies); Watch the Skies!, Curtis Peebles (1994)*

Peebles sets out the evidence against the May 1987 "Majestic 12" briefing document item by item. The Cutler memo carries a classification marking not used at the National Security Council until Nixon, and Cutler was out of the country on its date. Philip Klass showed that the Truman signature is identical to one on another 1947 letter, which cannot happen twice, and that the memo was typed on a machine first sold in 1963.

Charles Moeller's introduction says the devil's trump card is his anonymity, and that his masterpiece is the belief that he does not exist. He notes how writers play with this, such as Gide's devil asking why anyone should fear him when they know he does not exist. For Moeller a cover that convinces everyone the subject is imaginary is the strongest cover there is.

### Evidence that can never be proven
*Theory · Claim · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Moeller argues that the devil acts only through the senses and imagination, which are always ambiguous, so no case can ever be proven the way geometry is proven. The medical files in the book stay inconclusive, and Moeller says this is exactly what the devil's method would produce. The argument is built so that missing proof counts in its favor.

### John of the Cross: counterfeit first
*Method · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In the writings of St. John of the Cross, the devil begins by counterfeiting whatever God is doing in a soul. He can offer only a simulation, like a bankrupt dressed in a rich man's clothes. He tells truths first to win trust, as a leather worker draws a stiff bristle behind soft thread, and then slips in the lie. The saint also warns of a compromised spiritual director who asks his follower to obtain revelations for him.

### Counterfeit providence by arranged coincidence
*Method · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Joseph de Tonquédec says the devil cannot work true miracles. He can only counterfeit miracle and counterfeit providence, by grouping certain elements and arranging the coincidence of apparently accidental circumstances. That is the book's own description of how a pattern of accidents could be steered by someone who cannot touch the will directly.

### The cross as a mousetrap
*Theory · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Early Christian writers explained the Crucifixion as a trick played on the devil. Origen spoke of a coin paid to him, others used a fish hook baited with Christ or a hole in the dragon's jaws, and Augustine called the cross a mousetrap. Bernard Leeming catalogs these images and then defuses them as metaphors.

### Isaiah's Lucifer was borrowed
*Theory · Contested · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

A. Lefèvre, a Jesuit scholar, concedes that the Old Testament nowhere clearly reveals the fall of an angel. He calls Isaiah's 'Lucifer' literary poaching from the fall of the Babylonian god Enlil, and treats the desert animals and the Eden serpent as images. He still holds that Satan is real, but says the fall story comes mostly from outside the Hebrew Bible.

### Satan began as a courtroom job
*Theory · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In the Hebrew Bible 'satan' is first a job description for an obstructor or accuser. The book's examples are the angel who blocks Balaam's road, the figure in Zechariah's night vision dated to mid-February 520 BC, and the accuser in the prologue to Job. Over time the title became the devil's name.

### Pazuzu and the noon-day devil
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Lefèvre shows that disease demons of the ancient Near East stand behind biblical images. Pazuzu was the southwest wind that carried malaria, and an inscription on his own figurine speaks in his voice. The 'devil of noon' in the Vulgate's psalms comes from demons of night and midday called the débér and the qétéb. Germain Bazin adds that Christian devil iconography descends from Assyrian and Babylonian demons like Pazuzu.

### The Peacock Angel of the Yezidis
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Louis Massignon reports that the Yezidis of Mount Sinjar hold that Satan is a fallen archangel who has been pardoned. God put him in charge of the world and of the transmigration of souls, and he is called Malak Tawus, the Peacock Angel, for his regained splendor. The doctrine traces to Sheikh Adi, who died in 1162 at Lalish, and to the mystic Hallaj, executed at Baghdad in 922. Massignon counted about 60,000 Yezidis in 1951, and they reject being called devil worshippers today.

### The Ravenna angel and the scratched face
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

H.-I. Marrou points to the Last Judgment mosaic at Ravenna, about 520, where the fallen angel differs from the good one only in color. In a Paris manuscript of Gregory of Nazianzus from about 880, Satan is a graceful youth with huge wings, dressed like a philosopher. A later hand scratched out his face in all three groups of the miniature.

### Nightmare demons and the rider of women
*Theory · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Across cultures people describe a hairy monster crouching on the sleeper's chest, with paralysis, suffocation and a waking cry. Physicians such as Soranus of Ephesus blamed posture, heavy bedclothes and indigestion, while folk belief named the Alp, Mara, the Montenegrin Vjeschitza and the incubus, the rider of women. Jolande Jacobi notes that in the Middle Ages one nightmare could serve as proof that a woman had slept with a demon, and many innocent women were burned.

### Jeanne Fery of Mons, 1584
*Case · Contested · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Sister Jeanne Fery, a nun of the Black Sisters at Mons, was presented to the Archbishop of Cambrai in April 1584 as possessed and was exorcised until November 1585. Her story included blood-signed pacts, a host that bled when she struck it with a knife, and a letter from Mary Magdalen that came out of her mouth. Pierre Debongnie thinks the events during the exorcisms happened as described but suspects she staged much of it, always in the evening half-dark. He cannot decide whether it was diabolical cleverness or psychology, and says it may have been both.

### Boullan's pink-paper confession

Abbé Jean-Antoine Boullan (1824 to 1893) called himself John the Baptist returned and the sword of God against Rome. Thibault to Huysmans, who had already put him in Là-Bas as Dr. Johannes, then to Léon Leclaire and Louis Massignon, who sent it to the Vatican Library in 1930. The editor, Père Bruno, describes its folios in detail but refuses to print it, saying the reader could not have stomached it.

*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Anne de Chantraine was arrested for witchcraft in Namur in March 1620 at the age of seventeen. She was tortured four times, condemned, and twice left unexecuted with no document explaining the delay. ### Marescot examines Marthe Brossier, 1599
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In 1599 the Paris physician Marescot examined Marthe Brossier, a young woman whose possession had drawn crowds, and went through the classic proofs one at a time. *Method · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

F. X. Maquart corrects the rule of economy, which says to prefer a natural explanation. It is not enough for a natural cause to be possible in principle, he says, and investigators must show that it is credible in fact before they reject a marvelous event. ### Thurston and the invented witch executions
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Herbert Thurston found that the figures in circulation for witch executions were guesses. A 1606 Paris chap-book reported fifty executions at Douai, and local records showed it to be fabricated from beginning to end. A real Douai trial of 1599 cost over 1,000 livres for three witches, which makes mass burnings implausible. His rule was to accept only official documents and eyewitness testimony.

### Innocent VIII's bull of 1484
*Document · Contested · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Pope Innocent VIII's bull Summis desiderantes of 1484 is often blamed for starting the witch hunts. Thurston argues it was mainly a ruling in a dispute over jurisdiction, and that prosecutions soon moved to secular courts where torture and lynch law produced the worst excesses. The Malleus Maleficarum followed in 1486.

### The nun and the director's remark
*Case · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Neurologist Jean Lhermitte describes a young nun who became unstable after her spiritual director remarked that the devil was at work. ### Queen Nzinga's possessed sorcerer, 1658
*Case · Contested · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In 1658 at Matamba in Angola, a sorcerer in the service of Queen Nzinga was possessed by a spirit that claimed the name of her dead brother Ngola Mbandi and the title creator of all things in heaven and earth. A long exorcism failed, and the man broke his chains, injured bystanders, threw himself into a pit and died about an hour after being pulled out. Three years earlier five sorcerers had unexpectedly allowed her to abandon the law of the Jaggas. Dom Laurent Kilger weighs two rival Capuchin accounts and admits both were retouched before publication.

### Dante's tired Lucifer
*Scene seed · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In Dante's Inferno the devil is no grand rebel. Auguste Valensin describes a Lucifer who never thinks, weeps from six eyes at once, and works like a bellows-cum-mincing machine. Dante also borrows deathbed anecdotes, such as the black cherub who wins an argument with Guido da Montefeltro by saying, 'Ah! you never knew I was a logician!'

### Melmoth Reconciled: the gift sold down
*Scene seed · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

In Balzac's Melmoth Réconcilié (1835), the devil's gifts of infallible action and absolute knowledge are sold at a loss from owner to owner. They go to a ruined financier, then a house painter, then an amorous clerk who burns them up in a single night of debauchery. Albert Béguin reads this as an economic myth in which evil is devalued like a currency, worn smooth like an old coin.

### A genealogy of 'God is dead'
*Theory · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Paulus Lenz-Médoc traces the idea that God is dead through German thought from Leibniz through Wolff, Reimarus, Lessing, Kant, Hegel, Heine, Strauss, Feuerbach and Marx to Nietzsche. He argues the crisis was engineered by Christianity's would-be friends. Leibniz wanted to justify Christianity and in fact undermined it, and the German Enlightenment worked by encircling and occupying religion.

### Mager exorcises the Obersalzberg
*Scene seed · Documented · Satan, ed. Bruno de Jésus-Marie (Sheed & Ward, 1951)*

Dom Alois Mager, a Benedictine, wrote the book's last essay shortly before he died of overwork on December 26, 1946. In the essay he calls Hitler a medium of Satan and names lying and murder as the two signs, from John 8:44.

## MindBook: Sekret Machines: Man (22)

### Rendlesham Forest and John Burroughs
*Case · Contested · Sekret Machines: Man (Tom DeLonge and Peter Levenda)*

In December 1980, US Air Force personnel from the bases at Bentwaters and Woodbridge in England reported lights and a strange craft in Rendlesham Forest. The authors use his case to argue for biomarkers of contact, which they call the 'Man variable.'

### Cash and Landrum, Texas, 1980
*Case · Contested · Sekret Machines: Man (Tom DeLonge and Peter Levenda)*

On December 29, 1980, Betty Cash, Vickie Landrum and Landrum's young grandson were driving on a Texas road when they say they came close to a bright object shooting flame, with many helicopters nearby. All three became ill, and the two women reported symptoms like radiation sickness. The authors cite Green, Nolan and Puthoff on the injuries as an attempt to move from stories to measurable harm.

### Mack's two hundred abductees
*Case · Contested · Sekret Machines: Man (Tom DeLonge and Peter Levenda)*

Harvard psychiatrist John Mack worked with about 200 people who reported abductions. He found real post-traumatic stress in them and no other psychiatric illness. The authors use this to separate a genuine experience and trauma from the question of what caused it, which is why they treat abductee testimony as data.

### Hynek's cardinal mistake
*Method · Documented · Sekret Machines: Man (Tom DeLonge and Peter Levenda)*

J. Allen Hynek warned that the cardinal mistake in studying UFOs is to substitute an interpretation of the phenomenon for the phenomenon itself. In 1973 Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel proposed that life on Earth may have been seeded on purpose by another civilization. Among their points is that life uses molybdenum, which is scarce on Earth. *Scene seed · Claim · The Tibetan Book of the Dead, ed. W. Y. Evans-Wentz (trans. Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup)*

In the second book the dead person wakes in a body made of thought and goes home. He sees his relatives and calls to them but is not heard, and then he sees his own corpse and thinks, "I am dead! *Theory · Claim · The Tibetan Book of the Dead, ed. W. Y. Evans-Wentz (trans. Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup)*

After the clear light comes a fourteen-day sequence. He is told to move toward the frightening bright light and away from the pleasant dull one, because giving in to either leads to rebirth. The last seven days bring fifty-eight blood-drinking deities with skull-cups, whom the text says are the same peaceful gods in a changed form.

The text explains how its whole method works with one comparison. When the dead person sees his future parents together, the text gives a technique to prevent entry and then exactly five methods for closing the door of the womb. The text says recognition in the bardo is helped by having looked at painted images of the wrathful deities during life: "by having seen their painted likenesses and their images... recognition of them will result, and liberation. *Conspiracy · Contested · The Tibetan Book of the Dead, ed. W. Y. Evans-Wentz (trans. Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup)*

Tibetan tradition says the Bardo Thödol was hidden in the eighth century by the teacher Padma Sambhava and taken out centuries later by a discoverer named Rigzin Karma Ling-pa. Such hidden books are called termas and their finders tertons. European critics called this forgery, and Lama Govinda answered that the recovered corpus runs to about a hundred volumes, by tradition 108, which he said made forgery "absurd." Evans-Wentz added that the book's standing in Tibet would not change even if the terton claim were false, and Jacques Vallée later compared the tertons to Joseph Smith bringing out the Book of Mormon.

### One manuscript behind the whole book
*Document · Documented · The Tibetan Book of the Dead, ed. W. Y. Evans-Wentz (trans. Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup)*

Everything the book says rests on a single manuscript of 137 folios, which Evans-Wentz bought early in 1919 from a young lama at the Bhutia Basti Monastery in Darjeeling. The lama said it had been in his family for generations, and the translator judged it at least 150 years old. One missing folio was replaced from Dr. Van Manen's block-print. The editor wrote that no attempt was made to compare it with other manuscripts, "none having been available."

### The editor admits how he built it
*Method · Documented · The Tibetan Book of the Dead, ed. W. Y. Evans-Wentz (trans. Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup)*

In an opening footnote Evans-Wentz says his task is "to correlate and systematize and sometimes to expand" the notes the translator dictated, using "congenial matter, from widely separated sources." He also writes that the translator's English came through him as a "living English dictionary" and that his own knowledge of Tibetan was "almost nothing." Where the text disagrees with his reading, he blames corruption of the text, popular contamination or the silence of unnamed initiates. Nothing on the page separates the Tibetan text from his additions, so most readers never see this.

Might fit: The reading room, continued: how an editor shapes a text

## MindBook: Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig (16)

### Phaedrus and the court-ordered shock treatment
*Case · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

The narrator of Pirsig's book is the successor to his former self, Phaedrus, a hyperintellectual who went insane pursuing the meaning of Quality. The old personality was "liquidated without a trace" by twenty-eight consecutive electroshock treatments at about "800 mills of amperage," a court-ordered "Annihilation ECS." The narrator rebuilds Phaedrus from a trunk of notated books and what other people say, and fears his return. Pirsig says the book is in essence fact, though much was changed for rhetorical purposes.

### The beer-can shim
*Scene seed · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

On the road, John Sutherland is offended at the idea of fixing his BMW with a shim cut from a beer can. The narrator realizes: "I was seeing what the shim meant. He was seeing what the shim was." From this he draws the book's basic split between classical understanding, which looks at underlying form, and romantic understanding, which looks at immediate appearance. He calls them "two realities" that "don't match and they don't fit."

### The law of infinite hypotheses
*Theory · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

As a fifteen-year-old science student Phaedrus found that hypotheses multiply faster than experiments can eliminate them. He stated it as a law: "The number of rational hypotheses that can explain any given phenomenon is infinite." He took this as "a catastrophic logical disproof of the general validity of all scientific method" and said science was producing "scientifically produced antiscience," chaos. He was expelled at seventeen and began what the narrator calls his lateral drifts.

### You know Quality without defining it
*Method · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Phaedrus refused to define Quality for his rhetoric students and proved they knew it anyway. By show of hands and by ranking papers, a class agreed on which writing was better, and he said "whatever it is that caused the overwhelming majority to raise their hands... is what I mean by Quality." The narrator admits the move was "formally completely irrational" and that Phaedrus "wasn't teaching anymore, he was indoctrinating," yet it taught the students to write.

### Quality comes before subject and object
*Theory · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Asked by the faculty whether Quality is subjective or objective, Phaedrus answered that it is neither. Quality is an event that comes before the split into subject and object, and it is "the cause of the subjects and objects, which are then mistakenly presumed to be the cause of the Quality." He pushed the idea to absolute monism and found it matched the Tao Te Ching line for line: "He had broken the code." The narrator says this thought tipped Phaedrus into the avalanche of his breakdown.

### The law of gravity is a ghost
*Theory · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Asked by his son for a ghost story, the narrator argues that the laws of science contain no matter and no energy, so they exist only in people's minds. The law of gravity "did not exist before Isaac Newton," he says, and so "It's a ghost!" He calls belief in such laws "mass hypnosis. In a very orthodox form known as 'education.'"

### Poincaré and the subliminal self
*Theory · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Searching for anyone who anticipated Phaedrus, the narrator finds Henri Poincaré, who argued that the axioms of geometry are conventions ("Geometry is not true, it is advantageous") and that facts must be selected, since an unselective collection of facts has no more chance of producing science than a monkey at a typewriter has of producing the Lord's Prayer. Poincaré says a "subliminal self" does the selecting, guided by mathematical beauty. The narrator says the two trails "stop right at each other's end," like Robinson Crusoe's footprints.

### Aretê, the Sophists and the lost Good
*Theory · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Reading Aristotle's Rhetoric and Plato's Gorgias, Phaedrus decides there was a war for the future mind of man between the Sophists, who taught aretê ("excellence"), and the dialecticians Socrates and Plato, who won it for Truth and lost the Good. A passage by Kitto on Homeric aretê gave him the equation aretê = dharma = Quality, which he says existed before dialectic itself. Plato fixed the Good as an Idea, and Aristotle's invention of "substance" finished the demotion. Pirsig defines gumption as enthusiasm, from a Greek word for being filled with Quality, and sorts what drains it into setbacks from outside and hang-ups from inside. Value rigidity is the hang-up he illustrates with the South Indian monkey trap: a monkey will not let go of the rice in a hollowed coconut, so it stays caught. Truth traps are yes-or-no thinking that leaves out mu, the answer that will "unask the question." He says "the real cycle you're working on is a cycle called yourself."

### Stuckness as the start of understanding
*Method · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

In the stuck-screw parable the narrator reaches "the zero moment of consciousness. Stuck. No answer." He says treating the machine as pure objects causes stuckness, because there are "an infinite number of facts about the motorcycle" and nothing in the facts says which matter. Quality tells you where to go, and stuckness becomes "the psychic predecessor of all real understanding."

### The glass-door dream
*Scene seed · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

In a short chapter that is only a dream, the narrator watches his family through a glass door that turns into the door of his own coffin, and a dark figure tells him "The dead aren't permitted to speak." Later, on a foggy California cliff, he confesses to his son Chris that he was insane. Chris then asks about the hospital glass door, a dream the narrator never told him, and the narrator says "I am Phaedrus." Asked "Were you really insane?" he answers "No!", and Chris says "I knew it."

### Reading the narrator as unreliable
*Method · Documented · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

Pirsig tells readers in the twenty-fifth anniversary introduction not to trust the narrator's account of Phaedrus. He borrows Allen Tate's reading of Henry James's The Turn of the Screw, where the governess's hysterical belief in a ghost is what kills the children. The reader knows Phaedrus is evil only because the narrator says so, and the narrator, ruled by social values, saved his skin. Pirsig also admits that critics rightly called the ending a "Hollywood ending," and that Phaedrus means "brilliant" (the wolf was Lysias).

### The murder of Chris Pirsig
*Case · Documented · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

In the 1984 Afterword Pirsig writes about the 1979 murder of his son Chris, who was stabbed in San Francisco. He applies the book's own ideas to the question "Where did he go?", saying it is malformed until you ask "What is the 'he'?" He says Chris was a pattern, and a pattern can survive the loss of a body; he suggests it reasserted itself in his infant daughter Nell. The afterword also records that 121 publishers rejected the book.

### The Church of Reason at Bozeman
*Case · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

At a college damaged by ultra-right state politics, Phaedrus told colleagues the real University is "a state of mind," "the continuing body of reason itself," and that the legal corporation is only its shell. The same governor had drawn up a "list of fifty radical professors." Phaedrus said losing accreditation would be like excommunication, and the narrator notes the paradox that "his lack of faith in reason was why he was so fanatically dedicated to it."

### Were the atomic bombs illusory?
*Scene seed · Contested · Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance, Robert M. Pirsig*

At Benares Hindu University Phaedrus absorbed the idea that logic presumes a separation of subject from object, and therefore is not final wisdom. In a classroom he asked whether the atomic bombs dropped on Japan were illusory, and the answer was yes. That exchange made him leave India and give up the pursuit of the "ghost" of reason.
